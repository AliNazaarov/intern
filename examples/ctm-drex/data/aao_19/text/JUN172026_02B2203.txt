Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for 
Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he warranted an approval when 
considering the totality of the evidence. We dismissed a subsequent appeal. The matter is now before 
us on motion to reopen. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). Upon 
review, we will dismiss the motion. 
A motion to reopen must state new facts and be supported by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(2). According to the Instructions for Notice of Appeal or Motion (Form I-290B, Notice of 
Appeal or Motion), any new facts and documentary evidence must demonstrate eligibility for the 
required immigration benefit at the time the application or petition was filed. A motion to reopen that 
does not satisfy the applicable requirements must be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). 
We do not require the evidence of a "new fact" to have been previously unavailable or undiscoverable. 
Instead, we interpret "new facts" to mean those that are relevant to the issues raised on motion and 
that have not been previously submitted in the proceeding, which includes within the original petition. 
Reasserting previously stated facts or resubmitting previously provided evidence does not constitute 
"new facts." See Matter of Coelho, 20 I&N Dec. 464,473 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence 
to have the potential to change the outcome). 
A motion to reconsider must: (1) state the reasons for reconsideration, (2) be supported by any 
pertinent precedent decision to establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application 
of law or policy, and (3) establish that our prior decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the 
record at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). A motion to reconsider that does not satisfy 
these requirements must be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). Because the scope of a motion is 
limited to the prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.S(a)(l)(i), (ii). 
This filing contains several aspects that weigh against the Petitioner, even before factoring in his actual 
claims within the brief. First and most importantly, on the Form I-290B the Petitioner indicated this 
was a motion to reopen and he made no effort to identify the filing as a motion to reconsider or as a 
combined motion to reopen and reconsider, but contrary to that, the brief requests that we reopen and 
reconsider our decision to dismiss the appeal. Additionally, the Petitioner did not organize the brief 
into a cohesive manner, much less to identify which of his claims should be considered under the 
motion to reopen (i.e., new facts supported by evidence) and which should be evaluated under a motion 
to reconsider (i.e., that we misapplied the law based on the record as it existed at that time). Instead, 
he presented 35 pages of intermixed and recursive arguments. Even though it appears appropriate to 
summarily dispose of this filing, we will evaluate the propriety of our decision to dismiss the appeal. 
That decision included two independent bases for the finding that the Petitioner was not eligible for 
this restrictive immigration benefit. First that he did not demonstrate sustained acclaim, and second 
that he had not supported his claims with extensive documentation. 
First, we address what appear to be his claims that may fall under a motion to reopen. The Petitioner 
asserts that his national or international acclaim predated 2022 (when he came to the United States) 
and he supports that claim with several support letters. But this is not a new fact that he did not 
previously claim, as he presented achievements occurring before 2022 in an effort to demonstrate his 
acclaim under the ten regulatory criteria in the initial filing through his judging. As this claim does 
not meet the requirements for a motion to reopen, we will not consider it as part of his claims under 
that type of filing. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). His remaining motion to reopen claim pertains to those 
same support letters he offered before SCOPS. Within this motion briet: he states he is resubmitting 
the letters, but he does not state what facts are new and it appears that he is simply reoffering the same 
facts about the letters that he provided before. In the motion, he only lists the letter's author before 
presenting a lengthy blocked quote from the letter. Here, he fails to meet the requirements of a motion 
to reopen in which the filing party "must state the new facts to be provided in the reopened proceeding 
and be supported by affidavits or other documentary evidence." 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). 
Now to what appears to be the Petitioner's motion to reconsider arguments, the first of which is that 
he demonstrated that his national and international acclaim should be considered as sustained under 
the preponderance of the evidence. 
In the motion, the Petitioner points to a simple statement we made acknowledging his assertions in the 
record. Within our statement providing background on the Petitioner and his field of endeavor, we 
stated: "The Petitioner states that he began playing organized footvolley in his home country of 
Brazil .... [where] he won several amateur and professional footvolley titles and established a 
popular club for training footvolley athletes." From that nondescript statement mirroring the 
Petitioner's assertions, he then concludes: "This shows that the agency agrees that the Petitioner had 
achievements in the field before coming to the United States, which contradicts the argument that the 
Petitioner has not proven sustained a career [sic] in the field, this action from the agency violates 
AP A." But that is not the case as the Petitioner mistook our acknowledgement for endorsement. 
2 
We did not discuss evidence corroborating his assertions because he did not submit objective evidence 
of titles he earned before coming to the United States and instead, he offered letters describing his 
titles. But the regulation and the petition's instructions each informed the Petitioner that his initial 
evidence requirements included evidence he was the recipient ofprizes or awards to include accolades he 
claims before coming to the United States. A testimonial letter from someone in the industry that is not 
corroborated by the primary evidence of the prize or award falls short of meeting the Petitioner's burden 
of proof that he received the specified award. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(2) (indicating only where the 
filing party demonstrates that primary evidence does not exist or cannot be obtained may they rely on 
secondary evidence, and only where secondary evidence is demonstrated to be unavailable may the 
petitioner rely on affidavits or letters). 
Unsupported conclusory letters from those in the Petitioner's field are not sufficient evidence that a 
particular prize or award is nationally or internationally recognized. As a result, the Petitioner has not 
shown that our appellate decision-in particular the sustained acclaim portion-was incorrect based 
on the evidence in the record of proceedings at the time of the decision, which is a requirement for a 
motion to reconsider. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Ultimately, the Petitioner has not shown that he has 
sustained the requisite level of acclaim. In determining whether an alien has enjoyed "sustained" 
national or international acclaim, USCIS should ensure that such acclaim must be maintained. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
A motion to reconsider is the proper filing for arguing a change in the law. Gonzalez Hernandez v. 
Garland, 9 F.4th 278, 286 (5th Cir. 2021) ( discussing the appropriate venue for filing a motion based 
on a change in the law as a motion to reconsider); Salim v. Holder, 728 F.3d 718, 721 (7th Cir. 2013); 
Rodas-Orellana v. Holder, 780 F.3d 982, 985-86 (10th Cir. 2015) (finding that a change in case law 
is not considered new evidence for purposes of a motion to reopen removal proceedings; rather, it is a 
motion for reconsideration that asks the agency to consider a change in the law); Wu v. INS, 436 F.3d 
157, 163 (2d Cir. 2006). Also see Matter of 0-S-G-, 24 I&N Dec. 56, 60 (BIA 2006) (finding the 
moving party must rely on a motion to reconsider to show how a change in law materially affects a 
prior decision); Matter ofRamos, 23 I&N Dec. 336, 338 (BIA 2002); Matter ofJ-J-, 21 I&N Dec. 
976,977 n.1 (BIA 1997). 
The Petitioner notes what he perceives as a change in the law after a recent district court decision, 
issued after his petition denial, concluded that the final merits determination process was not proper. 
The opinion the Petitioner references is Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3 l 70, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 
(D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026). We begin noting the Mukherji court's determination has been rejected not 
only before it made it (Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 818 (E.D. Pa. 2025); Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *17 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025)), but also since (Taneja v. 
USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 1734266, at *6-8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026)). 
The Mukherji court noted that in 1995 the former Immigration and Naturalization Service proposed a 
two-step adjudicative process. The court also observed that the Office of Personnel Management's 
(OPM) docket showed that the 1995 notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) was classified as a 
substantive rule. Based on that classification of the entire NPRM, the court treated the two-step 
process as the sole reason the proposed rule was a substantive or legislative rule, and it did not evaluate 
whether the numerous other provisions within the proposed rule might have been the basis for OPM's 
classification. 
3 
On that understanding, the court concluded that the two-step adjudicative process constituted a 
legislative or substantive rule that could be implemented only through notice-and-comment 
rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act. In doing so, the court did not fully engage with 
USCIS' stated rationale for adopting the two-step process, namely that a federal circuit court required 
it, and that using one approach within that circuit and a different approach elsewhere was not prudent 
or feasible. USCIS' implementing memorandum discussed the circuit court's decision in detail and 
expressly adopted that approach. USCIS respectfully submits that this explanation satisfies any 
obligation to articulate a rational basis for the policy change. 
In addition, the Mukherji decision did not appear to address how that petitioner's satisfaction of the 
criteria in that case related to the method of adjudication. The court ordered approval of the petition 
without analyzing whether any favorable determinations made at step one had been reached under the 
prior approach in which USCIS did not conduct a qualitative sustained-acclaim analysis under each 
individual criterion. The five criteria the court found satisfied ( awards, published material, judging, 
contributions, and leading or critical role) thus were not adjudicated in the manner the court seemed 
to contemplate reinstating. USCIS respectfully submits that this omission was not harmless, because 
it bears directly on whether the court's remedy accords with the adjudicative framework it preferred. 
And about congressional intent, USCIS understands Congress to have intended that qualifying 
candidates for this immigration benefit be among those at the top of their field. The House Judiciary 
Committee explained: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small 
percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor." H.R. Rep. 
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at *6739 (House 
Committee Report). Mukherji, however, understood the legislative history differently, stating that the 
Conference Report on the Senate bill that ultimately became law "says absolutely nothing" about a 
requirement that persons seeking priority-worker extraordinary ability classification be part of "that 
small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field" or any similar 
formulation. H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, 101st Cong., 2d Sess. (Oct. 26, 1990). 
USCIS respectfully reads the legislative history differently. The "Senate Bill" material the court cited 
is from a Conference Report, while the "small percentage" language appears in a House Judiciary 
Committee Report. These documents serve different functions in the legislative process. When a 
conference report does not address a particular substantive issue, as is the case here, the committee 
reports that originally developed and explained the provision remain the most authoritative evidence 
of congressional intent. See Zuber v. Allen, 396 U.S. 168, 186 (1969) (noting that committee reports 
"represent the considered and collective understanding ofthose Congressmen involved in drafting and 
studying proposed legislation"); Garcia v. United States, 469 U.S. 70, 76 (1984) ( explaining that "[i]n 
surveying legislative history we have repeatedly stated that the authoritative source for finding the 
Legislature's intent lies in the Committee Reports on the bill"). 
The House Judiciary Committee Report, H.R. Rep. 101-723, supplies the substantive explanation of 
Congress's intent for the Immigration Act of l 990's extraordinary-ability classification, including the 
"small percentage" formulation. As summarized by the Congressional Research Service, committee 
reports typically outline the need and purpose of legislation and may include statements of legislative 
intent to guide both the executive branch in implementation and the judiciary in interpretation. See 
4 
Committee Reports: Required Contents, Congressional Research Service Products (Library of 
Congress) (Jan. 22, 2008). 
By contrast, the Conference Report, H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, primarily reconciles differences 
between the House and Senate versions and describes how those differences were resolved. Its focus 
is on the compromise text rather than on restating all underlying policy judgments that were already 
addressed in the committee reports. A conference report is just the final, official version of a 
compromise bill that only lists the specific legal text and the formal steps the House and Senate must 
take to pass it. Coeference Reports and Joint Explanatory Statements, CONGRESS.GOV (Jun. 11, 
2015), https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/98-382. In that light, the Conference Report's silence 
on the "top of the field" requirement is most reasonably understood as reflecting acceptance, rather 
than rejection, of the House Judiciary Committee's previously articulated intent. 
Accordingly, USCIS respectfully understands H.R. Rep. 101-723 to remain the controlling statement 
of congressional intent on this point. That understanding is reflected in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), which 
requires "a level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
The Petitioner further argues that he submitted support letters that we did not consider. But we did 
review and consider the letters in our decision dismissing the appeal, even if we did not list each one. 
In our decision on the appeal we stated: 
We agree that SCOPS appears to have overlooked some support letters and aspects of 
others. These materials not only attest to the Petitioner's playing and coaching abilities 
but also praise his training and teaching skills. For example, one letter-writer, a 
Brazilian professional soccer player, stated that he trained with the Petitioner in the 
United States and left "in top form." But the materials do not ultimately explain how 
the Petitioner ranks among the small percentage at his field's very top. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("[T]he petitioner must explain the significance of the 
submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained 
national or international acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise.") 
The presumption of regularity applies in the immigration context, and it is presumed that an agency has 
reviewed all ofthe evidence before it. Eiden v. Texas, 597 U.S. 785,811 (2022); INSv. Miranda, 459 U.S. 
14, 18 (1982). That presumption is applied before USCIS and an alien attempting to establish that an 
agency violated their right to due process by failing to consider relevant evidence is a heavy burden a 
filing party must overcome when alleging the agency did not review the evidence. E.g., Cruz v. Bondi, 
146 F.4th 730, 738-40 (9th Cir. 2025). Here, the Petitioner must demonstrate that USCIS ignored and 
did not consider their evidence in the decision on his appeal. This burden demands "that a petitioner 
present 'clear, affirmative evidence' that the agency did not review the evidence before it." Id. at 740 
(quoting Gov't ofGuam v. Guerrero, 11 F.4th 1052, 1060 (9th Cir. 2021)); Al-Hela v. Eiden, 66 F.4th 
217, 236-37 (D.C. Cir. 2023). A petitioner must first show what record evidence in particular was 
supposedly ignored or misconstrued by the agency, and such a showing cannot be vague, nor can it gesture 
broadly toward swaths ofevidence-it must be clear and affirmative. Cruz, 146 F .4th at 7 40 ( citing Gov 't 
a/Guam, 11 F.4th at 1060); Colindres v. US. Dep 't ofState, 71 F.4th 1018, 1025 (D.C. Cir. 2023).

Furthermore, a petitioner must explain why that evidence was not only material, but also "highly 
probative or potentially dispositive." Cruz, 146 F.4th at 740 (quoting Cole v. Holder, 659 F.3d 762, 772 
(9th Cir. 2011)). But here the Petitioner has not met that burden and he has not demonstrated that the 
evidence that was not specifically discussed would have affected this case's outcome (i.e., that he was 
prejudiced by the lack of discussion). Id. (quoting Najmabadi v. Holder, 597 F.3d 983, 991 (9th Cir. 
2010)). See also Rhoa-Zamora v. INS, 971 F.2d 26, 34 (7th Cir. 1992), as modified on denial ofreh'g 
(Nov. 4, 1992); Safari Club Int 'l v. Haaland, 31 F.4th 1157, 1177-78 (9th Cir. 2022). And the Petitioner 
has not achieved that in his motion to reconsider, here. 
Also, simply because the Petitioner has one view ofwhat the letters demonstrate, that is not a conclusion 
that we must share. A trier of fact should consider the issues presented and provide a sufficiently 
reasoned analysis. They are not however, required to interpret evidence in the manner a petitioner 
advocates. Matter ofLanders, 29 I&N Dec. 240, 245 (BIA 2025); Matter ofM-D-C-V-, 28 I&N Dec. 
18, 32 (BIA 2020); Matter ofJ-C-H-F-, 27 I&N Dec. 211,217 (BIA 2018); Matter ofZ-Z-O-, 26 I&N 
Dec. 586, 592 (BIA 2015 Matter ofD-R-, 25 I&N Dec. 445, 454-55 (BIA 2011) (explaining that an 
triers of fact may make reasonable inferences from direct and circumstantial evidence in the record as 
a whole and is not required to accept a respondent's account where other plausible views of the 
evidence are supported by the record). The final determination of whether evidence satisfies the 
requirements of a regulation or meets the burden of proof lies with USCIS. See Matter of Caron 
International, 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comrn'r 1988) (finding that the appropriate entity to determine 
eligibility is USCIS); see also Matter ofSkirball Cultural Ctr., 25 I&N Dec. 799, 806 (2012).

The Petitioner also alleges that we ignored new evidence he offered on appeal. However, because the 
governing regulations and SCOPS' request for evidence provided the Petitioner with both formal 
notice and a reasonable opportunity to submit the necessary documentation, we normally decline to 
consider new evidence proffered for the first time on appeal. Under 8 C.F.R. § l03.2(b )( 11 ), all 
requested evidence must be submitted together at one time. 
It is well-established that an administrative appellate body will not review or consider evidence first 
introduced on appeal when the filing party was put on notice of the requirement and given a 
meaningful opportunity to provide it before the denial. Matter ofFurtado, 28 I&N Dec. 794, 801-02 
(BIA 2024) (citing Matter of Soriano, 19 I&N Dec. 764, 766 (BIA 1988)); Matter of Izaguirre, 27 
I&N Dec. 67, 71 (BIA 2017) (same); Matter ofJin, 29 I&N Dec. 441,449 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter 
of Obaigbena, 19 I&N Dec. 533, 537 (BIA 1988)). Additional precedents reinforce this restriction, 
noting that the appellate process is not a venue for the primary submission of evidence. See Matter of 
N-P-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 347,349 (BIA 2025) (quoting Matter ofGerman Santos, 28 I&N Dec. 552,561 
n.4 (BIA 2022), aff'd, No. 22-2072, 2025 WL 2694101 (3d Cir. Sept. 22, 2025) ("[w]e do not consider 
new evidence for the first time on appeal")); Matter of D-J-L-, 29 I&N Dec. 485, 489 (BIA 2026); 
Matter ofD-G-E-A- & N-G-G-E-, 29 I&N Dec. 570,577 n.5 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter ofFedorenko, 
19 I&N Dec. 57, 73-74 (BIA 1984)). 
Although the Petitioner has submitted additional evidence in support of the motion to reopen, the 
Petitioner has not identified new facts as mandated by the regulation. On motion to reconsider, the 
Petitioner has not established that our previous decision was based on an incorrect application of law 
or policy at the time we issued our decision. Therefore, the motion will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(4). 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed.
