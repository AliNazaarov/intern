Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an educational entrepreneur, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability. Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner meets the classification's initial evidentiary requirements. The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if they have extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and their achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; they seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and their entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If the petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then they must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable material if they can demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCrS' two-step analysis of extraordinary 
ability "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 
131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANAL YSrS 
The Petitioner is the founder and director-general of an early childhood education center and special 
education school in Venezuela. She intends to establish and develop a similar business in the United 
States and claims eligibility for this classification based on extraordinary ability in education, 
specifically in the field of special education entrepreneurship. The Petitioner has a bachelor's degree 
in accounting and completed post-graduate studies in pedagogy and human resources. 
Because the Petitioner does not claim to have a one-time achievement, she must provide evidence 
meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded 
that the provided evidence did not meet any of the criteria the Petitioner claimed. On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that SCOPS overlooked relevant evidence and that she has met four evidentiary 
criteria, as well as meeting a fifth criterion through comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
The Petitioner farther asserts on appeal that, pursuant to the U.S. Supreme Court decision in Loper 
Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 144 S.Ct. 2244 (2024), "the AAO needs to re-analyze how it 
adjudicates all EB-1 criteria" and "put an end to the extra-statutory 'Final Merits Determination" in 
extraordinary ability cases. Notably, the Petitioner has not provided any support for this assertion and 
appears to misinterpret Loper's reach. Loper overturned Chevron USA v. Natural Resources Defense 
Council, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), in holding that federal courts need not defer to agencies' reasonable 
interpretations of ambiguous federal laws. Therefore, Loper provides the judiciary with the sole 
prerogative to "say what the law is," stating that "[ c ]ourts must exercise their independent judgement 
in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority." Loper, 144 S. Ct. at 2257, 
2273. While Loper guides federal courts that review our interpretation of ambiguous statutes, it does 
not modify our own adjudication of immigrant petitions. Accordingly, we are not persuaded by the 
Petitioner's assertion and will conduct a de novo review ofthe record considering the two-part analysis 
described in Kazarian and in the USCrS Policy Manual. 
The Petitioner's assertion that the two-part analysis set forth in Kazarian is "extra-statutory" is also 
based on her interpretation of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), which states that the evidence 
submitted to establish eligibility "shall" include evidence of a one-time achievement or at least three 
of the initial criteria. The Petitioner asserts that this "shall" requires users to approve any petition 
that meets the initial eligibility requirements. A plain language reading of the regulation, however,

indicates that this "shall" is not binding on USCIS, but on petitioners, since it pertains to what evidence 
petitioners are required to submit. See, e.g., Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 391 ('The regulation does 
not say that meeting the regulatory criteria presumptively qualifies an applicant for a visa. It addresses 
what evidence must 'accompany' a petition.") (cleaned up). 
Finally, regarding the individual criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), the Petitioner asserts that 
the standards for meeting certain criteria, such as those at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vi) are 
"remarkabl[y] low," and that USCIS must apply a similar standard with respect to all ten evidentiary 
criteria. We disagree. In evaluating evidence submitted under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), USCIS' first 
step of evidentiary review is limited to determining whether the evidence submitted with a petition 
meets the plain language of the regulatory criteria. In doing so, we consider the quality and caliber of 
the evidence to determine whether a particular criterion has been met, to the extent the criterion has 
qualitative elements. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. While some criteria do not have such qualitative elements, we are nevertheless bound by the 
plain language of the regulations in evaluating whether the evidence before us satisfies all 
requirements. We will address each claimed criterion below.

E. Comparable Evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
If any of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to a person's occupation, a 
petitioner may instead provide comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
For such evidence to be considered, however, petitioners must establish two factors: First, why a 
particular evidentiary criterion is not readily applicable to a person's occupation, and second, why the 
provided evidence is of comparable significance to the evidence required by that criterion. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
In her response to SCOPS' request for evidence, the Petitioner asserted that she qualifies under the 
comparable evidence provision, but did not specify which of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
5 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) are not readily applicable to her occupation or why her evidence was of 
comparable significance. SCOPS therefore did not grant any additional criteria under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). 
On appeal, the Petitioner provides an attorney letter asserting for the first time that the criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) does not readily apply to her occupation, since it pertains to commercial 
success in the performing arts. The letter further claims that the Petitioner's "innovative educational 
models or programs," her "management and leadership," and her "community outreach and 
partnerships" are all comparable to the evidence provided to establish eligibility under this criterion. 
The only reasoning provided for this conclusion is an unsupported assertion that "[ t ]he traditional EB 1 
criteria do not take these accomplishments into account." 
First, counsel's unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter of S-M-, 
22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("statements in a brief, motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence 
and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight"). Second, the appellate statement does not explain 
how the proffered evidence has comparable significance in the field ofeducation as box office receipts 
and other evidence of commercial success do in the performing arts. The Petitioner therefore has not 
established eligibility under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) through the submission ofcomparable evidence. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not provided the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and so has 
not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. Accordingly, the 
circumstances in this matter do not require the type of final merits determination referenced in 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also, INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) 
(holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level 
do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 
954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of her work is 
indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise 
demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that she 
is among the small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
