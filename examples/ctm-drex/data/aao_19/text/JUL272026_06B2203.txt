Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an educator, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least ten of the initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria

regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claims to have established eight of 
these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field; 
• (ii), membership in associations which require outstanding achievements of their members; 
• (iii), published material about the individual in professional or major media; 
• (iv), participation as a judge of others' work; 
• (v), original contributions of major significance to the field;

USCIS determined that the Petitioner met only two of the regulatory criteria: criterion (iv), relating to 
judging the work of others, and criterion (vi), relating to authorship of scholarly articles. USCIS 
therefore found that the Petitioner did not establish eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. On 
appeal, the Petitioner maintains that she has established eligibility for eight criteria.

2. Membership in Associations which Require Outstanding Achievements 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims eligibility under this criterion based on membership in the International 
Association of Academic and Teaching Professionals (IAATP) and the Association ofDevelopmental 
4 
Education Specialists (MARO). The Petitioner contends that, contrary to USCIS' findings, the 
evidence in the record sufficiently demonstrates membership in these associations is "based on a 
substantive evaluation of her professional record" and that she therefore fulfills this criterion. 
Regarding MARO, the record contains evidence ofthe Petitioner's membership in the association, the 
association's charter, and a letter from the secretary of the board of MARO regarding the Petitioner's 
admission as member. The charter states that the association is a non-profit organization created to 
protect the general and professional interests of specialists in the field of developmental education and 
that members may be "researchers, teachers, psychologists, methodologists, educators, and other 
individuals in the development and implementation of the theory and practice of developmental 
education." The letter from the MARO board secretary states that the Petitioner was accepted into the 
association "due to her many years of dedication to the community," including the development of 
educational methods and publication of textbooks and articles, among other work and recognition in 
the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims the letter from the board secretary confirms that she was accepted into 
the association "based on her documented professional achievements," and that admission was "not 
automatic or purely formal." The Petitioner claims the letter shows that "in the Petitioner's case, 
membership was granted on the basis of recognized professional accomplishments rather than mere 
participation." 
We conclude that the record does not show the Petitioner's eligibility for this criterion based on her 
membership in MARO. As USCIS noted, the charter for MARO does not support the claim that 
outstanding achievements are required for membership, but rather that membership is "open" to 
members of the profession. The regulation states that the organization must "require" outstanding 
achievements of their members. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). The Petitioner's claim that membership 
was granted "in her case" based on her particular dedication does not demonstrate that the association 
requires outstanding achievements ofits members. Moreover, the Petitioner has not shown that "years 
of dedication" in the field is equivalent to "outstanding achievements" as judged by "recognized 
national or international experts." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). Indeed, when an individual's 
membership is based solely on years of experience in a particular field, this generally indicates that 
membership is not based on outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra. 
We also conclude that the Petitioner has not established eligibility for this criterion based on her 
membership in IAATP. Regarding IAATP, the Petitioner submitted evidence of her membership and 
a copy of the association's regulations. The regulations for IAATP describe the association as a non­
profit, voluntary, independent, and international professional body dedicated to advancing the field of 
education. The regulations state that there are "categories" of membership, however the regulations 
do not enumerate or define these categories. The regulations further state that membership is open to 
individuals who have "achieved international recognition, distinction, and demonstrated excellence in 
the field of education." 
Regarding the application process, the regulations state that applicants must provide evidence from a 
list ofrequirements, such as having had a leadership role at a distinguished institution, authoring books 
or journals, having a role as a peer reviewer, demonstrating academic influence through citations to 
5 
their work, receiving awards, fellowships, or honors for excellence in education, and making active 
contributions to equity, responsibility, or educational access. Finally, the regulations state that 
applications are reviewed by existing members ofthe association, who are "internationally recognized 
experts with significant contributions to education." 
The Petitioner has not shown that the requirements for membership in IAA TP reflect outstanding 
achievements, nor that this requirement is judged by recognized national or international experts. 
While we recognize that IAATP regulations mirror some of the language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h), there 
is no additional information in the record about this association, its members, and how membership in 
the association is viewed by others in the field. Evidence that merely reiterates USCIS' definitions 
relating to this classification is generally not persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra 
at F.2(8)(3) (discussing letters of support). The IAATP regulations on their own lack sufficient detail 
for us to determine the specific level of achievement required to gain membership and whether, 
therefore, the current members are, in fact, recognized national or international experts in the field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
For these reasons, the Petitioner has not established that she fulfills this criterion. 
3. Published Material about the Petitioner in Professional or Major Trade Publications 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field

4. Original Contributions of Major Significance to the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for evidence of an individual's "original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that their contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, have 
remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major 
significance. See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an 
original contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation ofthe alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative ofmajor significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra.

5. Leading or Critical Role for Distinguished Organizations or Establishments 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof: has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra.

6. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration for Services 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." USCIS 
found that the Petitioner did not establish this criterion because she provided evidence of her income 
tax statements from 2010 to 2014, but evidence of comparative salary data for different years, 
specifically 2014 and 2015. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that this analysis places "undue weight on a technical mismatch" 
and that the comparative salary data should be considered a reasonable and probative basis for 
comparison. 4 We agree with USCIS that the evidence provided is not sufficient to establish this 
criterion. 
The comparative salary data is from the 2016 Current Statistical Survey, a statistical review of social 
and economic data in Russia, which provides data for 2014 and 2015. It provides the "accrued average 
monthly nominal wages of employees of organizations," organized by industry. For the education 
industry it states the average wages for 2014 were approximately 26,000 rubles monthly and 27,000 
rubles monthly for 2015. But comparative wage data should be appropriate to the individual's 
occupation and location to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the 
field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. The Petitioner has not provided evidence 
particular to her specific occupation or location; instead, the data is national and averages wages for 
the entire education industry. 
Because the Petitioner has not provided sufficient evidence demonstrating the salaries of others in her 
occupation, the Petitioner did not establish she commands a high salary in relation to others in his 
field. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability

For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
