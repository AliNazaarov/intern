Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software development company, seeks classification for the Beneficiary as an alien 
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that while the Petitioner established that the Beneficiary met 
the initial evidentiary requirements for this classification by meeting at least three of the criteria under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the record did not establish that he has garnered sustained national or 
international acclaim for his achievements and is among the small percentage of technology 
entrepreneurs at the top of the field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 
103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

A. Original Contributions of Major Significance in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) seeks "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." In 
order to satisfy the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must establish that not only 
have they made original contributions, but that those contributions have been of major significance in 
the field. For example, a petitioner may show that the contributions have been widely implemented 
throughout the field, have remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a 
level of major significance. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134-5 (D.D.C. 2013).

B. Additional Criteria 
As noted above, USCIS concluded in its decision that the Petitioner had not established that the 
Beneficiary meets an additional four evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that USCIS did not give sufficient consideration to the evidence submitted in support 
of these four criteria, but does not directly challenge USCIS' conclusions regarding the individual 
criteria. Rather, it states that "USCIS no longer contests this criterion" when referring to evidence of 
the Beneficiary's remuneration and the display of his work, and the discussion of this evidence takes 
place within the context of the final merits determination and the three evidentiary criteria which 
USCIS granted. We will therefore not consider the Beneficiary's potential qualification under these 
four criteria. See Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230,234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (declining to address a "passing 
reference" to an argument in a brief that did not provide legal support). 
Because per the analysis above we have withdrawn one of the three criteria which USCIS concluded 
that the Beneficiary met, he no longer meets the requisite three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
He therefore does not meet the initial evidence requirement for classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. However, as the final merits determination was the primary focus of USCIS' 
decision and the appeal brief before us, we will review that aspect of the decision below.

C. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the 
field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
Because extraordinary ability is an elite level ofaccomplishment whose recognition necessarily entails 
a judgement call, it cannot be established through meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
alone. The final merits determination is the ultimate statutory inquiry of whether the applicant has 
extraordinary ability as demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim. Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, at 395 (2022)(finding USCIS' two-step analysis of extraordinary ability 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that USCIS did not give sufficient consideration to some of the 
evidence in the record, particularly pertaining to those evidentiary criteria which they determined the 
Beneficiary did not meet. We will address the totality of the record below. 
As discussed above, the Petitioner has established that under the Beneficiary's leadership,~!---~ 
has been adopted amongst other developers and technology startups to some degree. But while the 
evidence indicates that this software platform has achieved a level of popularity in the field and 
attracted fonding from well-known venture capitalist firms, the totality of the record is insufficient to 
show that this level of interest places the Beneficiary amongst the very few technology entrepreneurs 
at the top of the field. Notably, some of the Petitioner's claims of implementation by other startups 
are not supported in the record. Further, while the record indicates thatI Iis a product 
focused on user authentication for software applications, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that its 
popularity in this niche equates to acclaim for the Beneficiary in the broader field of technology 
entrepreneurialism. 
The Petitioner asserts that the Beneficiary's remuneration is significantly high, noting his 44% equity 
stake in the Petitioner which it asserts is valued at $17.6 million. We first note that of the SAFE 
agreements in the record which provide a figure for the "Post Money Valuation Cap" the Petitioner 
relies upon for this calculation, most place this figure at $35 million, which would put the value of the 
Beneficiary's stake in the Petitioner at $15.4 million. More importantly, the record shows that this is 
a highly speculative figure. Notably, the article from https://dlapiperaccelerate.com submitted with 
the Petitioner's initial filing defines "post-money value" as "the amount that a given pre-money value 
infers the company to be worth at the moment immediately following an investment." It goes on to 
define "pre-money value" as "the amount that an investor and the company agree to deem the company 
to be worth immediately prior to the investor's investment." After humorously explaining the process 
of determining pre-money value, the article explains that it "is often not derived from accounting 
measures such as revenue, free cash flow or EBITDA," and "is often highly negotiated, driven by 
market forces and inherently speculative." Accordingly, the figure proposed by the Petitioner for the 
Beneficiary's remuneration is insufficiently supported, and the record does not show that his salary or 
remuneration places him among those technology startup entrepreneurs at the very top of this field.
