Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a neurosurgeon, requests classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for 
U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
one of ten initial evidentiary requirements - two less than needed for a final merits determination. On 
appeal, she contends that she met six additional evidentiary requirements. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that she has not satisfied the minimum number of evidentiary criteria. 
We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (holding that USCIS' two-step analysis in EB-1 
cases "is consistent with the governing statute and regulation;" see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and Her Field 
The record shows that, in her native country of Brazil, the Petitioner earned a medical degree and a 
master's degree in health sciences. She also received specialized education in neuro-oncology and 
pediatric neurosurgery. Since 2012, she has worked for Brazilian hospitals as a neurosurgeon. She 
also recently opened her own office. The Petitioner has further authored or co-authored research 
articles in peer-reviewed journals and at conferences. She states that: 
the opportunity to reside and work as a neurosurgeon in the United States would allow 
me to demonstrate my experience and share the knowledge gained from my research 
in a country with abundant resources, cutting-edge tools, and modern techniques to 
improve patents' health, prevent diseases, and enhance their quality of life, which 
would be a significant contribution to the nation. 
B. The Initial Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - her receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. She therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of her 
authorship of scholarly articles in her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). On appeal, she contends 
that she met the following six other evidentiary requirements: 
• Membership in associations in her field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• Published material about her and her work in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in her field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in her field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for her services.

1. Membership in Associations 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for 
which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of her membership in international, Latin American, and European 
neurosurgical associations. She provided copies of the associations' constitutions and bylaws. She 
argues that all the groups require their members to be "formally vetted" by internal boards or 
committees comprised of national or international experts. 
As SCOPS found, however, the associations' constitutions and bylaws do not indicate that the groups' 
members must have outstanding achievements. The documents indicate that membership candidates 
must be neurosurgeons and that their membership applications must include their resumes and 
recommendation letters from members. The documents state that association boards or committees 
consider the applications before the groups' general memberships vote on them. But the 
documentation does not specify that members must have outstanding achievements. See Braga v. 
Poulos, No. CV 06-5105 SJO FMOX, 2007 WL 9229758, at *5 (C.D. Cal. July 6, 2007), aff'd, 317 F. 
App'x 680 (9th Cir. 2009) ("[The petitioner] has failed to show that any of these organizations require 
outstanding achievement ... to join and therefore he does not meet this criterion.") 
The Petitioner also claimed membership in American and Brazilian neurosurgical associations. But 
her evidence does not identify her as a member of those groups. She submitted a copy of a certificate 
indicating her attendance at the American association's 2025 annual meeting. But the certificate does 
not specify that she is a member ofthe association. Also, she provided a copy ofa letter recommending 
her membership in the Brazilian association. But the record lacks evidence that the Brazilian 
association ultimately admitted her as a member. 
The Petitioner submitted farther evidence of her membership in other medical associations. The 
record, however, lacks evidence that these other groups require outstanding achievements of their 
members or that recognized national or international experts judged their members. 
The Petitioner has not submitted documentation of her membership in associations in her field 
requiring outstanding achievements oftheir members as judged by recognized national or international 
experts. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding.

2. Published Material About the Petitioner 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, 
date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." Id. 
When adjudicating this criterion, USCIS first determines whether published material relates to a 
petitioner and their specific work in their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 3. If so, USCIS then determines whether the publication qualifies as a professional 
publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. Id. Relevant factors for professional 
or major trade publications include their intended audiences and, for major trade publications or other 
major media, their relative circulations, readerships, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted printouts ofresults for her name in Internet search engines and in an academic 
research database. The results list articles she has authored or co-authored, and presentations she has 
made at medical conferences. 
As SCOPS found, however, "a search site does not qualify as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication." Search engines are not publishers; rather, they are databases. 
Most users who visit the search engine sites that the Petitioner provided will not see information about 
her. Rather, only those visitors who search the databases for her name will see that information. 
The Petitioner also submitted additional evidence of research she has written and presented in her 
field. Contrary to the regulatory requirement, however, these materials are not "about the alien ... 
relating to the alien's work in the field." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Although the Petitioner 
authored or co-authored the materials, they are about diseases, such as medulloblastoma, and medical 
procedures, such as preoperative embolization. 
The Petitioner provided printouts of information about her from medical directories. This information 
primarily lists her credentials and area of specialization. The materials do not provide enough details 
to qualify as being about the Petitioner and her work in her field to meet this criterion. 
The Petitioner also submitted copies of pages from her personal website. These materials, however, 
appear designed to promote her services as a neurosurgeon. For example, the website states: "Trust 
us for neurosurgical procedures and personalized care in the comfort ofyour home, in the office or via 
telemedicine. Contact us now." Under USCIS policy,"[m]arketing materials created for the purpose 
of selling the person's products or promoting the person's services are not generally considered to be 
published material about the person." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Three. Thus, the 
Petitioner's website pages also do not meet this criterion. 
The Petitioner has not submitted published material about her and work in her field in professional or 
major trade publications or other major media. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding.

3. Judge of Others' Work 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a 
judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is 
sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that she served as: a teaching assistant in a 2022 course at a U.S. 
university school of public health; a moderator of a pediatric neurosurgery module at a 2023 
conference in Brazil; and a "preceptor" of a neurosurgical internship program at a Brazilian hospital. 
She asserts: "Collectively, these roles constitute direct and verifiable participation as a judge of the 
work ofothers in the field ofneurosurgery, meeting the evidentiary requirements under this regulatory 
criterion." 
The Petitioner, however, has not provided enough details or corroborating evidence about her activities 
to demonstrate her participation "as a judge of the work of others." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
She states that, as a teaching assistant, she "act[ ed] as a 'judge' of the students' work, including 
supervising them in class, grading assignments and calculating grades." But the evidence she 
submitted does not corroborate her claim. The certificate she provided recognizes her only "for your 
outstanding performance and exceptional commitment to teamwork." See generally 6 USCJS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(3) ("[S]tatements made by the witnesses should be corroborated by documentary 
evidence in the record."); see also Onaghise v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 
WL 4073344, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (affirming USCIS' finding that teaching, training, and 
mentoring other people in a field "do not constitute judging the work of others, either individually or 
on a panel.") 
The Petitioner states that, as a moderator of the pediatric neurosurgical module at the conference, her 
duties included "evaluating the quality and coherence of the lecturers' scientific contributions and 
ensuring adherence to academic standards central to a national neurosurgical congress." The record, 
however, lacks documentary evidence confirming her claim. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(3) ("[S]tatements made by the witnesses should be corroborated by documentary evidence in 
the record.") 
The Petitioner states that, as a preceptor of the neurosurgical internship program, she "evaluates and 
assesses the performance of advanced medical students." But documentation regarding the internship 
lacks copies of any evaluations or other details to confirm her claim. 
The Petitioner also contends that she evaluated and commented on research manuscripts of others in 
her field on academic databases and platforms, "providing professional feedback to authors 
worldwide." But she submitted insufficient evidence to confirm that claim. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence of her participation as a judge of others' work in her field. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding.

4. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence ofthe[ir] original scientific, scholarly, 
artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
users first determines whether a petitioner has made original contributions in their field. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Five. If so, USCrS then determines whether 
the original contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted: numerous letters from neurosurgeons, professors in the field, and patients; 
evidence of her authorship or co-authorship of research articles; and citation information about some 
of her articles. Despite these materials, SCOPS found insufficient evidence that the Petitioner's 
original contributions have had major significance in her field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner points to the expert letters she provided. She notes that: 
[d]etailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the 
person's contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed 
original contributions of major significance, particularly when the record includes 
documentation corroborating the claimed significance. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)(l ), Criterion Five. She contends that "her innovative surgical research, 
peer-reviewed publications and clinical methodologies have materially advanced the practice of 
neurosurgery - particularly in pediatric neuro-oncology." 
The expert letters that the Petitioner submitted, however, do not sufficiently explain the claimed 
significance of her contributions to her field. Most of the letters praise her credentials and skills. But 
the letters do not explain how she has purportedly influenced her field. A few of the letters mention 
her development of a transversal curved incision for lipoma of the conus surgery and her research on 
cerebellar mutism in the pediatric neuro-oncology field. But the letters do not explain the significance 
of these contributions. For example, a letter from a U.S. chief of spine surgery states that the 
Petitioner's research on cerebellar mutism "has already produced significant results, contributing to 
importance advancements in the field." But neither the letter nor the record's remaining evidence 
details the results of her research or explain its importance to the field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 
CV 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *8 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (affirming USCTS' finding that "[t]he 
reference letters do not contain detailed information showing the substantial influence the self­
petitioner's contributions have had on the overall field"); see generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l ), Criterion Five ("Submitted letters should specifically describe the person's contribution 
and its significance to the field.") (emphasis added). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that a published article she co-authored has generated more than 30 
citations from other researchers. 
[P]ublished research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from 
others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to 
6 
others' work in that field, may be probative of the significance of the person's 
contributions to the field of endeavor. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion Five. But the Petitioner has not documented whether the 
article's citation level is high for her field, or whether the article otherwise generated widespread 
commentary. She has therefore not sufficiently demonstrated the significance of her original

The Petitioner did not submit evidence of her original contributions of major significance to her field. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding. 
5. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for 
organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCTS first determines whether a petitioner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Eight. For a leading role, USCIS examines 
whether the evidence establishes the person as a leader within the organization, establishment, 
division, or department. Id. For a critical role, USCIS considers whether the evidence establishes 
that the person "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the 
organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or 
establishment." Id. 
Second, USCTS considers whether an organization, establishment, department, or division for which 
a petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Eight. The word "distinguished" ordinarily means 
"marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, 
Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. USCIS considers not only an 
organization's relative size or longevity but also other relevant factors such as the scale of its customer 
base or relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)( I), Criterion Eight. 
SCOPS found evidence that the Petitioner performed in a critical role for the hospitals where she has 
worked. But SCOPS concluded that the record does not establish that any of the hospitals have 
distinguished reputations. 
The Petitioner submitted information about each hospital. The record, however, does not establish 
that any have a distinguished reputation. The record does not explain each hospital's distinction. For 
example, a letter from the director of one institution describes it as the first municipal hospital in Brazil 
to attain "Qmentum International certification at the Diamond level." But neither the letter nor the 
remaining evidence explains the certification, the level, or their significance. 
7 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that she performed in a leading or critical role for 
organizations that have distinguished reputations. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding. 
C. The Remaining Requirements 
The Petitioner has not met five of the six additional evidentiary criteria she claims to have satisfied on 
appeal. Thus, she cannot meet the requisite amount of three evidentiary requirements. We therefore 
need not reach and hereby reserve consideration of her arguments that she met an additional 
evidentiary requirement regarding compensation for her services and is among the small percentage 
at her field's very top. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the minimum number of evidentiary criteria for the requested 
immigrant visa category. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
