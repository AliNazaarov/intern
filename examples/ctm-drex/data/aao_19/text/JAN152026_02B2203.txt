Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a cosmetics expert, seeks permanent employment-based immigrant classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( I )(A), 
8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three often initial criteria for the classification as required and had 
not demonstrated she sought to enter the United States to continue to work in her area of claimed 
expertise. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(b)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Field of Expertise 
Upon de novo review, we withdraw SCOPS' conclusion that the evidence in the record indicated the 
Petitioner did not intend to continue to work in her claimed area of extraordinary ability in the United 
States. We are not unsympathetic to SCOPS' concerns. The evidence in the record confusingly 
expresses the Petitioner's claims and proposed activities in the United States. However, for the reasons 
below we conclude the Petitioner's contemplated activities occupy space within the realm of her 
claimed area of extraordinary ability. 
The statute and regulations require a petitioner seek to continue work in the area ofexpertise in the United 
States. See sections 203(b)(l)(A)(i) and (ii) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1153(b)(l)(A)(i) and (ii), and 
8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3) and (5). The Petitioner indicated the beneficiary's job description as leading 
a team to develop healthy and safe cosmetics. And whilst the "career plan" emphasized the formation 
of a proposed business, it also asserted the Petitioner would create cosmetic "formulations exclusive 
to the U.S. brand, focusing on healthy, organic products adhering to ... stringent health and safety 
standards" in pursuit of "create[ing] a global beauty brand." So, in the instant matter before us, we 
conclude the record supports the Petitioner's intention to continue working in her field of endeavor. 
Thus, we will consider if the Petitioner satisfies the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) through her 
achievements as a cosmetics expert.

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of 
accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087 at *l (stating that EB-1 
visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount ofindividuals with extraordinary 
ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of her work is indicative ofthe 
required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work 
in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. Whilst the Petitioner need not establish that there is 
no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to 
demonstrate that she is among the small percentage at the top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
