Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner received a 
major, internationally recognized award or met the requirements of at least three ofthe ten evidentiary 
criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable evidence ifhe or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). users concluded that the Petitioner satisfied the criterion related to 
performing a leading and critical role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). However, USCrS farther 
determined that the Petitioner did not meet the remaining three claimed evidentiary criteria associated 
with original contribution, high salary or remuneration, and comparable evidence to authorship of 
scholarly articles at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), (vi), (ix) and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that he meets these criteria. Upon de novo review, we conclude that the Petitioner 
has not met the requisite number of evidentiary criteria and therefore is not eligible for classification 
as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
A. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that his or her contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, 
have remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major 
significance. See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an 
original contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation ofthe alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative ofmajor significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay 
statements. Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and 
location should be submitted to establish that his or her compensation is high relative to others working 
in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner contends he is eligible for this criterion based on his salary of $248,700 as a "Specialist 
Leader" atl I However, he did not provide the proper comparable salary information for his 
particular occupation. The Petitioner contends that the title of "Specialist Leader" is specific to his 
employer and that the duties are comparable to that of "Computer and Information Systems 
Managers." The evidence does not demonstrate that the two positions are comparable. For example, 
in the article "Computer and Information Systems Managers: Salary, career path, job outlook, 
education and more," the position duties, in part, include to "analyze [the] organization's computer 
needs and recommend possible upgrades for top executives to consider, " to "plan and direct the 
installation and maintenance of computer hardware and software," and to "ensure the security of an 
organization's network and electronic documents."2 This position appears to be an internally facing 
managerial position dealing with the daily operations of an organization and its technological needs. 
In a letter of support from S-C-, the author describes the Petitioner's role as a "trusted advisor to 
consumer business executives in the strategy, planning, and implementation of AI/ML- integrated 
performance management technologies" and as a "a negotiator and deal maker with leading companies 
in the consumer and high-tech space ...." This letter indicates his position is outward facing and deals 
with the company's Financial Planning and Analysis teams and its customer solutions. Therefore, we 
conclude the positions are not comparable. Both precedent and case law support this application of 8

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner claimed to satisfy four of the ten initial evidence requirements under 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3). However, we conclude that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the four claimed 
criteria. Since the Petitioner has not fulfilled the initial evidentiary requirement of three and has not 
satisfied the threshold initial evidence requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we need not determine 
whether the Petitioner meets the final claimed evidentiary criteria related to comparable evidence of 
authorship of scholarly articles. 3 Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary 
ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and 
present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial 
evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of 
extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d 
at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision).
