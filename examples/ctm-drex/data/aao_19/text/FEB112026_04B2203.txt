Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a cloud engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner satisfied the initial 
evidentiary requirements for this classification but did not establish, as required, that she has sustained 
national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of her field. The 
matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for the entry of a new decision consistent 
with the foregoing analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue to work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376.

A. Published materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual at F.2(B)(l ). 
In support, the Petitioner provided an article from The Nation, along with a Similarweb printout. The 
Similarweb printout indicates "Total visits" of 1.404 million from August 2024 to October 2024. 
However, the Petitioner has not explained how this statistic supports a determination that The Nation 
qualifies as "major media" by having a high circulation or distribution relative to other online 
publications. Evidence of published material in major media publications about the Petitioner should 
establish that the circulation (online or in print) or viewership is high compared to other statistics and 
identify the intended audience. Id. Here, the record does not contextualize those statistics, indicate 
their significance, or elaborate on how that information could establish that the website is the type of 
major media contemplated by 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Moreover, given the lack of information 
regarding the intended audience of The Nation, the record does not establish that it is a professional 
or trade publication. 
In addition, the Petitioner provided a press release from Marquis Who's Who, along with a published 
interview with the editor of Who's Who and an article about the 60th edition of Who's Who in The 
New York Times. First, the press release does not include the author ofthe material as required. Kumar 
v. Dir. ofUS. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. 
Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding plaintiff's evidence did not include the required title, date, or author). 
Second, as the article is labeled as a "press release," it indicates that an unnamed third party submitted 
the article for publication. The origin of the article is material to this proceeding because promotional 
or marketing materials are not generally considered to be published material about the person. This 
includes seemingly objective content about the person in major print publications that the person or 
the person's employer paid for. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual at F.2(B)(l). While the article 
is identified as a press release, the Petitioner has not identified the source of that press release, which 
undermines the value of the article. 
We therefore withdraw SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner satisfied this criterion.

a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 
A credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location

Both precedent and case law support this application of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). See Matter of

As the record does not establish that the Petitioner meets three ofthe ten regulatory criteria and SCOPS 
did not address the Petitioner's other claimed criteria, we will remand the matter. SCOPS should 
consider all of the evidence submitted in support of the claimed criteria and specifically address its 
deficiencies and inconsistencies. 
For example, regarding the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), the Petitioner asserts eligibility for 
this criterion based on her Global Recognition Award and the Gartner's Global Supply Chain Top 25 
awarded to her former employer for rising to number four in the supply chain rankings. In support, 
the Petitioner submitted information about the awards including media articles and criteria. Notably,

Finally, to establish original contribution criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), the Petitioner

SCOPS should determine if the evidence shows that the Petitioner has made original contributions of 
major significance in the field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
If SCOPS determines the Petitioner satisfies three of the ten criteria listed in the regulations, SCOPS 
should conduct a final merits determination of the totality of the evidence (including the Petitioner's 
claims on appeal). We observe that SCOPS did not consider all of the evidence together under the 
final merits determination. The new decision should include an analysis of the totality of the evidence 
evaluating whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained 
national or international acclaim, her status as one of the small percentage at the very top of her field 
of endeavor, and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
ORDER: SCOPS' decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
