Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a marketing and communications director in the field of digital marketing, requests 
classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an 
alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 
(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this 
category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document 
recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner 
satisfied three initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, she did not show as required her 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that she is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

C. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall 
include the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation. " 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
To fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate there is published material about her in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, as well as the title, date, and author of 
the material. The published material should be about the alien and his or her work, rather than the 
alien's employer and his or her work (or about an organization and its work). Professional or major 
trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, whereas other major media and major 
trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to other media in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner's claim to eligibility under this criterion is grounded in her contention that she has been 
featured in national and international media outlets for her leadership in marketing innovation and 
entrepreneurship. However, the evidence in the record does not support her claim of eligibility under 
this criterion.

H. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner did not meet the evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), 
(iii) of the Act. So, we are not required to make a final merits determination. But, as the Petitioner's 
appeal contends that she has sustained acclaim and will substantially benefit the United States of 
America prospectively as a permanent resident, we will conduct a final merits determination. Upon 
our de novo review of the record we conclude that, even if the Petitioner had established that she met 
the evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, we would 
nonetheless conclude she did not demonstrate eligibility for the classification because the evidence 
does not indicate she is one of the small percentage at the very top of her field of endeavor. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if her successes are sufficient to by a preponderance of the evidence 
demonstrate that she has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor through sustained national or 
international acclaim, that she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, 
and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
The record as a whole does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's achievements are reflective of a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990). As stated above, the Petitioner claims to have received several awards in her 
individual capacity. She also claims awards received by her previous employers for apps, campaigns, 
or other initiatives the employer undertook during her period ofemployment with them. But the record 
does not indicate that the receipt of the commendations the Petitioner presented, either individually 
for herself or for her employers, reflects sustained acclaim in her field nationally or internationally 
such that we can ascertain if she has risen to the level ofthe small percentage ofindividuals at the very 
top of the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner's membership in~------------------------~is 
not supported with evidence that sustained national or international acclaim or a career of acclaimed 
work is supported by entry into the memberships. See Donskoy v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. 
Servs., No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 11, 2021) (upholding the 
petitioner's membership did not demonstrate the requisite level of achievement or sustained acclaim 
to establish extraordinary ability). 
Moreover, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how her memberships reflected that she was amongst 
the small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. The record does not contain 
evidence indicating that she received national or international recognition based on her memberships. 
In addition, the Petitioner did not establish that she has distinguished herself from others in the field 
based on her memberships, gaining national or international attention or placing her among the upper 
echelon in her field. 
Regarding the Petitioner's service as a judge of the work of others, an evaluation of the significance 
of her experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence indicates the required extraordinary 
15 
ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22.2 The Petitioner 
listed several occasions where she served in a judging capacity and so met the plain language of the 
criterion. However, the Petitioner did not establish that these judging opportunities contribute to a 
finding that she has a career of acclaimed work in the field or indicate her sustained national or 
international acclaim. The Petitioner did not show, for example, how her judging experience compares 
to others at the very top of the field. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner apart from others in his 
field, such as evidence that she has a consistent history of reviewing or judging recognized, acclaimed 
experts in her field, the Petitioner has not shown that her narrow judging experience places her among 
that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See Hamal v. United 
States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding the petitioner did not 
show that he served as a judge of renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists or the competitions 
were considered prestigious or attracted significant attention by the field). 
Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The record 
reflects the Petitioner presented evidence showing that she has published a book and articles in 
scholarly journals. But the Petitioner did not show the significance of her authorships or how her 
publication record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Jafarov 
v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 
(S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding it reasonable for USCIS to focus on the fact that a publication record 
of six publications over seven years inconsistent with a career of acclaimed work and sustained 
national or international acclaim). And the Petitioner provided no citation history or other evidence 
of the influence of her written work so that we could evaluate the impact and recognition that her 
publications have had on the field and whether such influence has been sustained. See Bhanu v. 
Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the Petitioner did 
not show the significance of authorships reflecting that he is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field). 
The Petitioner provided recommendation letters generally praising her and her work. But the letters 
do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show the Petitioner made original 
contributions of major significance such that the Petitioner is viewed by the overall field, rather than 
by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that she has garnered recognition on a national or 
international scale, consistent with being among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 
20, 2021) (finding the plaintiff submitted letters only from people within his circle of professional 
acquaintances, and none of the letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his 
accomplishments to show sustained national or international acclaim). Although the letters highlight 
the Petitioner's talents and responsibilities, they do not reflect a career of acclaimed work in the field, 
garnering the required sustained national or international acclaim. See Bodhankar v. United States 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (finding the 
plaintiff's work for organizations did not result in national or international acclaim). Moreover, the 
Petitioner did not establish that her roles received widespread acclaim from the field, drew significant 
attention from the greater field, or the overall field view her among the leading individuals in the field.

The record, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's eligibility for

statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability." Matter ofPrice, 20 
I&N Dec. at 954. Whilst the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to 
qualify for the classification sought, we find the record insufficient to demonstrate that she has 
sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the top of her field.
