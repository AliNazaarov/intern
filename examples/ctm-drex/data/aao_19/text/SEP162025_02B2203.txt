Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a data scientist, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification 
makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their 
field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had satisfied 
at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Membership in Associations 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a pet1t10ner must submit "[d]ocumentation of the alien's 
membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). Associations may have multiple levels of 
membership, and the level of membership afforded to the alien must show that in order to obtain that 
level of membership, the alien was judged by recognized national or international experts as having 
attained outstanding achievements in the field for which classification is sought. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l), Criterion 2. 
The Petitioner argues that her "senior membership" with the Institute of Electrical and Electronics 
Engineers (IEEE) fulfills this criterion. The Petitioner submitted her IEEE membership certificate, 
which states that she is a "Senior Member in good standing through December 2024, denoting a 
personal and professional commitment to the advancement of technology," and a copy of the IEEE 
bylaws and website information regarding the requirements for "IEEE Senior Member Grade." 
In denying the petition, SCOPS determined that the IEEE's stated requirements for admission as a 
senior member did not rise to the level of "outstanding achievements of its members, as judged by 
recognized national or international experts" in the field, consistent with this regulatory criterion. The 
Petitioner claims on appeal that SCOPS did not "meaningfully engage with the petitioner's arguments 
and supporting documentation" and "a decision [was] made without a thorough or individualized 
assessment." 
Upon de novo review, we acknowledge that SCOPS did not fully explain the reasoning behind this 
determination; however, we nevertheless agree with SCOPS's ultimate finding that the Petitioner's 
membership in the IEEE falls short of meeting the requirements of this criterion. Relevant factors that 
may lead to a conclusion that the person's membership in the association(s) was not based on 
outstanding achievements in the field include, but are not limited to, instances where the person's 
membership was based solely on the following factors (by themselves or in the aggregate): a level of 
education or years of experience in a particular field; the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an 
association's publications; and a requirement, compulsory or otherwise, for employment in certain 
occupations, as commonly seen with union membership or guild affiliation for actors. Id. 
According to the IEEE bylaws, "a candidate shall be an engineer, scientist, educator, technical 
executive or originator in IEEE-designated fields ... The candidate shall have been in professional 
practice for at least ten years and shall have shown significant performance of a period of at least five 
ofthose years." The requirements for the "Senior Member Grade" on the IEEE website further specify 
that "significant performance" does not require "special awards, patents, or other extremely 
sophisticated technical accomplishments," but can include "substantial job responsibilities such as 
team leader, task supervisor, engineer in charge of a program or project, engineer or scientist 
performing research with some measure of success (papers), or faculty developing and teaching 
courses with research and publications." 
3 
The record, therefore, does not demonstrate that the IEEE's standards for the senior grade membership 
rise to the level of "outstanding achievements" as contemplated by the plain language of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). While this membership level does not appear to be available to all professionals 
with 10 years of experience in the field, the Petitioner must establish that IEEE requires senior 
members to have "outstanding achievements" as an essential condition of membership to satisfy this 
criterion. Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06-5105 SJO FMOX, 2007 WL 9229758, at *5 (C.D. Cal. July 6, 
2007), aff'd, 317 F. App'x 680 (9th Cir. 2009) (finding the organization itself must require outstanding 
achievements as a condition for membership). The Petitioner has not established that the IEEE 
requirements of 10 years of relevant experience in a particular field with "substantial job 
responsibilities [ ofbeing a team leader, supervisor, in charge of projects, or performing research]" rise 
to the level of "outstanding achievements" consistent with this regulatory criterion. 
In addition, the Petitioner did not establish that recognized national or international experts judge the 
outstanding achievements for senior membership with IEEE as required by 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The bylaws discuss how applications for membership are "referred to the Admission and 
Advancement Committee" but the Petitioner has not shown that this committee's makeup consists of 
recognized national or international experts in the field. 
The Petitioner also claimed that she meets this criterion through her membership with the British 
Computer Society (BCS). However, we agree with SCOPS that this membership was obtained after 
the filing of the petition and is not probative. A petitioner must establish their eligibility "at the time 
of filing the benefit request." 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l). In fact, the Petitioner acknowledges that her 
BCS membership was obtained in 2025, after the filing of this petition. 
Based on the foregoing, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that she meets this regulatory criterion. 
2. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). For this 
criterion, the Petitioner must establish that not only has she made original contributions but that they 
have been of major significance in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. For example, a petitioner may show that her contributions have been widely 
implemented throughout the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance in the field. 
See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022) (for a petitioner's contributions to be "ofmajor 
significance," evidence must demonstrate that their work has been widely adopted or replicated by 
industry actors unaffiliated with them).

3. Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). A critical role means an alien contributed in a significantly important way 
to the activities ofthe organization, establishment, division, or department. See generally 6 USC IS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 8. Next, the Petitioner must establish that the organization, 
establishment, division, or department has a distinguished reputation. The word "distinguished" 
ordinarily means "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." 
Distinguished, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant 
considerations in determining whether an organization is "distinguished" include not only its relative 
size and longevity but also other factors, such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage of it. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l ), Criterion 8.

4. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). Evidence 
regarding whether the person's compensation is high relative to that of others working in the field may 
take many forms. Examples may include, but are not limited to, geographical or position-appropriate 
compensation surveys and organizational justifications to pay above the compensation data. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l), Criterion 9. 
The Petitioner initially submitted evidence of her monthly payroll statements from Turkey as well the 
I ldata showing an average salary of a "Manager Data Scientist." However, 
in the request for evidence (RFE), SCOPS correctly pointed out that average salary levels are not 
comparable when evaluating whether a salary was high "in relation to others in the field." E.g., 
Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 2023) (finding USCIS reasonably concluded a 
filing party's evidence comparing average and median salaries was insufficient to establish he 
commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field). 
7 
In response to the RFE, the Petitioner offered salary figures of data scientists and data analysts from 
kariyer.net. However, the salary information from kariyer.net was based on wage surveys "prepared 
with data from 79 users" for data scientists and "prepared with data from 92 users" for data analysts. 
Therefore, SCOPS properly concluded that such salary data was neither sufficient nor probative source 
of evidence for the positions the Petitioner has held, especially given that there is no documentation 
establishing the validity of these surveys. 
On appeal, the Petitioner provides additional salary information from glassdoor.com and indeed.com, 
as well as Reed's 2024 Salary Guide for Turkey. However, as the Petitioner has been given an 
opportunity to respond to the deficiency in the evidence, we will not accept evidence offered for the 
first time on appeal. Matter ofSoriano, 19 I&N Dec. 764 (BIA 1988); Matter ofObaigbena, 19 I&N 
Dec. 533 (BIA 1988). 
Even if we were to accept this evidence in addition to the Petitioner's initial filing and RFE response, 
it does not establish eligibility. Although the Petitioner claimed that she meets this criterion solely on 
the wages that she earned in Turkey, the printout from indeed.com only shows salary figures for data 
scientists in the United States. USCIS evaluates persons working outside of the United States based 
on the wage statistics or comparable evidence relevant to the applicable work location, rather than by 
simply converting the salary to U.S. dollars and then viewing whether that salary would be considered 
high in the United States. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l), Criterion 9. 
Moreover, the Petitioner has not shown that the printout from glassdoor.com corresponds to the salary 
of data scientists who are similarly situated. The salary figures in the printout do not show that they 
are grouped by similar locality, region, or country, or differentiated by employee experience levels or 
employment types. Therefore, they are insufficient to provide a valid point of comparison to establish 
that the Petitioner's salary was significantly high "in relation to others in the field." 
Furthermore, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that Reed's salary information for data analysts in 
Turkey corresponds to the salary of data scientists who may perform higher level of work and duties. 
On the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Worker, the Petitioner classified her position under 
Standard Occupation Classification (SOC) code 15-2051, "Data Scientist" and listed her job titled on 
the Form I-140 as "Senior Lead Data Scientist." Therefore, her assertion that her position's salary 
aligns with that of data analysts is not the proper basis for comparison. Both precedent and case law 
support this application of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( considering a professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); 
see also Skokos v. US. Dept. ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding 
salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); 
Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus 
other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) (comparing salary of 
NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
Because of the deficiencies in the various salary evidence provided, the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated that her salary is high when compared to others in the field who are similarly situated. 
The evidence does not provide a clear picture ofthe comparable range of salaries earned by other data 
scientists in Turkey. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not establish that she satisfies this criterion.

B. Reserved Issues 
The above conclusions determine the outcome of the appeal. Therefore, we decline to reach and 
hereby reserve the Petitioner's appellate arguments regarding whether she seeks "to enter the United 
States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability" and whether her entry "will substantially 
benefit prospectively the United States," under section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii)-(iii) of the Act. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need 
not farther analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also 
Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F. Supp. 3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the 
regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks 
an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility 
for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
