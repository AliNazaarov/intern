Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a photographer and multimedia artist, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability in the arts. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 
8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to 
those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record does not satisfy at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b )( 1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). If a criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily apply to 
a petitioner, they may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner intends to "continue producing high-end fashion and bridal, and celebrity photography 
for recognized clients and publications, while contributing to the visual arts field through exhibitions." 
USCIS concluded that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vii) but not

§§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x).

their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields."

On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that I I has "a selective admission process based on 
merit/achievement." However, he does not claim that recognized national or international experts in 
their disciplines or fields judge prospective members. 
The record contains a certified English translation of _ statutes and admission regulations, which 
list five minimum membership criteria: 
1. Proven professional experience of at least 8 to 10 years, or equivalent achievements of public 
recognition. 
2. Exceptional artistic quality, assessed through a professional portfolio. 
3. Recognitions, awards, publications or exhibitions that demonstrate outstanding merit. 
4. Verifiable professional recommendations. 
5. High standards of professional ethics. 
Here, the five minimum membership criteria do not sufficiently establish that requires 
outstanding achievements of its members. The first criterion contemplates "achievements of public 
recognition," but it does not require such achievements to be outstanding, and those achievements may 
be substituted with "8 to 10 years" of professional experience, regardless of whether the individual 
accrued outstanding achievements during that period. The second criterion addresses subjective 
"artistic quality," not achievements. Although the third criterion focuses on awards or other 
recognition, it seeks demonstration of "outstanding merit," not outstanding achievements. The fourth 
criterion considers recommendations from others, and the fifth criterion generally requires 
professional ethics, without elaborating on how the evaluation committee would objectively determine 
this. 
Further, neither I Istatutes and admission regulations nor the remainder of the record establishes 
that recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields judge prospective 
members. Although the professional evaluation committee composition article contemplates that two 
of the three or four evaluation committee members will have a "recognized track record," it does not 
clarify whether they must be nationally or internationally recognized, and whether their records must 
indicate that they are recognized as experts in their disciplines or fields. In tum, the third mandatory

C. Published Material About the Alien 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the 
person's products or promoting the person's services are not generally considered to be published 
material about the person (this includes seemingly objective content about the person in major print 
publications that the person or the person's employer paid for). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F .2(B)(l ). Published material that covers a broad topic may be considered material about 
the alien relating to the alien's work if it includes a substantial discussion of the alien's work in the 
field and mentions the person in connection to the work. See generally id. Relevant factors include 
the intended audience (for professional or major trade publications) and the relative circulation, 
readership, or viewership (for major trade publications or other major media). See generally id. 
USCIS acknowledged that the record contains several documents without identified authors or dates 
of publication, and one article that addresses many artists, including the Petitioner, published online 
by El Pais, datedl 2025. However, USCIS observed that none of the articles contain a 
substantial discussion of the Petitioner's work in the field. Moreover, USCIS noted that the lack of 
either a date of publication or an author for the majority of the documents does not satisfy the plain 
language of the criterion. And even if the documents otherwise met the criterion's requirements, 
USCIS further addressed the record's omission of information about intended audiences and 
circulation data for both those publishers and their competitors, in order to establish whether the 
publishers qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media. See generally id. 
Based on those issues, USICS determined that the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner generally states that the "record contains multiple feature-style items­
print/digital features and broadcast segments-where I am the subject or the clearly identified 
professional discussed," consisting of"substantial discussion within the meaning ofthe criterion." He 
submits a document that purports to provide, in relevant part, a summary ofcirculation data for El Pais 
and other publications, with lists of internet URLs; however, he does not submit printed copies of the 
websites that he claims provide this information.

D. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." Websites 
that provide user-reported salary data may not be valid comparisons of an alien's salary or other 
remuneration in relation to others in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). 
USCIS acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted copies of his 2019-23 Colombian tax returns and 
comparative salary information for photographers in Colombia in general and in I IColombia, 
specifically. However, USCIS observed that the Petitioner's tax returns include income from capital 
gains and rent, which interfered with USCIS' ability to determine the portion of his income derived 
from photography rather than "income he received as an owner or investor." USCIS further noted that 
the comparative salary information merely "consists of average wage data rather than high salary data 
7 
for photographers," which does not inform whether the Petitioner may have commanded a high salary 
or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field. Based on those 
issues, USCIS determined that the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner reiterates, "Colombian tax returns (2019-2023) and wage benchmarks 
demonstrate remuneration well above typical photographer compensation in Colombia." 
Beyond the issue of whether the Petitioner's 2019-23 Colombian tax returns adequately differentiate 
between his sources of income in a given year, there are additional deficiencies. The record contains 
information from four sources: Tulsario, Computrabajo, the Economic Research Institute, and Jobted. 
The Tulsario information states, "Minimum and maximum salary for a Photographer - from COP 
$2,278,755 to COP $12,324,908 per month - 2025," but it does not inform what the salary range was 
for any time period from 2019 to 2023, the years of the Petitioner's tax returns. Similarly, the 
Computrabajo information reports the average monthly salary for a photographer in Colombia "in the 
last 12 months" with the "latest update [on] December 6, 2025," although it reports that the monthly 
average was COP $1,671,995 per month, which is substantially less than the minimum monthly salary 
of COP $2,278,755 reported by Tulsario. The Economic Research Institute information was "Last 
updated: December 12, 2025," but it does not clarify the period(s) in which it reports the average 
photographer salary inl IColombia. Likewise, the Jobted information is copyrighted 2013-25, 
but it does not elaborate on what period( s) it reports the average monthly salary for photographers in 
Colombia, which we note is even less than that reported by Computrabajo. Here, the Petitioner must 
resolve these inconsistencies in the record with independent, objective evidence pointing to where the 
truth lies. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. at 591-92. 
Even ifthe wage information sources reported what the salary or other remuneration for services others 
in the field received during the years for which the Petitioner submitted his Colombian tax returns, 
which they do not, and even if they were consistent, which they are not, the wage surveys are overly 
broad and they rely on flawed methodology, which further reduce their probative value. The wage 
information notes that photographers' salaries vary substantially based on factors such as the 
individual's level of professional experience and areas of expertise, such as fashion photographers, 
wedding and event photographers, advertising photographers, scientific photographers, still 
photographers, photojournalists, aerial photographers, photographic directors, etc. Therefore, the 
broad generalization of all photographers, averaging all salaries among all levels of experience and 
specializations, obscures what the salaries or other remuneration of photographers with experience 
and specialization( s) similar to the Petitioner were, whether in 2025 or during the years addressed by 
his Colombian tax returns, 2019-23. In turn, the wage surveys' methodology relied on"salary survey 
data collected directly from employers and anonymous employees in Colombia" and 
generalized "sources obtained from companies, users and employees in the last 12 months." The wage 
surveys do not establish that their methodology objectively determined what photographers' income 
actually was during any particular period, as opposed to relying on partial, selective, and self-reported 
data. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
In summation, the record does not establish whether the Petitioner commanded a high salary or other 
significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field, during the same period 
of time and at the same level of experience and specialization; therefore, it does not satisfy this 
criterion.

For the foregoing reasons, the record does not satisfy at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
