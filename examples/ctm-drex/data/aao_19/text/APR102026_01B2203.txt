Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur and inventor, requests classification under the employment-based, 
first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien of "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). Successful 
petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or 
international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that, contrary to the Act, 
the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he seeks to continue working in the United States in his field 
or that his work would substantially benefit the country. SCOPS also found that he did not meet any 
initial evidentiary criteria for the requested category. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS 
misapplied evidentiary standards and misevaluated evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that he did not satisfy the requisite amount of evidentiary criteria. Wewe 
will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (establishing a two-step process for 
adjudicating extraordinary ability petitions); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner graduated from an industrial school in his home country of 
Moldova,2 where he specialized in refrigeration equipment and automation systems. After working 
for a few years in Russia, he returned to Moldova where he started a furniture business with his twin 
brother. Although a regionwide economic crisis forced him and his brother to sell the business, they 
ultimately founded other companies in fields such as online commerce and construction. The 
Petitioner further developed scientific devices, obtained a patent, and worked for his region's 
government. Fearing that Russian security services would arrest him for his pro-Ukrainian and pro­
European beliefs, he said he left Moldova in February 2022. 
Now in Germany, the Petitioner states that he has several projects that he wants to develop in the 
United States, including a hydrogen generator prototype. He states that, if this petition is approved, 
he can build successful businesses in this country based on his inventions. He states: "Without 
exaggeration, your favorable decision will be historic! I am certain that this decision will change not 
only my future, but also America's."

recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at

1. Nationally or Internationally Recognized A wards 
This criterion requires "documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner - as opposed to their 
employer or a company they own, for example - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion One. If so, USCIS then considers whether the awards received 
national or international recognition and were issued for excellence in the relevant field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of a certificate, and a transcript and photographs from a television 
program, demonstrating that he won a $15,000 grant in a 2014 competition for start-up businesses. 
He states that, out of 344 total business plans, his was one of 15 selected for a grant in the international 
competition, which was organized by the European Union and the United Nations Development 
Program. 
As SCOPS found, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the award generated national or 
international recognition. He described the competition as "international" and stated that his award 
received "extensive coverage on all public and private TV and radio channels." But the record lacks 
objective evidence that the media coverage was national or international in scope. See Visinscaia v. 
Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence of how a larger audience viewed 
Plaintiff's awards, there was no way for the agency to evaluate whether those awards were recognized 
widely enough to satisfy this criterion.") 
3 
The Petitioner also claimed that, in a similar contest for start-up businesses in 2019, his proposal for 
a water purification company placed first in Moldova. He stated that, in the contest's following part, 
his proposal competed internationally against start-up plans from entrepreneurs in 144 other countries. 
The record, however, contains insufficient evidence to confirm the Petitioner's receipt of the claimed 
2019 award, or its generation of national or international recognition. He submitted copies of photos, 
one of which shows him and another person holding an award. But, in the photo, the words on the 
award are illegible. The other photo shows the Petitioner and others cheering, purportedly during the 
award's announcement. The evidence lacks sufficient details to confirm his receipt of the claimed 
award. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) 
( affirming USCIS' finding of insufficient evidence to meet this criterion where "there was no 
'primary' evidence of the awards"). 
The Petitioner has not submitted documentation establishing his receipt ofnationally or internationally 
recognized awards for excellence in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding. 
2. Judging Others' Work 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge ofthe work ofothers in the same or an allied field ofspecification 
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he served on government commissions and work groups 
regarding small businesses and related issues. SCOPS concluded: "The evidence provided does not 
show that the petitioner has been invited to judge the work of others, and there is no evidence to 
establish that the petitioner actually participated in the judging of the work of others." 
In response to SCOPS' request for additional evidence, the Petitioner had argued that, "[i]n the context 
of EB- lA requirements, 'judging' includes formal expert assessment, criticism, and the formulation 
of conclusions regarding legislative, regulatory, and programmatic initiatives developed by other 
individuals or government bodies." He gave examples of when, as a member of government panels, 
he purportedly criticized laws or proposals. 
The Petitioner's examples indicate that, as a member of commissions and work groups, he criticized 
government officials or their work. But the criterion requires evidence that he judged others "in the 
same or an allied field." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). The record identifies the Petitioner as an 
entrepreneur, businessman, and inventor. The evidence does not demonstrate that, as a member of 
government commissions and work groups, he judged the work of other entrepreneurs, 
businesspeople, or inventors, or members of an allied field. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that he judged others' work in the same or an allied field. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this requirement.

C. The Remaining Issues 
The Petitioner has not met two of the four evidentiary criteria he claims to have satisfied on appeal. 
Thus, he cannot meet the requisite amount of three evidentiary criteria. We therefore need not reach 
and hereby reserve consideration of his arguments that he: 
• Satisfied two other evidentiary criteria; 
• Demonstrated his intent to continue working in his field in the United States; 
• Established that his work would substantially benefit the country; and 
• Demonstrated overall exceptional ability in his field in a final merit determination. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not meet the requisite number of initial evidentiary criteria for the requested 
immigrant visa category. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
