Appeal of Texas Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a general and operations manager, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability in the field of business. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 
8 U.S.C. § l l 53(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish 
the Petitioner qualifies as an individual of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, 
internationally recognized award, or at least three of the ten regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
The Petitioner indicated on the Form I-140, Immigrant Petitioner for Alien Workers, that he is 
applying for the employment-based, first preference visa as an alien of extraordinary ability. In 
response to the Director's notice of intent to deny (NOID), the Petitioner reiterated the same. 
Therefore, we will review the appeal under the regulations at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h). In addition, upon 
review ofthe Petitioner's response to the NOID, the Petitioner's documents in a foreign language were 
accompanied by a full English language translation. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; who seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and whose entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner 
can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement, that is, a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit this 
evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of 
the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Where a petitioner meets the initial evidence requirements through either a one-time achievement or 
meeting three lesser criteria, we then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits 
determination and assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
The Petitioner stated in the record that he will provide advisory services and guide businesses in process 
automation, digital customer service, and strategic planning, while also supporting emerging 
entrepreneurs by providing mentorship, networking opportunities, and access to investors. He further 
stated that his endeavor, rooted in technological and human development, directly aligns with the current 
demand for qualified professionals to drive innovation and sustain economic growth in the U.S. He 
asserted that his extraordinary ability and proven contributions to the business sector will undoubtedly 
have a lasting impact on the U.S. economy and society. The petitioner outlined his "extensive education, 
diverse skill set, and international exposure" and noted that it all "positions him as an extraordinary and 
well-rounded professional in the fields ofbusiness administration and management." 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner stated that he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (v), (vi), (vii), 
and, (viii) for receipts of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in the 
field of endeavor; membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; publications 
about the petitioner in professional or major trade publications or major media; original contributions 
of major significance; authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media; display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases; and, leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments. The Director 
detailed the evidence and provided an analysis for each of the claimed criteria and discussed how the 
evidence did not sufficiently demonstrate that the Petitioner met each criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that an "inadequate analysis and reliance on an initial superficial 
review of the translations has resulted in a failure to conduct a thorough review as mandated, leading 
to a premature conclusion that I do not satisfy the criteria for EBl category." The Petitioner does not 
specifically contest the Director's decision and requests a "thorough and detailed review of each piece 
of evidence." However, the Director listed the evidence submitted by the Petitioner and provided an 
analysis for each of the claimed criteria and discussed how the evidence did not sufficiently 
demonstrate that the Petitioner met each criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner did not present an 
argument or submit evidence to overcome the Director's concerns of each criterion. Giday v. INS, 
113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (declining to address a "passing reference" to an argument in a 
brief that did not provide legal support). The brief contains no evidence or arguments addressing the

For example, regarding the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), the Director listed the evidence 
submitted by the Petitioner including his membership in the audiovisual and integrated experience 
association; the SGS (general company of supervision); the national professional council of business 
administration (integrity, service and excellence); the association of cattlemen I I and, his 
professional license as a business administrator. The Director noted that the documentation submitted 
in support of the membership requirements did not meet the plain language of the criterion and 
determined that the Petitioner did not submit documentary evidence demonstrating that outstanding 
achievements are required for membership in these organizations. In addition, the Petitioner does not 
explain if all of these associations relate to his field of business administration and management, and 
whether the associations utilize national or internationally recognized experts to judge the 
achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are outstanding. On appeal, 
the Petitioner solely requests that the evidence submitted with the petition is reviewed but does not 
provide evidence or arguments to overcome this issue. 
The Director also outlined the evidence submitted in support of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and 
explained that the record did not include documentation showing that the materials were published in 
any form of media listed in the criterion. Again, the Petitioner did not present any evidence or 
arguments on appeal to overcome the Director's concerns. 
Pertaining to original contributions of major significance at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), the Director 
listed several documents submitted for this criterion and stated that the evidence does not demonstrate 
the major significance of the Petitioner's original contributions. The Director also noted discrepancies 
found in certain letters such as one author noting he rented a room from the Petitioner and some authors 
referring to the Petitioner as a "doctor" but the evidence in the record does not demonstrate the 
Petitioner obtained a doctorate. The Director further stated that the letters of support alone are 
generally not sufficient. On appeal, the Petitioner does not address the evidentiary deficiencies noted 
by the Director and does not overcome the Director's conclusions on this issue. 
Regarding 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), the Director listed all submitted evidence and determined that 
the record does not establish the articles are scholarly in nature and whether the articles were published 
in professional or trade publications or other major media. The articles in question should be written 
for learned persons in that field including all persons having profound knowledge of a field. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l),https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Again, the Petitioner did 
not present any arguments or evidence on appeal to overcome the Director's concern on this issue. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." The Director indicated that the Petitioner did not 
establish that the work displayed constituted his own work product presented as the subject of an

artistic exhibition or showcase. On appeal, the Petitioner did not present arguments or evidence to 
overcome the Director's concerns. 
Regarding 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), the Petitioner stated in his response to the NOID that he 
consistently held leadership and critical roles in his prior employments with several companies. The 
Director indicated in the decision that the Petitioner has not submitted sufficient evidence to establish 
that the organizations or establishments in which they claim to have performed in a leading or critical 
role possess a distinguished reputation. The Director further noted that the Petitioner did not show 
that he played a critical role where he has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the 
outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division of department of the 
organization or establishment. As stated, on appeal the Petitioner requests a reconsideration of all 
evidence but does not provide any arguments or evidence to overcome the Director's concerns on this 
issue. 
On appeal, the Petitioner has not identified a specific error related to these criteria but rather requests 
that the Director reconsider all the submitted evidence. Moreover, he has not addressed the evidentiary 
deficiencies the Director identified. It is the Petitioner's burden to submit evidence that sufficiently 
corroborates its claims. Statements made without supporting documentation are of limited probative 
value and are insufficient to satisfy the Petitioner's burden of proof Matter ofSojfici, 22 I&N Dec. 
158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). As such, he has not overcome the Director's determination under these 
criteria. 
As previously noted, the Petitioner also asserts that he meets the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), 
which requires evidence of his receipt of lesser nationally or internally recognized prizes or awards 
for excellence in the field of endeavor. However, because the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he 
meets three of the four evidentiary criteria claimed in the petition, he would not establish that he met 
the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). As such, we need not determine whether he meets the 
requirements of this criterion, nor do we need to provide the type of final merits determination 
referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Accordingly, we will reserve these issues. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The Petitioner has not shown that he met either a one-time award, or three of ten initial criteria. The 
Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of 
their respective fields. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level 
do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 
954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is 
indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise 
demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that he 
is one of the small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
