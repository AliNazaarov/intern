Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a postdoctoral bioelectronics researcher, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l l 53(b )( I )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as 
an individual of extraordinary ability either as a recipient of a one-time achievement that is a major, 
internationally recognized award, or as someone who initially satisfied at least three of the I 0 
regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We dismissed a subsequent appeal. The 
matter is now before us on motion to reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
I. LAW 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit.

Although the four opinion letters express their interest in the Petitioner's contributions, their claims of 
his contributions' significance are not supported by documentary evidence of the contributions' 
significance, such as evidence that his published research has provoked widespread commentary on 
its importance from others working in the field of bioelectronics, and documentation that his research 
has been highly cited relative to others' work in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The record contains information regarding articles 
the Petitioner authored or co-authored between 2016 and 2024, inclusive, with a combined total of 
1,575 citations, or between only one and 490 citations per article. He also submitted Clarivate 
Analytics information regarding citation rates for the category of engineering in general for 
publications dated between 2013 and 2023, inclusive. However, the category ofengineering in general 
is overly broad, including specializations in which there may be typically few or no citations per 
article, which may affect the average citations in the category of engineering in general. Neither the 
Clarivate Analytics information nor the remainder of the record reflects the citation rates of articles 
published within the field of bioelectronics. Thus, the documentary evidence in the record does not 
establish that the Petitioner's articles' citations are high relative to others' work in the field of 
bioelectronics, nor does it corroborate the letter authors' opinions regarding how widespread 
commentary regarding the Petitioner's work may be. Moreover, even if the citations to articles the 
Petitioner authored or co-authored may be high relative to others, the record does not establish the 
proportion of favorable and negative citations that may indicate whether commentary regarding his 
articles support or discredit them. 
The Petitioner's general references to Matter ofSkirball Cultural Center and Matter of Caron Int 'l, 
Inc., are unpersuasive. As a matter of discretion, we may use opinion statements submitted by a 
petitioner as advisory. Matter o_fCaron Int'l, Inc., 19 I&N Dec. at 795. We are ultimately responsible 
for making the final determination regarding an individual's eligibility for the benefit sought; the 
submission of expert opinion letters is not presumptive evidence of eligibility. Id.; see also Fed. R. 
Evid. 702 (providing that an expert opinion may "help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or 
to determine a fact in issue," not that it is evidence of fact itself). We explained in our prior decision 
that the letters ofrecommendation are not corroborated by supporting evidence of fact. Therefore, the 
Petitioner does not establish how we may have misapplied Skirball or Caron Int 'l.

Next, the record does not support the Petitioner's assertion that we applied a heightened standard. 
Rather, the Petitioner mischaracterizes our opinion by paraphrasing language that we did not use. The 
Petitioner describes our decision as "intimat[ing] it holds the position that [his] original contributions 
cannot be of major significance just because methods, techniques, and/or technologies he has 
developed have advanced emerging rather than established technologies." On the contrary, our 
decision explains, "The authors' assertions in the above-referenced letters do not explain how the 
Petitioner's research findings have been widely implemented in the field or establish that the 
Petitioner's work has had a demonstrable impact on the field as a whole commensurate with a 
contribution ofmajor significance." Thus, the concerns are not whether the technologies are emerging 
or established but, rather, whether the Petitioner's contributions have been implemented or merely 
posited, or whether the contributions have affected the field of bioelectronics as a whole or merely 
localized to limited individuals or entities. The Petitioner also claims that we "denied [his] original 
contributions are of major significance because the evidence does not show that [he] has made the 
most significant contribution in his field or made contributions that are significant beyond his field." 
On the contrary, as exemplified above, our decision notes that the record does not establish whether 
the Petitioner's contributions have had a demonstrable impact on the field ofbioelectronics as a whole, 
not whether they have been the most significant contribution in his field, or whether they have been 
significant beyond his field, as the Petitioner asserts. 
The Petitioner also specifically objects to our decision "dismiss[ing] the value of the Clarivate 
Analytics ESI data provided that provides precisely ... the value of citation data at the article level," 
noting that the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) "only requires a [petitioner] to demonstrate the 
major significance of their own contributions in the field, not that of others." The criterion requires 
"[ e ]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). The Petitioner submitted 
Clarivate Analytics information noted above to use the number of citations articles he authored or co­
authored received in comparison to the average number of citations other articles received. Thus, the 
Petitioner introduced the issue of the significance of his articles' citation rates as compared to those of 
others. In order to conduct a meaningful comparison ofthe Petitioner's articles' citations, the question 
becomes whether he purports to compare his bioelectronics research articles' citations to other 
bioelectronics research articles' citations, or to all discrete fields within the category of engineering in 
general. 
The Clarivate Analytics information in the record directly undermines its own probative value, with 
the disclaimer that "Citation frequency is highly skewed with many infrequently cited papers and 
relatively few highly cited papers. Consequently, citation rates should not be interpreted as 
representing the central tendency of the distribution." The Clarivate Analytics information in the 
record also explains that its data "are calculated from citation counts of papers that meet the criteria 
for inclusion," not based on all papers actually published within a given category, and that "the margin 
of difference is impossible to determine." Thus, although articles the Petitioner authored or co­
authored may have been cited more frequently than the particular group of articles Clarivate Analytics 
selected for its data, including environmental engineering articles, health and safety engineering 
articles, materials engineering articles, petroleum engineering articles, and other articles that may skew 
the central tendency of citations among articles in the category of engineering in general, such 
information bears limited probative value for determining the significance of the Petitioner's 
4 
contributions in the field ofbioelectronics engineering. Accordingly, the Petitioner does not establish 
how we may have erred by considering the information he submitted. 
Next, the record does not support the Petitioner's claim that we did not evaluate evidence "in the full 
context of the totality of the evidence provided." On the contrary, we specifically explained: 
Considered together, the evidence consisting of the citations to the Petitioner's 
published findings, the citation statistics, the information regarding I Iand the 
reference letters from his fellow bioelectronic researchers and other experts, establishes 
that the Petitioner has been productive, and that his published data and findings have 
been relied upon by others in their own research. It does not demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has made a contribution of major significance in the field ofbioelectronics. 
Thus, we not only examined information in the record both individually and within the context of the 
totality of the evidence, we also specifically stated that we considered the various information in the 
totality of the record together, in its full context. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 369 
( directing USCIS to "examine each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, 
both individually and within the context of the totality of the evidence"). 
Because the Petitioner does not establish that we misapplied a law or policy to the record at the time 
of our prior decision, the motion to reconsider will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. §§ 103.5(a)(3)-(4). 
ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
