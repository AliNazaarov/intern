Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a business manager and entrepreneur, requests classification under the employment­
based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b )(l)(A). Successful 
petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or 
international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS found that the Petitioner met the 
category's evidentiary requirements but, in a final merits determination, concluded that he did not 
demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in the business field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends 
that SCOPS overlooked and undervalued evidence. 1 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he did not meet the requisite minimum number of evidentiary requirements. 
We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
1 On his Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion, the Petitioner indicated that he would submit a written brief, additional 
evidence, or both within 30 days of the appeal's filing. As of this decision's date, we have not received any additional

accompanied the Form I-290B. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).2 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

l. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards for Excellence 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines if a petitioner - as opposed to their 
employer, for example - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), 
Criterion 1. If so, the Agency then determines whether the awards are for excellence in the applicable 
field and nationally or internationally recognized. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of six awards. Copies of certificates for four of them - a 2000

2. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence that [they] ha[ve] performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is ( or 
was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division or department. Id. In contrast, a critical 
role means that a petitioner "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome 
ofthe organization or establishment's activities or those ofa division or department ofthe organization 
or establishment." Id. 
Next, USCIS determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. In this context, the word "distinguished" means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant factors include not only an 
organization's relative size or longevity but also other information such as the scale of its customer 
base or relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. 
The Petitioner claimed that evidence of his former roles as a portfolio manager, bank director, and 
senior analyst met this criterion. He submitted letters from his former employers confirming his 
experience. The letter from a Canadian bank demonstrated his performance in a leading role, stating 
that he hired, trained, and supervised a 20-member team at the bank's office inl I But the 
letters from his other two former employers do not explain how he performed in a leading or critical 
role. See Noroozi v. Napolitano, 905 F. Supp. 2d 535, 544 (S.D.N.Y. 2012) (finding that "letters, 
while descriptive of a highly selective process to achieve membership on the Iranian table tennis team, 
simply do not describe, in any detail, how Noroozi performed in a 'leading or critical role"'); see 
4 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8 (requiring "detailed and probative 
information that specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization, establishment, 
division, or department was leading or critical"). The Petitioner listed accomplishments at his former 
employers. But the record lacks supporting evidence that these achievements demonstrate his 
performance in a leading or critical role. 
The record establishes the Petitioner's leading role in the Canadian and U.S. companies that he 
founded. But the record lacks evidence that either these companies or the Canadian bank he worked 
for inl lhave distinguished reputations. The Petitioner described his former employers as 
"renowned national and international companies" and stated that the companies he founded "play a 
vital role in the healthcare field, providing quality services and contributing to the workforce in 
hospitals and care centers across Canada and the United States." But the record lacks supporting 
evidence that the companies have distinguished reputations. The Petitioner submitted documentation 
about the Canadian company he founded, including copies of its incorporation documents and social 
media screenshots. But these materials do not demonstrate the firm's purported distinguished 
reputation. See Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *8 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) 
("Successful businesses can be expected to have satisfied customers and the positive esteem of peers. 
But it takes more to have a 'distinguished reputation."') 
The Petitioner has not submitted documentation of his performance in leading or critical roles for 
organizations with distinguished reputations. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
3. Commandment of a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted an April 2024 statement from accountants indicating that, in fiscal year 2024, 
he earned $147,000 (US). 3 He also provided online information from a search engine stating that the 
average CEO salary in Canada is $183,118 (US), with average additional cash compensation of 
$47,624 (US). 
The evidence shows the Petitioner's receipt of less than the average CEO salary in Canada. He 
therefore did not submit documentation that he received a high salary or significantly high 
remuneration relative to others in his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not met any of the requested category's ten evidentiary requirements. He therefore 
does not qualify as an alien with extraordinary ability. Even ifhis evidence met the requisite minimum 
number of criteria, he would not merit a favorable final merits determination.

To establish final eligibility, the Petitioner must demonstrate that he has sustained national or 
international acclaim and that his achievements have been recognized in his field, establishing him 
among the small percentage who has risen to the field's very top. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). We must consider any potentially relevant evidence in the totality, even if it does 
not fit a regulatory evidentiary requirement or was not submitted as comparable evidence. Id. 
The Petitioner's burden of proof includes explaining evidence's significance and how it demonstrates 
his sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval 
or denial stems from the evidence's type and quality. Id. 
SCOPS found the Petitioner to be "knowledgeable and competent" in the business field. But SCOPS 
concluded that the record lacks sufficient evidence ofacclaim and recognition establishing him among 
the small percentage at his field's very top. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS undervalued: thel IAward issued to 
the Canadian company he founded; his original contributions in the healthcare sector; his roles in 
prominent organizations; and his position on a Canadian bank's board of directors. 
SCOPS described the 2000 I IAward as "noteworthy" but ultimately insufficient to 
show acclaim and achievement meriting the petition's approval. As previously discussed, the 
certificate does not indicate its issuance to the Petitioner, and the record does not explain how the 
award demonstrates his acclaim or his achievements' recognition in the field. Thus, the record 
supports SCOPS's finding that the certificate does not establish the Petitioner's extraordinary ability 
in the field. 
SCOPS recognized the Petitioner's service in "several high-level management roles" but ultimately 
found insufficient evidence "showing how [he] excelled at said roles and how [he] separated himself 
from others in the field holding similar roles." SCOPS stated: "One can be a successful entrepreneur 
as well as hold significant roles with reputable establishments and still not have national or 
international acclaim or be at the very top of their field of endeavor." 
The record supports SCOPS's finding. Although the Petitioner served in high-level management roles, 
the record lacks evidence that he received acclaim or recognition in these roles sufficient to reflect 
extraordinary ability in the field. Letters from his former employers state his titles, duties, employment 
dates, and, in some cases, his salaries. But the letters do not indicate his outstanding performance in 
these roles. The Petitioner states purported accomplishments during his tenures with these former 
employers. But the record does not sufficiently support the claimed achievements. See section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring "extensive documentation" of a petitioner's acclaim and their 
achievements' recognition). 
SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's membership on the Canadian bank's board of directors, 
describing his ascent to the position as "an accomplishment." But SCOPS found the directorship 
evidence insufficient to meet the extraordinary ability standard. 
We agree. As proof of his claimed bank directorship, the Petitioner submitted a copy of a 2010 
membership certificate from a Canadian limited liability company (LLC). The certificate indicates 
6 
that he owns 99% of the LLC. But the certificate does not identify him as a member of the LLC's 
board of directors, assuming it has one. Also, the Petitioner provided online information about the 
bank's purported Indian parent company. The information, however, identifies the bank as the 
parent's "wholly owned subsidiary." If the Petitioner owns 99% of the bank, it would not be the 
parent's wholly owned subsidiary. Thus, the record does not establish the bank's relationship to its 
purported parent or the Petitioner's claimed position on its board of directors. See Marllantas, Inc. v. 
Rodriguez, 806 F. App'x 864, 869 (11th Cir. 2020) (quoting Matter of Ho, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591 
(BIA 1988)) ("USCIS properly placed the burden on 'the petitioner to resolve any inconsistencies in 
the record by independent objective evidence"'.). 
The Petitioner submitted five recommendation letters on his behalf. But these letters also fall short of 
demonstrating acclaim and recognition in the field. Two of the letters are from pastors. While these 
documents reflect positively on the Petitioner's activities and character, they have little value in 
establishing his claimed extraordinary ability in business. 
Two of the remaining three recommendation letters - one from a bank vice president and the other 
from the CEO of a consulting company - contain identical language. Both letters state that the 
Petitioner's "resume aligned with his extensive professional practical experience, with an important 
track record of success that I have witnessed, are a solid indication that he will serve the national 
interest of the United States now and in the future." The documents' identical language suggests that 
their signatories did not individually write them, casting doubt on their authenticity and credibility. 
See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 n.3 (D.D.C. June 
8, 2021), aff'd, No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (finding that identical 
language in letters suggests that they "were all prepared by the same person and calls into question the 
persuasive value ofthe letters' content"). Also, the letters' conclusions that the Petitioner would serve 
the United States' national interest do not demonstrate his extraordinary ability in business. 
The Petitioner also submitted an expert opinion letter, which concluded that he has extraordinary 
ability in the field of "Entrepreneurship and Business Management in the healthcare services sector in 
Canada." But the letter - from a U.S. professor of management, marketing, and entrepreneurship -
bases its conclusion, in part, on the prospective economic impact of the Petitioner's U.S. business 
ventures. To establish extraordinary ability, a petitioner must already have received acclaim and 
recognition of their achievements. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act (stating that a qualifying 
alien has extraordinary ability "which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation") 
(emphasis added); see also 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l) (requiring a petitioner to demonstrate eligibility "at 
the time of filing the benefit request"). Thus, the expert letter - even when combined with other 
evidence ofrecord- does not demonstrate the Petitioner's claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
In sum, the record contains insufficient evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and 
recognition of achievements sufficient to establish the Petitioner among the small percentage at his 
field's very top.
