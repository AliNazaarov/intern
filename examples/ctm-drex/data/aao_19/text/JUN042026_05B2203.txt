Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an industrial engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate 
their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner had not established he had a major, 
internationally recognized award, or that he met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria to establish 
the initial evidentiary requirements for the classification. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits detennination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. 
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCJS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCJS 
Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step 
extraordinary ability analysis).

C. Criteria Withdrawn 
USCIS determined the Petitioner demonstrated 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) and (viii), which requires 
evidence he received lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards, and held a 
leading or critical role for a distinguished organization or establishment. We disagree and for the 
reasons discussed below, we withdraw USCIS' determination with respect to these two criteria. 
1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or A wards 
In order for the Petitioner to meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), he must establish that he 
has received lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field 
of endeavor. The Petitioner submitted a 2013 "'Public Recognition' Diploma for Patriotism and 
Dedication" and a 2000 award for "Courage in an Emergency," both issued by ministers of the 
Ukrainian government. 3 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated the awards are nationally or internationally recognized. The 
Petitioner provided laws evidencing that the awards were issued by the Ukrainian government. 
However, a national government agency's issuance of an award does not alone establish a prize's 
national recognition. See generally Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) 
("[A]s reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not the awarding 
organization.") (emphasis in original). The Petitioner must show how others beyond the Ukrainian 
government ministry viewed his award. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 136 ("Without 
evidence of how a larger audience viewed Plaintiff's awards, there was no way for the agency to 
evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion.") With respect 
to the 2013 "Public Recognition" award, the Petitioner submitted three 2012 articles from an online 
publication titled Agrarian Week Ukraine in support of its national recognition. However, the articles 
do not speak specifically to the Petitioner's award but rather discusses the "Public Recognition" 
category of awards. The articles also do not provide information on whether the awards are widely 
recognized, and the Petitioner did not explain how, or provide evidence supporting, coverage by the 
publication Agrarian Week Ukraine amounts to national or international recognition. 
With respect to the national recognition of the "Courage in an Emergency" award, the Petitioner 
referred to an article from 2025, titled "State Awards of Ukraine for Civil Servants," and claims the

D. Remaining Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Published Material in a Professional or Major Trade Publication or Medium 
In order to meet 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), the Petitioner must establish that material has been 
published about him and his work in the field for which classification is sought, and that the medium 
in which the material was published was a professional or major trade publication or other major 
media. In addition, 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) plainly states that the evidence must include the title, 
date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation. USCIS determined the Petitioner did 
not submit articles that included the date and author of the material and that the printouts from 
Similarweb.com, providing information on the publications, were illegible. On appeal, the Petitioner 
argues USCIS overlooked that the submitted articles did include a date and author, and as a result he 
has met the requirements of the criterion. 
The Petitioner submitted an article from VINnews.com that is datedl I2025 and authored by 
"Anonymous." At the end of the article, an author is listed. However, the Petitioner does not explain 
why the author would be listed as anonymous in one location and then later identified at the end of the 
article. The Petitioner also submits a different article, published a few days after the prior article, in 
midhudsonnews.com. The author is not listed at the beginning of the article but the author's name 
5 
appears at the end of the article. 4 We acknowledge that a majority of the content of each article 
describes the Petitioner's education and work history, and quotes his thoughts on sustainability 
measures, but the record is not clear why different articles would be published within a few days of 
each other by different publishers quoting the Petitioner. For example, the articles do not refer to 
current events and it is not clear what the purpose ofthe articles are, other than to discuss the Petitioner. 
As a result, the articles appear to be more aligned with marketing or promotional pieces, rather than 
published material about the person. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) 
(providing, as guidance, that marketing materials, including those with seemingly objective content 
about the person in major print publications that are created for the purpose of selling the person's 
products or promoting the person's services, are not generally considered to be published material 
about the person). 
Further, the Petitioner claimed that these online publishers are professional or major trade publications. 
In evaluating whether a submitted publication is a professional publication, major trade publication, 
or major media, relevant factors include the intended audience. Id. Here, for example, the Petitioner 
submitted readership information showing that the industry distribution for VINnews.com is 23% 
community and society, faith and belief; and 44% other; and midhudsonnews.com is 40% news and 
media and 3 7% other. The Petitioner has therefore not established that the publications are intended 
for professionals or individuals of a certain trade. 
In addition, the Petitioner also has not demonstrated that either publisher is major media. Relevant 
factors to establish that the published material qualifies as other major media include the relative 
circulation, readership, or viewership. Id. The Petitioner submitted information from Similarweb.com 
for VINnews.com from October 2025 and for midhudsonnews.com from May 2025, even though the 
articles were published inl I2025. The information from Similarweb.com evidences that 
VINnews.com had total visits at 3.3 million, a global rank of 24,131, a country rank of 5,295, and an 
industry rank of 118. Formidhudsonnews.com, the total visits were 491,019, a global rank of 119,317, 
a country rank of 23,241, and an industry rank of 7,168. However, because the Petitioner did not 
provide context for these figures, their significance has not been established such that we may conclude 
that either qualify as major media. For these reasons, the Petitioner has not established the criterion. 
2. High Salary in Relation to Others in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner demonstrate that he commanded a 
high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field. A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Comparative wage or remuneration data 
that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location should be submitted to establish that their 
compensation is high relative to others working in the field. Id.

3. Reserved Criterion and Issue 
USCIS also determined the Petitioner did not meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), which 
requires evidence that he made original contributions of major significance in the field. As the 
Petitioner would be unable to establish that he meets at least three of the ten threshold "initial 
evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve our analysis of this remaining criteria. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required 
to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F .3d at 1119-20 ( discussing a two-step review, 
where if the required number of criteria are met by the documentation, then it is considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 
2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa"). Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby 
reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
