Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a neuroscientist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Petitioner filed the petition in December 2023. USCIS denied the petition in September 2024. 
The Petitioner filed an appeal, which USCIS treated as a motion to reopen under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .3(a)(2)(iii). USCIS denied the petition for a second time in February 2026, concluding that 
although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidentiary requirements, it did 
not establish, as required, that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is an 
alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. The matter is now before us on appeal under 
8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that she received a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied three of these criteria, pertaining to participation as a judge of 
the work of others; original contributions of major significance; and authorship of scholarly articles. 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner had satisfied the three claimed criteria, but concluded that she 
had not demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, sustained national or international acclaim 
and that she is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor, whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. 
A final merits determination involves analyzing a petitioner's accomplishments and weighing the 
totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (stating that USCIS officers should then evaluate the 
evidence together when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has 
established, by a preponderance of the evidence, the required high level of expertise for the immigrant 
classification). In this matter, we agree with the determination that the Petitioner has not established 
eligibility.

The USCIS Policy Manual does not require a discussion of prior O- lA approval, but strongly 
recommends that "officers issuing denials in such cases should provide a brief discussion as to why, 
notwithstanding the previous 0-1 nonimmigrant visa petition approval, the petitioner has failed to meet 
its burden to establish that the beneficiary is eligible for classification as an immigrant with extraordinary 
ability." See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(3). Each petition is separate and 
independent and must be adjudicated on its own merits, under the corresponding statutory and regulatory 
provisions. Id. In this case, USCIS explained in several different ways how the record does not support 
a finding of eligibility. The lack of discussion of the Petitioner's prior O- lA status does not, by itself: 
wholly undermine the denial decision. 
On appeal, the Petitioner cites a district court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. 
Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), in which the court objected to USCIS' reliance on Kazarian to justify its use of 
the final merits determination as the basis for denying a petition. The Petitioner observes that her 
petition was adjudicated in Nebraska, under the ruling court's jurisdiction. 
We disagree with the Petitioner's reliance on this non-precedent decision. In contrast to the 
precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are not bound to follow a 
decision of a United States district court, even in cases arising within the same district. See Matter of 
K-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). 
Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, unpublished 
opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att '.Y Gen. United States, 147 
F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non-precedential 
opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 n.5 (5th Cir. 
2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 ( considering the reasoning of district court decisions). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). 
The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
3 
The final merits determination hinges on whether the Petitioner has established sustained national or 
international acclaim and has reached the small percentage at the very top of the field. This 
determination is related to, but separate from, the antecedent question of whether the Petitioner has 
satisfied at least three of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Therefore, we review the record with an eye toward the broader issue of sustained acclaim rather than 
the narrower focus of those initial criteria. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that USCIS did not give sufficient weight to the citations of the 
Petitioner's work, articles discussing that work, and letters attesting to the significance of her research. 
But in the denial notice, USCIS discussed the Petitioner's citations and selected letters in the record. 
USCIS acknowledged that the Petitioner's scholarly articles "have been cited 600 times overall." The 
Petitioner also submitted letters from various individuals in her field, describing her work and explaining 
its significance. USCIS concluded that the evidence relating to the Petitioner's research satisfies the 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) and (v), relating, respectively, to authorship of scholarly articles and 
original contributions ofmajor significance. But USCIS concluded that the Petitioner had not shown that 
she has "maintained ... a record of sustained national or international acclaim," and "did not provide 
evidence showing how [her] articles advanced the field and set [her] apart [from] others in the field." 
USCIS noted that others in the Petitioner's field "have garnered citations numbered well in the 
thousands." 
The Petitioner asserts that researchers with lower citation rates have been found to qualify as aliens of 
extraordinary ability. But citation rates alone cannot establish eligibility. 
The Petitioner argues on appeal that, given the acknowledged significance of her highly-cited 
publications, USCIS contradicted itself by concluding that the Petitioner had not established the necessary 
acclaim and recognition. But there is no inherent contradiction. Contributions of major significance 
relate to one of several evidentiary criteria; they do not suffice, by themselves, to establish eligibility as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. Likewise, the Petitioner has emphasized the reputations of some of the 
institutions where she has trained and worked, but she must establish her own sustained acclaim. 
Letters in the record describe the Petitioner's work in technical detail and explain how the Petitioner has 
contributed to knowledge in her field. But the letters do not establish that the Petitioner's work has 
attracted sustained attention throughout her field, or that it has proven to be influential at a level 
commensurate with sustained national or international acclaim. 
Letters of support should be corroborated by documentary evidence in the record. See, generally, 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3). It is generally expected that one whose accomplishments 
have garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have received recognition for their 
accomplishments well beyond the circle oftheir personal and professional acquaintances. Id. The writers 
who provided letters on the Petitioner's behalf hold her in high regard, but the record lacks sufficient 
evidence to show that this opinion represents a more widely-held consensus in the field. 
Materials submitted as showing media coverage ofthe Petitioner's research appear to be, or to be derived 
from, press releases from the research institutions themselves. The Petitioner did not show that this initial

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), ajf'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023)

Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in 
Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree of recognition of her work that rises to the level of 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
