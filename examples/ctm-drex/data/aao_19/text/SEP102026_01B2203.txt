Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a data analyst, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in the 
sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish either that she received a 
major, internationally recognized award, or that the record satisfied at least three of the 10 initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b )(I) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). Ifa criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily 
apply to a petitioner, he or she may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner 
described the proposed endeavor as a plan "to pursue advanced education and 
professional opportunities that align with my long-term goals in research and academia," ultimately 
"obtain[ing] a faculty appointment at a U.S. university, where I will teach and mentor students in data 
science with a special focus on healthcare analytics." 
As addressed above, only aliens who seek to continue to work in the area of extraordinary ability may 
be eligible for first preference immigrant classification as individuals of extraordinary ability. Section 
203(b)(l)(A)(ii) ofthe Act. Continuing to work in one's area ofextraordinary ability, as contemplated 
by Section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) of the Act, means "working in the same profession in which one has 
extraordinary ability, not necessarily any profession in that field." Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. Ill. 2002). For example, an individual who has "extraordinary ability as a baseball player 
does not imply that [the individual] also has extraordinary ability in all positions or professions in the 
baseball industry such as a manager, umpire or coach." Id. 
We note, as a general matter, that the Petitioner does not claim to have extraordinary ability as a 
teacher or mentor of students. Therefore, although the Petitioner's stated goal of pursuing further 
education and ultimately becoming a teacher and mentor of students in data science with a special 
focus on healthcare analytics is in a field related to her work thus far as a data analyst, her proposed 
endeavor does not appear to continue working in the same profession in which she claims to have 
extraordinary ability, as contemplated by Section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) of the act. See id.; see also Matter 
of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376 (noting that petitioners bear the burden of proving their claims are 
more likely than not true under a preponderance of evidence). Relatedly, the Petitioner's aspiration to 
obtain a faculty appointment at some uncertain point in the future after "pursu[ing] advanced 
education" does not indicate that she already "ha[ s] risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

2. Membership in Associations in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
USCIS acknowledged that the record contains information about the Hackathon Raptors and the 
International Association of Software Developers SmartSng.Dev (IASDSSD). The Petitioner is a 
member of both associations. However, USCIS determined the record does not establish that either 
association requires outstanding achievements of its members and that nationally or internationally 
recognized experts in their disciplines or fields judge prospective members. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that both Hackathon Raptors and IASDSSD membership 
requirements are synonymous with requiring outstanding achievements of their members, and that 
membership applications are subject to "the expert-review process." 
The record establishes that the Petitioner became a Hackathon Raptors fellow in 2025. The 
record contains a copy of the Hackathon Raptors charter and information regarding criteria for 
fellowship and the selection process. In relevant part, fellowship candidates "must demonstrate a track 
record of significant achievements upheld at an exceptional standard for at least five continuous years, 
typically within the past decade." The Hackathon Raptors information in the record further supports 
the conclusion that, although other membership categories do not satisfy this criterion, the fellowship 
membership level requires outstanding achievements as contemplated by the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). However, although the fellowship selection process entails a peer review conducted 
by "at least five existing fellow members," the record does not establish that the peer-review panel of 
fellows who select prospective fellows are recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or fields, as opposed to having been recognized merely by the preexisting fellows within 
the organization who selected them. Rather, the fellowship information states that the elector pool is 
derived from fellows who: 
• Have at least 5 years of significant performance; 
• Represent extraordinary achievements and expertise in technology and innovation; 
• Represent diverse areas of expertise within technology and innovation; 
• Have demonstrated continued excellence in their field since becoming a fellow; and 
• Are willing to serve as electors and can declare no conflicts ofinterest with current candidates. 
Thus, the record does not establish that Hackathon Raptors fellowship is judged by nationally or 
internationally-as opposed to merely intra-organizationally- recognized experts in their disciplines 
or fields.

3. Published Material About the Alien 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Relevant factors include the intended audience (for 
professional and major trade publications) and the relative circulation, readership, or viewership (for 
major trade publications and other major media). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). 
The extent of USCIS' analysis of this criterion was naming two articles the Petitioner submitted, 
followed by the conclusory sentence, "As such, the submitted evidence meets this criterion," without 
elaboration. We withdraw USCIS' determination that the record satisfies this criterion for the 
following reasons. 
The record contains copies of three articles about the Petitioner, relating to her work in the field for 
which classification is sought, allegedly published on the internet by The AIJournal, TechBullion, and 
The Tech. Although The AIJournal and The Tech articles include titles, dates, and authors, as required 
by the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), the TechBullion article omits the date on which it

cannot-and it does not-establish eligibility, and we need not address it further. 1

4. Authorship of Scholarly Articles in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." In academic 
fields, a scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, 
generally peer-reviewed by other experts in the field of specialization. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For other fields, a scholarly article should be written for learned persons 
in the field, meaning persons with profound knowledge of a field. See generally id. Examples of 
scholarly article authorship include, but are not limited to, publications in professionally relevant, 
peer-reviewed journals and published conference presentations at nationally or internationally 
recognized conferences. See generally id. 
users acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted copies of articles she wrote, which were published 
in ACADEMIC and American Journal of Engineering and Technology (AJET). However, USeIS 
noted that the record does not establish that the publications qualify as major trade publications or 
other major media. Therefore, users determined that the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that ACADEMIC and AJET "are professional, peer-reviewed, field­
specific publications that regularly disseminate scholarly research in engineering, technology, and 
applied sciences." She also states that users misapplied criteria for major trade publications or other 
major media to ACADEMIC and AJET and, unlike major trade publications or other major media, a 
record need not establish a professional publication's circulation statistics. 
The Petitioner correctly states that the plain language of this criterion provides that scholarly articles 
contemplated by this criterion need not be published in major trade publications or other major media, 
provided they are instead published in professional publications. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
However, the record does not establish that ACADEMIC or AJET qualify as professional, peer­
reviewed journals. 
The Petitioner supplemented a copy of her ACADEMIC article with a congratulatory letter, which 
describes ACADEMIC as an "international scientific journal." However, neither the letter nor the 
remainder of the record corroborate her claim that her article-or any article in general-published in 
ACADEMIC underwent a peer-review process before publication, whether by other experts in the field 
of specialization or otherwise. In tum, the Petitioner supplemented her AJET article with a copy of an 
apparent portion ofAJET's website, which describes itself as an "open access, peer reviewed journal." 
However, other than merely characterizing itself as a "peer reviewed journal," neither the excerpt from 
AJETthat the Petitioner submitted nor the remainder ofthe record substantiate that statement, in order 
to establish whether-and how-submissions to this open-access website in general-and the

Petitioner's article more specifically-may have actually undergone the type of peer review by other 
experts in the field of specialization contemplated by this criterion. Therefore, the record does not 
establish that the articles the Petitioner authored were published in professional publications. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). In tum, the Petitioner does not assert, and the 
record does not support the conclusion, that the articles the Petitioner authored were published in major

B. Final Merits Determination 
Because the record does not satisfy at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
we need not conduct a final merits determination of whether the record shows sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrates that the Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of the 
Act; 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2)-(3)). Nevertheless, we note that, for the following reasons, the record 
does not establish the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that 
she is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See id. 
Black's Law Dictionary defines "sustain" as "to support or maintain, especially over a long period of 
time" or "to persist in making (an effort) over a long period oftime." Sustain, Black's Law Dictionary 
(11th ed. 2019). 
Generally, evidence in this case pertaining to the respective evidentiary criteria is limited to few 
instances, which occurred shortly before the Petitioner filed the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for 
Alien Workers, in July 2025; the evidence does not show that she maintained any level of acclaim 
over a long period of time. More specifically: 
• The only prize or award the Petitioner references is dated 2025 for a 2024 submission; 
• The Petitioner's Hackathon Raptors fellowship began in 2025, her honorary 
membership in the IASDSSD began in 2025 just days before she filed the Form 1-140, 
and her general membership began shortly before; 
• The Tech and The AIJournal articles addressed above are dated! 2025 and 2025, 
respectively, and the Petitioner claims the undated TechBullion article was published in 
2025; 
• The articles the Petitioner reviewed in the context ofthe criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) 
are dated 2024-25; 
• License agreements the Petitioner purports establish she has made original contributions of 
major significance in the field are dated 2024-25 and a presentation related to these license 
agreements is undated; 
• ACADEMIC andAJETpublished the Petitioner's articles in 2025; and 
• The Petitioner asserts she performed roles for seven different organizations or establishments 
within the period of 2021-25. 
Given that much of the evidence in the record is dated only months and even just days before the 
Petitioner filed the Form 1-140 in 2025, the Petitioner has not established she may have sustained any 
particular degree of acclaim over a meaningful period of time.

III. CONCLUSION 
The evidence does not meet at least three ofthe 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Moreover, 
even ifthe evidence satisfied at least three ofthe 10 initial evidentiary criteria, the totality ofthe record 
does not support a conclusion that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required 
for the classification sought. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. USCIS has long held that even 
athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" 
standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). The Petitioner has not 
shown that the significance of her work is indicative of the required sustained national or international 
acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by 
Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sep. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The 
record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or international 
acclaim in the field and that she is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the 
field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; see also 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
