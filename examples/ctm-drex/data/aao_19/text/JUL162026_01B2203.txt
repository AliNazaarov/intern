Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an electrical engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

In response to a request for evidence, the Petitioner submitted a ScholarGPS Ranking Analytics table,

In terms of citations, the ScholarGPS table ranks the Petitioner at 33,563, ranking in the top 9.44%. 
Citation figures are one factor among many to consider. By themselves, citation counts generally do not 
suffice to establish the major significance of the Petitioner's original contributions. We must also 
consider evidence and information addressing those specific contributions. The USCIS Policy Manual 
provides instructive language: 
Published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from 
others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to 
others' work in that field, may establish the significance ofthe contribution .... Detailed 
letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the alien's 
contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original 
contributions ofmajor significance, particularly when the record includes documentation 
corroborating the claimed significance. 
See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). By referring to "widespread commentary 
... and documentation that [the work] has been highly cited," it is evident that the citations themselves 
do not necessarily amount to widespread commentary on the significance of the work. See Gadhave v. 
Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) ("Any citation to 
an academic work could equally critique it or laud it."). The Petitioner has submitted partial copies of a 
few ofthe citing articles, without establishing that these citations are a representative sampling ofthe rest.

The record contains letters from some of the researchers who have cited the Petitioner's work. Detailed

petitioner must objectively demonstrate that the field considers the work to be of major significance).

The Petitioner has produced useful research as a student and later as a postdoctoral researcher. But he 
seeks a highly restrictive immigrant classification, and the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires 
original contributions of major significance, which is a much higher threshold than utility, novelty, or 
relevance. Evidence that other researchers have found his work useful is not sufficient to establish the 
major significance that the regulation demands. The Petitioner's research concerns electric power 
generation, which is a practical, real-world activity rather than an abstract concept confined to the realm 
of theory. Therefore, we would expect contributions of major significance to be reflected in real-world 
power generation. The record lacks independent, objective evidence to show the extent of 
implementation of his work and the impact of that implementation on the field as a whole. 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the requirements of this criterion. 
In light of the above conclusions, the Petitioner does not meet the initial evidentiary requirement of 
three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability. We need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
