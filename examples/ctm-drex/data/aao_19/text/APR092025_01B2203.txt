Appeal of Texas Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a physical therapist, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 C.F.R. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for 
U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition. The Director concluded that the 
Petitioner met one of ten initial evidentiary requirements - two less than needed for a final merits 
determination. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he also submitted evidence of his: 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment ofa high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in his field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), (viii), (ix). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he did not satisfy the evidentiary criteria regarding original contributions and 
performance in a leading or critical role. Because the Petitioner cannot meet the requisite minimum 
number of evidentiary requirements, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, USCIS must make a final merits determination. To merit approval, the record- as a whole 
- must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized 
achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner, a Brazilian native and citizen, earned a bachelor's degree in 
physical therapy and post-graduate diplomas in cardio-respiratory and manual/postural physiotherapy 
from schools in his home country. He has more than 20 years' experience in Brazil's healthcare sector, 
including work in administrative and management roles. He established and managed two healthcare 
companies in Brazil. Now in the United States, he states his intention to continue working as a physical 
therapist in this country. 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He must therefore meet at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports the Director's finding that the Petitioner submitted evidence ofpublished material 
about him and his work. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). The Director found that the Petitioner did 
not provide evidence of his memberships in associations in the field requiring outstanding 
achievements of their members or display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (vii). But, because the Petitioner does not challenge those two findings on 
appeal, he has effectively "waived" the issues. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 
2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 T&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012)). 
We will now review the Director's findings regarding the three evidentiary requirements that the 
Petitioner claims to have met. To satisfy these criteria, his evidence must objectively meet the 
parameters of the applicable regulatory descriptions. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B). 
"[N]either USCIS nor [the] AAO may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010).

B. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has made original 
contributions in their field. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. If so, the 
Agency then determines whether the original contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he developed a downloadable program for evaluating patients 
in home care. He claims that the tool "streamlines" patient assessments and increases clinics' 
efficiencies. He also cited to a newspaper article about the program and provided six recommendation 
letters on his behalf. 
The Director found that the Petitioner's program constitutes an original contribution to his field. But 
the Director found insufficient evidence that the tool has major significance in the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that his program has been "widely adopted" in his field. He states 
that one of his companies is integrating the tool into its information technology system and that 
physiotherapists across Brazil can obtain the tool. He claims that his program has been downloaded 
from four continents and that he holds its exclusive intellectual property rights. 
Evidence that a petitioner developed a technology attracting attention or commercialization could 
establish the significance of their original contribution to their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 5. Also,"[ d]etailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature 
and significance of the person's contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the 
claimed original contributions of major significance, particularly when the record includes 
documentation corroborating the claimed significance." Id. 
The Petitioner's evidence, however, does not establish that his program has major significance in his 
field. See Strategati, LLCv. Sessions, No.: 3:18-cv-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. 
May 31, 2019) ( citations omitted) ( stating that "major significance" means "substantial influence beyond 
one's employers, clients, or customers.") The three-paragraph, 2014 newspaper article describes the 
program, mentions the Petitioner, and states that the tool may save practitioners up to 30% in costs. 
But the article does not state the source of its information. As the Petitioner himself could have 
provided the article's information, we do not find it to constitute independent, unbiased, or reliable 
evidence. Also, the article casts doubt on the Petitioner's claimed sole development of the program. 
The article states: "A multi-disciplinary team formulated this groundbreaking program within 2 years 
of study." ( emphasis added). 
The Petitioner also submitted a data page regarding the program's number of "views." The page, 
entitled "All Time Statistics," appears to be a "screen shot" of a program part. The page indicates: 
269 views in Brazil; 33 views in the United States; two views in Ireland; and one view each in Angola 
and Cape Verde. The page does not define the term "view." We therefore do not know whether a 
view indicates the program's use. The data therefore do not demonstrate widespread use of the 
Petitioner's program. Even if the page's "views" reflect the number of clinics around the world using 
3 
the Petitioner's program, the record would not demonstrate that it has major significance. Further, the 
recommendation letters discuss the Petitioner's accomplishments in his field. But none of the listed 
accomplishments include his development of the program. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that his original contribution has major significance in his field. 
We will therefore affirm the Director's finding. 
C. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[ e ]vidence that [they] ha[ve] performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is (or 
was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division, or department. Id. In contrast, a critical 
role means that a petitioner "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome 
ofthe organization or establishment's activities or those ofa division or department ofthe organization 
or establishment." Id. 
USCIS next determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. The word "distinguished" means "marked by eminence, 
distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, 
www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant factors include not only the relative size or longevity of an 
organization but also other information such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. 
The Petitioner submitted the six recommendation letters, a letter from the company he founded where 
he served as a partner and physical therapist since 2009, and a copy of a 2014 newspaper article about 
him and the company. 
The Director found insufficient evidence of both the Petitioner's performance in a leading or critical 
role and the distinguished reputation of an organization. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that evidence of his work with the company met this criterion. He 
points to the letter from a company human resources representative (who is also his spouse), stating 
that, under the Petitioner's leadership, the firm expanded its physiotherapy services from one to 14 
cities in Brazil and augmented its services to include speech therapy, occupational therapy, nutrition, 
and psychology. 
"A title, with appropriate matching duties, can help to establish that a role is ( or was), in fact, leading." 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. The Petitioner founded and owns the company, and 
its letter lists his duties as including: financial management; strategic planning; and development of 
4 
new products and services. Thus, contrary to the Director, we find that the Petitioner has submitted 
evidence of his performance in a leadership role at the firm. 
But, like the Director, we find insufficient evidence that the company has a distinguished reputation. 
We acknowledge the company's growth over the years. But, founded in 2009, the firm is relatively 
young, and the record lacks media coverage of the company since 2014. The record therefore does 
not establish that the firm has a distinguished reputation. See Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 
WL 4678939, at *8 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) ("Successful businesses can be expected to have satisfied 
customers and the positive esteem of peers. But it takes more to have a 'distinguished reputation."') 
The Petitioner's evidence demonstrated his performance in a leading role but not that his organization 
has a distinguished reputation. We will therefore affirm the Director's finding on this criterion. 
D. The Remaining Evidentiary Requirement 
The Petitioner has met one evidentiary criterion but claims satisfaction of only one more. Thus, he 
cannot meet the requisite minimum of three criteria. We therefore need not reach and hereby reserve 
consideration ofwhether he submitted evidence that he commanded a high salary or significantly high 
remuneration relative to others in his field. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to 
their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not satisfy two of three evidentiary requirements he claims to have met. He 
therefore cannot meet the minimum number required for a final merits determination. We will 
therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
