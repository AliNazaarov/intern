Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in the arts. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish either that he received a 
major, internationally recognized award, or that he satisfied at least three of the 10 initial evidentiary 
criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010).

While we acknowledge the possibility of a petitioner's extraordinary claim in more than one field, a 
petitioner must demonstrate "by clear evidence that the alien is coming to the United States to continue 
work in the area of expertise." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). In this case, based on the record before us, 
the Petitioner intends to continue to work in the area of street dance education and cultural 
development rather than competing as a street dancer. Thus, the Petitioner cannot satisfy the 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) through his achievements as a street dancer.

3. Leading or Critical Role 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
On appeal, the Petitioner objects to SCOPS' omission of a discussion of this criterion and, instead, its 
discussion ofother criteria under which he did not claim eligibility. He reasserts that he "is the founder 
and CEO" and that his organization has been "recognized with numerous prestigious industry awards 
and accolades[, which] demonstrates both the distinguished reputation ofthe organization ... and [his] 
leading and critical role," thus satisfying the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
First, we acknowledge that SCOPS did not address the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). But, 
because SCOPS determined that the record only satisfied one of the five claimed criteria, even if it 
had addressed the criterion and concluded that the Petitioner satisfied it, he nevertheless would not 
have satisfied at least three ofthe 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(ix), which was the basis for 
denying the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers. Accordingly, this claimed error was, 
at most, harmless. See generally Matter of O-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing 
cases regarding harmless or scrivener's errors). 3

and oversees as its chief executive officer based in China. An employment confirmation letter 
states that he is the company's founder and chief executive officer, and it summarizes his duties;

To the extent that the employment confirmation letter written by an unidentified individual may 
establish that the Petitioner performs a leading or critical role at that company, the question becomes 
whether that organization or establishment has a distinguished reputation, as required by the criterion 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). Factors for determining whether an organization or establishment has 
a distinguished reputation include, but are not limited to, the relative size, longevity, scale of customer 
base, relevant media coverage, and fonding of an organization or establishment. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The record contains limited information regarding the Petitioner's company's compos1t10n and 
activities. Various documents in the record, including the Petitioner's curriculum vitae and the 
employment verification letter, claim that the Petitioner founded the company in 2016; however, the 
record does not corroborate these assertions with documentation of when the company was founded, 
and by whom. Therefore, the record does not establish the company's actual longevity. The 
employment verification letter generally describes the company as "dedicated to promoting street 
dance culture through comprehensive education and training programs, captivating performances, and 
high-profile commercial events. It provides a creative platform for street dance enthusiasts to grow 
and excel." However, neither the letter nor the remainder of the record elaborates on any other 
employee(s) of the company and their job duties. Moreover, the record does not establish the sizes of 
similar companies, whether i~ IChina, or elsewhere. Therefore, even if the record established 
the company's size, it would not-and it does not-establish the relative size of the company as 
compared to similar companies. Even if the record addressed the number and duties of any other 
individuals who work for the company, neither the letter nor the remainder of the record clarify how 
the company is fonded, and what the extent of its fonding has been in any particular year. Similarly, 
beyond generally describing the company's activities as promoting culture, conducting performances, 
and providing education or training, neither the letter nor the remainder of the record elaborate on the 
scale of the company's customer base or audience, such as the sizes of its performances' audiences or 
the number of students or trainees at any given time and throughout its alleged 10-year existence. 
We acknowledge that the record contains information purportedly regarding awards given to the 
company and its students or trainees. However, the record does not establish whether-and when­
the individuals were students or trainees of the company, nor does the record establish the number of 
other competitors for the awards in question and the criteria for receiving them. Therefore, even if the 
record established that the company or its students or trainees received the awards depicted, the record 
does not establish the significance of having done so, in order to assess how that may inform whether 
the company has a distinguished reputation.

Based on the foregoing, the record does not establish that the Petitioner performed a leading or critical 
role for any particular organization or establishment, or that such an organization or establishment has 
a distinguished reputation; therefore, the record does not satisfy the criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
Based on the foregoing, the record would not satisfy at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) even if it satisfied the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii); therefore, we 
reserve our opinion regarding that criterion. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision); Matter of L-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to reach 
alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
