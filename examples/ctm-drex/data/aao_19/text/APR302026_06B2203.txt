Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a management analyst, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate 
their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner was not the 
recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, and, did not satisfy at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria to meet the initial evidentiary requirements of the classification. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, In c., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204. 5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204. 5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation 
is frrst counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCJS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally recognized 
award under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). He must therefore satisfy at least three of the regulatory criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Based on our de novo review, the Petitioner has not met this showing. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria Waived 
The Petitioner did not assert in the record below or on appeal that he meets the criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (vi), (vii), or (x) regarding receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards, authorship of articles, display of his work, and commercial successes in 
the perfonning arts. We therefore deem the Petitioner's eligibility under these four criteria waived. 
See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 I&N Dec. 
657, 658 n.2 (BIA 2012))(An eligibility ground that is not raised on appeal is waived.) 
B. Evidentiary Criterion Established 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner met the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), which requires 
evidence of his participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the 
same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought. We agree. 
C. Evidentiary Criterion Withdrawn 
SCOPS also found the Petitioner established the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), which 
requires evidence that he performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments 
that have a distinguished reputation. The Petitioner submitted supporting letters and contracts 
demonstrating that he served as an advisor for three agricultural technology (AgTech) companies, two 
of which the evidence identifies as startups. However, the Petitioner did not provide sufficient 
evidence of the distinguished reputation of these companies. Distinguished is defined as "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." See Distinguished, 
Merriam-Webster.com, www.merriam-webster.com. For a startup business, we may consider 
evidence that the business has received significant funding from government entities, venture capital 
2 
funds, angel investors, or other such funders commensurate with funding rounds generally achieved 
for that startup's stage and industry, as a positive factor regarding its distinguished reputation. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). Other relevant factors for evaluating the 
reputation of an organization or establishment can include the scale of its customer base or relevant 
media coverage. Id. While the Petitioner provided online articles discussing the two start-up 
companies, their work, and that they secured funding, neither the articles nor the other evidence in the 
record provide context for the funding amounts achieved by the companies for us to understand 
whether their funding is significant commensurate with other startups within their stage and within the 
AgTech industry. In addition, the Petitioner did not provide information on the scale of any of the 
companies' customer base and how their base compares to other similar AgTech companies. The 
Petitioner also submitted a letter indicating he served as the vice president of a board that represents a 
coalition of companies from various industries. Other than the letter describing the Petitioner's 
membership in the coalition, the record did not contain any information about the distinguished 
reputation of the coalition. We therefore disagree with SCOPS that the Petitioner provided sufficient 
evidence to demonstrate the distinguished nature of the establishments to meet the requirements of 
this criterion and withdraw SCOPS' determination to the contrary. 
D. Remaining Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Criteria Not Established 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not establish the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and (ix) 
and we agree. 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires the Petitioner establish his membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought. In addition, the Petitioner must establish 
that the association requires outstanding achievements of its members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii); see generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The Petitioner established that he is a member of the 
Brazilian Institute of Corporate Governance (IBGC) and its "GT Agro" division. However, as SCOPS 
explained both in its Request for Evidence (RFE) and in its decision, the documents, including the 
IBGC's bylaws and policies, did not establish that outstanding achievements were required of its 
members or that members were judged by national or international experts in their fields. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues SCOPS' RFE did not provide notice to the Petitioner of the 
deficiencies in his evidence with respect to this criterion. As discussed above, SCOPS provided proper 
notice in its RFE and explained in its decision that the Petitioner did not sufficiently explain how 
meeting the requirements for membership amounts to outstanding achievements. 
The Petitioner also argues that SCOPS did not thoroughly review the evidence, specifically identifying 
several articles in IBGC's bylaws, and claims that IBGC membership candidates go through 
evaluation such as background investigations and ethical screenings and are thereby assessed by 
governance specialists. But the documents and this explanation do not support that the IBGC, or any 
division within, requires outstanding achievements as judged by recognized national or international 
expe1is in the field. The Petitioner also highlights his position in other organizations but does not 
connect how being, for example: a member of a board of directors, a senior executive, an expert in 
3 
corporate governance matters, a partner for a business, where he adhered to good ethics and corporate 
government practices, meet the requirements of being a member in an association requiring 
outstanding achievements of its members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in 
their disciplines or fields. The Petitioner has therefore not demonstrated this criterion. 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner establish that he commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field. A credible 
employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary or 
remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Comparative wage or remuneration data that is 
appropriate for the alien's occupation and location should be submitted to establish that their 
compensation is high relative to others working in the field. Id. According to the Petitioner's initial 
filing, he does not receive a conventional fixed salary as "his earnings derive from advisory 
agreements, variable project-based remuneration, investment returns, and profit distributions." In 
support of his remuneration, the Petitioner submitted a table from an accountant listing his "annual 
value" and "monthly average" income from 2014 to 2023. The Petitioner also submitted salary data 
for business management consulting analysts as comparative wage data. In response to SCOPS' RFE, 
the Petitioner again stated that his "earnings derived not from a fixed wage but from profit participation 
and consulting remuneration in startup governance and investment roles, consistent with high-level 
positions in venture capital, agribusiness consulting, and advisory boards." In its decision, SCOPS 
explained that because the record did not specifically identify what portion of his income qualified as 
a salary, which was the type of comparative data he provided to establish that he meets this criterion, 
there was not a proper basis to compare his salary to other salaries. 
On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that his salary is not a conventional wage but remuneration for 
services rendered as a "startup evaluator, governance specialist, and advisor." However, he has not 
provided data regarding remuneration for similarly situated individuals with a similar compensation 
structure, to demonstrate his compensation is high relative to others in the field. Rather, the Petitioner 
provided only salary data for business management consulting analysts I as comparative data, after 
repeatedly indicating that his source of income is not salary related, but based on profit participation 
and consulting remuneration. As a result, he has not demonstrated his data is comparative and that he 
meets this criterion. 
2. Issues Reserved 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not establish he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), 
requiring evidence of published material about the Petitioner in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media, relating to the Petitioner's work in the field for which classification 
is sought; and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), requiring evidence of the Petitioner's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field. As the 
Petitioner would still be unable to establish that he meets at least three of the ten threshold "initial 
evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude he is an alien of extraordinary 
ability and reserve our analysis of the remaining two criteria as well as a final merits detennination in

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
