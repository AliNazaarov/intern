Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a synthetic organic chemist, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied 
at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria, as required, he did not show his sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrate he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner met at least three of the claimed evidentiary criteria. However, the 
SCOPS concluded the Petitioner did not show he garnered sustained national or international acclaim 
and his achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating he is one of that 
small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field. On appeal, we will review the totality of 
the evidence in the context of the final merits determination below. 1

In addition, the Petitioner offered evidence of his appointments to the editorial boards of Science 
Journal of Chemistry (SJC) and ARKIVOC. Regarding SJC, the Petitioner provided screenshots 
showing editorial board members require a minimum of 4 years ofresearch or professional experience 
and a minimum of 5 publications on the same topic, with priority given to associate professors or

did not show that such requirements signify his standing among that small percentage at the very top 
of the field or how his position as an editorial board member, for instance, compares to a chief editor

screenshots of a partial list of other editorial board members, which appear to show that ARKIVOC

Furthermore, the Petitioner pointed to letters from B-D-U- and K-C-K- and claimed that these "expert 
statements confirm [his] significant peer-review contributions." However, the letters make general 
statements, such as "[h ]is participation in the importance [sic] task of peer review demonstrates that 
the staff of these journals have come to respect [the Petitioner's] scientific acumen and in-depth 
knowledge" and "[the Petitioner's] frequent invitations to peer review demonstrate the recognition of 
his outstanding reputation and the high caliber of his research." The letters do not show that the 
Petitioner has risen to the upper echelon of his field or that he garnered national or international 
acclaim based on his judging reputation. See Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, 
at *23 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding that plaintiff did not show how she judged to such an 
extent, or at a high enough level, that her judging surpasses the majority of the field and places her in 
the top echelon of the field). 
Here, the Petitioner did not show, for instance, that he garnered wide attention from the field based on 
his journal paper reviews. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner apart from others in his field, such 
as evidence that he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging recognized, acclaimed experts in 
his field or that he serves in an editorial position for a highly ranked or prestigious journal, the 
Petitioner has not shown that his judging experience places him among that small percentage who has 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, 
at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the petitioner did not offer evidence of a consistent history 
of completing a substantial number of review requests relative to others, serving in editorial positions 
for distinguished journals or publications, or chairing technical committees for reputable conferences).

As authoring scholarly articles is often inherent to the work of scientists and researchers in academia, 
the citation history or other evidence of the influence of an individual's articles can be an indicator to 
determine the recognition they have received in the field and whether such recognition has been 
sustained. Such an analysis at the final merits determination stage is appropriate pursuant to Kazarian, 
596 F. 3d at 1122. Here, the Petitioner has consistently emphasized that the individual and collective 
citations to his work, according to several metrics and statistics, show that he has received the required 
recognition for his research achievements.

Finally, the record reflects that the Petitioner provided around a dozen recommendation and reference 
letters. However, the letters are not sufficiently persuasive. The letters generally highlight the 
Petitioner's work and indicate the authors' personal implementation of the Petitioner's work into their 
own work. Letters that make general and expansive statements regarding the beneficiary and the 
beneficiary's accomplishments are generally not persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(8)(3). The letters do not claim, nor does the content show, that the Petitioner achieved 
national or international acclaim from the field. Moreover, the letters do not demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has attracted a level of attention placing him the very top ofhis field. In addition, the letters 
do not show that he has enjoyed a career of acclaimed work. See Jafarov, 2024 WL 69056, at *9 
(finding testimonial letters were insufficient to establish his relative status in the field and do not 
compare him to others). 
Here, the letters do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that the Petitioner is 
viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that he has 
garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 
2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the plaintiff's letters point to

the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for 
the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage 
at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
