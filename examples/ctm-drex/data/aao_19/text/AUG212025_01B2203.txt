Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a geologist, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish the Petitioner met the 
requirements for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. The matter is now before us 
on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 20 l 0) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijalv. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner indicates that she is currently working as an "operations earth scientist, pore pressure 
and fracture gradient geologist" with the .______________, in the United States. 
Because the Petitioner has not asserted or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the evidence submitted with the initial petition and in 
response to a request for evidence (RFE), did not establish eligibility for the benefit sought. In denying 
the petition, SCOPS determined the Petitioner satisfied two criteria, judging and authorship of 
scholarly articles at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vi), respectively, of the six claimed evidentiary 
criteria. The Petitioner asserts on appeal that she additionally meets the criteria for membership, 
original contributions, leading or critical role, and salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (v), (viii), and 
(ix), respectively, and that the record establishes her eligibility for classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. She contends that SCOPS erred in the analysis of the evidence, applied an 
incorrect evidentiary standard, and misinterpreted the law. Upon review, we determine the Petitioner 
has not shown her eligibility for the additional claimed criteria and also note the record reflects SCOPS 
properly analyzed the submitted documentation and applied the correct evidentiary standard. 
A. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

D. Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 6 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility for this criterion based on the 2024 compensation she received from 
I Iwhile working in Nigeria for the first half of 2024 and in the United States from July to 
December 2024. She referred to her December 2024 earnings statement; IRS Form W-2, Wage and 
Tax Statement (Form W-2), which shows she earned $366,392 for "wages, tips, other compensation"; 
her Gross Remuneration and Payment of Tax letter, which indicates she received 239,087 Nigerian 
Nairas; and salary and labor data for geologists and geoscientists in the United States. 
In denying the petition, SCOPS found that the record did not contain a detailed, accurate breakdown 
of the Petitioner's salary and other remuneration for her services. SCOPS noted that the earnings 
statement indicated she received compensation for "Inpat Housing/Utility," "Goods & Services 
Supplement," and "Inpat Vac Exp Allowance," which appear to be additional incentives rather than 
part of her salary. SCOPS indicated that the Petitioner did not address these categories of payment in 
her RFE response. Further, while the regulations allow for evidence of other significantly high 
remuneration for services in relation to others in the field, the evidence did not demonstrate the 
additional incentives are high compared to other geoscientists. On appeal, the Petitioner argues that

ITT. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa"). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
