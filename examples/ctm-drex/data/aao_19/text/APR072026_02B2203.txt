Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a financial and travel-related services company, seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish either that the Beneficiary 
received a major, internationally recognized award, or that the record satisfied at least three of the 10 
initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). If a criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily apply to 
a petitioner, they may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner described the endeavor as a plan to continue employing the Petitioner as a top executive. 
SCOPS concluded that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and (ix); however, 
SCOPS found that the record does not satisfy the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), and (viii), 
noting that the Petitioner did not assert eligibility under the remaining criteria. On appeal, the 
Petitioner reasserts that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii)-(iii), (v), (viii)­
(ix), and that the record contains evidence comparable to the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi),

B. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 
The extent of SCOPS' analysis of this criterion was acknowledging that the Petitioner submitted 
"[e]vidence of salary" and "[s]alary data," summarizing the criterion's requirements, and concluding 
that "the submitted evidence meets this criterion." 
We withdraw SCOPS' conclusion that the record satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) 
for the reasons discussed below. 
The record contains information regarding the Beneficiary's annual salary and other remuneration 
since a 2023 start date, including an employment contract, a new-hire annual compensation 
opportunity summary, an executive compensation term sheet, a year-end performance and 
compensation statement, and paystubs. The Petitioner also submitted wage information provided by 
the U.S. Department of Labor's Office of Foreign Labor Certification (OFLC) and from 
3 
Careeronestop.com, which is sponsored by the U.S. Department of Labor. The OFLC information 
regards chief executives' wage levels among "all industries" between July 2024 and June 2025, 
inclusive, although the Careeronestop.com information does not clarify the date range for its 
information. Both the OFLC and Careeronestop.com information report chief executives' wages 
among all entity sizes and for all levels of executives' employment experience in the area of "New 
York-Newark-Jersey City, NY-NJ-PA." 
The breadth of the wage information regarding the general chief executive occupational category 
provides minimal informative value for assessing whether the Beneficiary commands a high salary or 
other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the same field as the 
Beneficiary, with comparable job duties, employment experience, and other factors-such as 
employers' annual revenue and payroll budgets, and localized cost-of-living adjustments-that may 
explain wage differences separate from ability, whether extraordinary or otherwise. The OFLC 
information also omits estimates for remuneration other than wages; therefore, it provides limited 
informative value for assessing the Beneficiary's total compensation, which includes remuneration 
other than wages. Moreover, we note that the Careeronestop.com wage data provides limited 
informative value as a general matter. It states that both median and high wages for chief executives 
are "$239,200+." That the Careeronestop.com median and high wages are identical, specific, and 
indefinite indicates that Careeronestop.com's wage information is artificially capped at "$239,200+," 
so the actual median value is some unspecified amount greater than $239,200, and the actual high 
value is some unknown rate greater than the unspecified median rate. This informs that the 
Beneficiary's income is greater than the lowest salaries among others in the same occupational 
category but, without establishing a definite median income value, it does not assist us in 
understanding whether the Beneficiary's income even is equal to that median income value, let alone 
greater than it. 
The record does not otherwise contain evidence that informs what salary or other remuneration others 
in the Beneficiary's field-withjob duties, experience, employer factors, and localized cost of living 
comparable to his-receive. For all of the foregoing reasons, the record does not support SCOPS' 
conclusion that the record satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), and we withdraw its 
statement to the contrary. 
C. Published Material About the Alien 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Factors for determining whether a publication qualifies as 
professional or major trade publications include the intended audience and, for major trade 
publications and other major media, the relative circulation, readership, or viewership. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
SCOPS acknowledged that the record contains certain published articles and information about 
publishers of those articles. However, SCOPS determined that circulation information for the 
publications did not sufficiently establish that they qualify as "professional or major trade publications 
or other major media."

D. Authorship of Scholarly Articles in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." For fields other 
than academia, scholarly articles should be written for learned persons in the field, meaning 
individuals having profound knowledge gained by study. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F .2(B )( 1 ). As noted above, the regulations permit petitioners to submit comparable evidence 
to establish eligibility if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to a 
beneficiary's occupation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
SCOPS did not address whether the record contains evidence comparable to authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications.

On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) does not readily apply 
to the Beneficiary's occupation, that the Beneficiary "has been repeatedly invited to speak at 
prominent industry conferences attended by senior executives, technology leaders, and decision­
makers from across the digital payments and financial services sectors," and that these public-speaking 
events are comparable evidence for this criterion, as contemplated by 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
We acknowledge that the record establishes that the Beneficiary was invited to speak at certain 
industry conferences; however, the record does not establish that the Beneficiary actually attended and 
spoke at the events and, more to the point, the substance of what his speeches were and whether the 
audiences consisted of individuals having profound knowledge gained by study. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Therefore, the record does not establish whether the 
Beneficiary's speeches-if they occurred-were written for and given to individuals in the field 
having profound knowledge gained by study and, thus, comparable to authorship of scholarly articles 
in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Because the record does 
not establish the Beneficiary attended and spoke at the referenced events, we need not determine 
whether the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) does not readily apply to him and whether the 
claimed public speaking is comparable to that criterion. 
Because the record would not satisfy at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
even if the Beneficiary met the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(v) and (ix), we reserve our opinion 
regarding those criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision); 
Matter of L-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to reach alternative issues on 
appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
E. Prior Nonimmigrant Classification 
We acknowledge that the Petitioner objects to SCOPS' omission of an explanation of why, despite it 
having previously granted the Beneficiary 0-1 nonimmigrant classification under section 
10l(a)(l5)(O)(i) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 110l(a)(l5)(O)(i), it determined the Beneficiary does not 
qualify for immigrant classification as an individual of extraordinary ability under section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. However, the prior approval does not preclude U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services from denying an immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a 
different standard - statute, regulations, and case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-
01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 
(JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 
2025 WL 815087, at *l (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). Many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS 
approves prior nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., Sunlift Int'l, Inc. v. Mayorkas, No. 20-CV-08869-
JCS, 2021 WL 3111627, at *11 (N.D. Cal. July 22, 2021); Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. 
Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. US. Dep 't ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 
(D.D.C. 1999), ajf'd sub nom. IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep't ofJust., INS, No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 
(D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 1999); Fedin Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), aff'd, 
905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority over SCOPS, the office adjudicating 
nonimmigrant visa petitions, is comparable to the relationship between a court of appeals and a district 
court. Even if SCOPS has approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf of an individual, we are not 
bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another immigrant petition. See Louisiana

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
