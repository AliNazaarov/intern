Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an advanced data scientist, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability 
in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that, while the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required, she bad not demonstrated her extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and achievements that have been recognized 
in her field through extensive documentation. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 
C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that , 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is an advanced data scientist inl IIndia. At the time of filing, she was a senior 
specialist in data sciences at I Ifocused on machine learning, graph mining, and natural 
language processing. She indicates that she will continue to work in this capacity in the United States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed to have met four criteria relating to 
published material, judging, original contributions of major significance, and scholarly articles under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (iv), (v), and (vi), respectively. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner satisfied three of the claimed initial evidentiary requirements 
relating to published materials, judging, and authorship of scholarly articles, but did not meet the 
criterion relating to original contributions of major significance in the field at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi). On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS erred in concluding that she had not 
met the criterion related to original contributions of major significance in the field at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi). However, regardless of whether SCOPS erred in making this finding, we do not

reach this issue on appeal because the Petitioner has shown that she satisfies the minimum requirement 
of at least three criteria, and we will therefore evaluate the totality of the evidence, including that 
relating to original contributions, in the context of a final merits determination, discussed below. See 
Berardo v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 3:19-CV-01796-SB, 2020 WL 6161459, 
at *3 n.2 (D. Or. Oct. 20, 2020) (declining to address applicant's argument that USCIS erred by failing 
to attribute certain evidence for criteria that USCIS concluded he had met as any error would have 
been harmless). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the 
field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
In denying the underlying petition, SCOPS concluded that the totality of the evidence in the record 
was not sufficient to demonstrate that the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim, that 
she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that her achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. On appeal, the Petitioner first 
argues that SCOPS did not afford her an opportunity to address newly identified deficiencies in these 
expert opinion letters. However, the record shows that SCOPS issued a request for evidence in which 
it advised the Petitioner of these deficiencies and that it considered the Petitioner's response in its 
decision. 
The Petitioner further argues that SCOPS dismissed and discounted evidence relating to the published 
material and scholarly articles criteria, mischaracterized evidence relating to the judging criterion, and 
failed to properly consider expert opinion letters in the record. In support of this assertion, the 
Petitioner relies upon a non-precedent decision in which we found that evidence of sustained acclaim 
had been overlooked by the service center director and sustained the appeal. This decision was not 
published as a precedent and therefore does not bind USCIS officers in future adjudications. See 8 
C.F.R. § 103.3(c). Moreover, the record reflects that SCOPS appropriately considered this evidence 
in reaching its conclusion that the Petitioner had not demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, 
her sustained national or international acclaim, that she is one of the small percentage at the very top 
ofthe field ofendeavor, and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. 
As noted above, in a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and 
weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. We evaluate whether the Petitioner has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim, that 
she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that her achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(A)(l ), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

Here, the record reflects that the Petitioner has experienced some press coverage, authored scholarly 
articles, judged and served as a peer reviewer of the work of others, and has made contributions. 
However, in considering the totality of the evidence, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that her 
achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. 
H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at 
*6739. 
Furthermore, the Petitioner has not shown that she has risen to that small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The commentary for the proposed regulations 
implementing section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high 
standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the 
petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 
56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). 
Regarding media coverage, the Petitioner offered seven articles published in 2019, 2023, and 2024 
about her presentations at various conferences. While the published material indicates some attention 
to her and her work, the Petitioner did not establish that such reporting is consistent with a level of 
success among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, has 
achieved sustained national or international acclaim, 1 or has a career of acclaimed work. The 
Petitioner, for instance, did not show how the amount ofher media coverage compares to others in her 
field who are among the very top. Further, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how her nominal press 
coverage reflects a career of acclaimed work in the field or shows that her achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 
No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. 
United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
(upholding plaintiff did not show his press coverage was indicative of a level of success consistent 
with being at the top of his field); see also Bodhankar v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 
No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the lack of press 
about the plaintiff was not consistent with national or international acclaim). 
As it relates to the Petitioner's service as a judge ofthe work ofothers and her membership on technical 
committees for conferences, an evaluation of the significance of her experience is appropriate to 
determine if such evidence indicates the required extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive 
classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22; see also generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l) (providing that the second step requires USCIS to evaluate the person's judging 
participation to determine whether it was indicative of the person being of that small percentage who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and enjoying sustained national or international 
acclaim.). In her brief: the Petitioner reviews the evidence related to this evidentiary criterion and 
asserts that these materials show her international recognition in the field. This documentation shows 
that she has reviewed the work ofher peers for publication in three journals and has served as a member 
of the technical committee or as a reviewer for ten international conferences in her field. However, 
she does not discuss the relevance or significance of her judging involvement in the context of a final

Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The 
Petitioner presented evidence showing that she has authored a total of 19 peer-reviewed works 
published in research journals, conference proceedings, and as book chapters. However, she did not 
demonstrate that her publication record is consistent with having a career of acclaimed work, 
sustaining national or international acclaim, and being among the small percentage at the very top of 
her field. The Petitioner, for instance, did not show the significance of her authorship or how her 
publication record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See id. 
(upholding the Petitioner did not show that the significance of authorships reflect that he is among the 
small percentage at the very top of the field). 
In addition, the citation history or other evidence of the influence of the Petitioner's written work can 
be an indicator to determine the impact and recognition that her publications have had on the field and 
whether such influence has been sustained. For example, numerous independent citations for an article 
authored by the Petitioner may provide solid evidence that her work has been recognized and that 
others have been influenced by her work. Such an analysis at the final merits determination stage is 
appropriate pursuant to Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1122. In the record below, the Petitioner offered 
evidence, such as a Google Scholar document, showing that 12 ofher research articles have been cited 
by others a total of 145 times,2 and that a 2025 journal article had been cited a total of 34 times, as 
well as information about the journals and conference proceedings that published her articles. The 
Petitioner also provided evidence to demonstrate the frequency with which her articles had been 
downloaded or viewed. However, the Petitioner did not show her research articles received a level of 
interest in the field commensurate with sustained national or international acclaim or a career of 
acclaimed work. Further, the Petitioner did not show the impact or influence that her written materials 
have had on the field or otherwise show the significance or relevance ofher publications. For example, 
while the Petitioner notes that her articles have been cited by researchers worldwide, she has not shown

The Petitioner also offered letters of recommendation from independent experts. While we discuss 
only a sampling here, we have considered each one individually. Some of these letters discuss the 
Petitioner's citation and publication records, such as one from P-K- stating that the Petitioner "has 
established herself as an internationally recognized scholar through her extensive publication record." 
However, these letters do not indicate how the Petitioner's citation or publication record compares 
with others at the very top ofher field. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 
23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding testimonial letters were insufficient to establish 
his relative status in the field and do not compare him to others). 
These recommendation letters also praised the Petitioner's work and contributions. For example, P-K­
praises the Petitioner for her "impressive achievements and pioneering contributions to her field", 
while S-I- describes the Petitioner's "adeptness at harnessing the potential of advanced data science 
techniques to revolutionize decision-making processes." Here, the letters are not sufficiently 
persuasive as they make general and expansive statements regarding the Petitioner and her 
accomplishments. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3). Further, this 
correspondence does not demonstrate that the Petitioner has attracted a level of attention placing her 
at the very top of her field. Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's work, they do not reflect a 
career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international 
acclaim. See Bodhankar, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (finding the submitted letters failed to establish the 
plaintiff's contributions resulted m significant acclaim); Mishra v. Richardson, No. 
120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the 
plaintiff's letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his accomplishments to show 
sustained national or international acclaim). 
For the above stated reasons, the record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not 
establish the Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive 
visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than 
those progressing toward the top. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) 
( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for 
classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th 
Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Onaghise v. 
Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) 
(holding that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and 
are "reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). In this case, the Petitioner has not that the shown 
the significance of her work is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or 
it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification 
sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that she is among the small percentage at the top of 
her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
