Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an independent film director, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition, concluding that the record did not 
establish that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. 
The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
After earning a bachelor's degree in economics and working in finance, the Petitioner began working as 
an aerial photographer in 2008; made his first independent short film in 2018; and made his first feature 
in 2021, released on a streaming platform in 2024. The record does not indicate that the Petitioner has 
made any other films. The Petitioner, a native of China, has resided in Canada since 2022. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner initially claimed to have satisfied three of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards; 
• (ii), Membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; and 
• (v), Original contributions of major significance. 
In response to a request for evidence (RFE), the Petitioner claimed to satisfy a fourth criterion: 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media. 
The Petitioner's response to the RFE also referred to "other comparable evidence." 
The Director concluded that the Petitioner had not met any of the claimed criteria. Upon review of 
the record, and as discussed below, we agree with the Director's conclusions.

3. Published Material 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) calls for published material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought. The evidence must include the title, date, and author of the material, and any 
necessary translation. 
The Petitioner did not initially claim to have satisfied this criterion. In response to the RFE, he submitted 
a 2019 article from Douban, which he called "China's largest and most authoritative film website." 
Submitted materials refer to Douban as an "online database and social networking service."1 
The article, credited to "Douban Shadow Man PRO," describes a writing class by a professor at the 
University ofCalifornia, Los Angeles (UCLA). The article indicates that the professor was impressed by 
the Petitioner's short film. 
The Director concluded that the Douban article mentions the Petitioner but is not about him, and that the 
Petitioner had not established that the article appeared in qualifying media. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the Douban article "fully meets the requirements" because it 
"accurately describes [ the Petitioner] as a director, and [ reports that his] short film has won praise from 
the master screenwriter." 
We agree with the Director that the Douban article is not about the Petitioner as the regulation requires. 
The article is about the screenwriter and the class he taught at UCLA to a visiting group of Douban 
members. It includes a brief mention of the Petitioner in the context of that class, showing that the 
screenwriter provided encouraging feedback about the Petitioner's work. But this six-sentence anecdote 
does not amount to the "substantial discussion of the person's work" necessary to make the article about 
the Petitioner and his work. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner submits additional information about Dou ban on appeal, stating that he did not submit this 
information previously because the Director "didn't [request it] in the RFE." But the Petitioner's initial 
submission did not include any claim of qualifying media coverage. The Director, in the RFE, did not 
request information about, or clarify the evidentiary requirements for, criteria that the Petitioner had not 
initially claimed to have satisfied. 
The record does not indicate that Douban is a publication in its own right, comparable to a professional 
or major trade publication or other major media. Rather, the record describes Douban as a platform with 
millions of members, through which users essentially self-publish their own content, in a manner akin to 
blogs or social media posts. The Petitioner has not established that the channel that carried the post by

4. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field. 
The Petitioner shot much of his feature film under challenging conditions at a high altitude in Tibet. He 
asserted that his "original film is of great significance in the field of film" because "no independent film 
has ever been shot on location at an altitude ofmore than 5,000 meters." The Petitioner stated: 
[T]his film ... will inspire you to pay attention to the people and environment in Tibet's 
high-altitude areas. From an artistic point of view, the film captures a unique natural 
beauty that adds a new form of expression to the language of film. From a commercial 
point of view, the film will appeal to a specific audience group, inspire new ways of 
making films, and drive innovation in the field of film . 
. . . In 2022, all the herders in Shuanghu County have been relocated to lower altitudes, 
and many of the video clips in the film have become the last memories of Shuanghu 
County in Tibet. The film has also become a valuable heritage of the human history of 
Tibet and the world's highest altitude regions. 
The Petitioner submitted photographs of the harsh filming environment and media reports about the 
relocation of the herders, which took place shortly after the Petitioner finished filming. These materials 
provide insight into the background of the film, but the Petitioner did not explain how the filming 
conditions and subject matter gave major significance to his film. 
The Petitioner submitted two letters from individuals in the U.S. film industry. A screenwriter and retired 
professor at UCLA, the subject of the Douban article discussed above, stated that the Petitioner's "short 
film was simply wonderful" and that that his feature "is of extraordinary significance and value, not only 
showing the nature and culture of Tibet, but also exploring the choices and meaning of life, showing the 
collision and integration between different people and cultures." 
A producer, director, and screenwriter stated: "I believe [the Petitioner] is an independent director of 
extraordinary ability. He has the potential to be a great director in the United States and to greatly 
contribute to the field of film. Especially valuable, should he come to the United States, is the unique 
perspective he will bring with him." 
In the RFE, the Director acknowledged the letters the Petitioner had submitted, but concluded that the 
Petitioner had not submitted "documentary evidence demonstrating that the beneficiary's work has had a 
substantial impact in the industry, has significantly influenced the work ofothers in the field, or otherwise 
constitutes original contributions of major significance in the field." 
6 
In response, the Petitioner asserted that his feature film "is a significant original contribution" because 
"no one has ever shot a film in Shuanghu County, Tibet," and because it is the last visual record of 
"herders living above 5,000 meters above sea level" before their government-ordered relocation. 
The Director acknowledged the letters and other evidence, but concluded that the Petitioner had not 
shown that his "work has had a substantial impact in the industry, has significantly influenced the work 
of others in the field, or otherwise constitutes original contributions of major significance in the field." 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that capturing "the last humanistic image record of the life ofherders 
in Tibet [at] the highest altitude of all mankind" amounts to "a significant original contribution." The 
burden is on the Petitioner to establish that his contribution is not only original, which the Director 
acknowledged, but also of major significance to the field of film production. The Petitioner's assertion 
that the significance of his contribution is self-evident by "very simple logic" does not meet this burden. 
The claimed uniqueness of the subject matter attests to originality rather than to significance. 
The Petitioner states that the Director did not give sufficient consideration to the submitted letters. 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions ofmajor 
significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed significance. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1). 
The letters that the Petitioner submitted do not rise to this standard. The letters include sincere praise for 
the Petitioner's skills and work. But they do not explain how the Petitioner's films are not only 
"wonderful," a term used in both letters, but also ofmajor significance in the field of filmmaking. Also, 
the record does not include corroborating documentation showing the broader impact, influence, or 
significance of the Petitioner's films. 
The Petitioner has not met his burden of proof regarding the requirements of this criterion. 
5. Comparable Evidence 
If the standards listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the beneficiary's 
occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). We consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the 
beneficiary's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria 
listed in that regulation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1). A general 
unsupported assertion that a particular evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the beneficiary's 
occupation is not probative. Id. 
The Petitioner's initial submission did not mention or claim comparable evidence. In response to the 
RFE, under the heading "Other Comparable evidence," the Petitioner stated that he is "one of the few 
directors in the world who can shoot in some ofthe harshest environments on earth," and "one ofthe very 
few film directors in the world who has a psychological counseling qualification." The Petitioner also 
asserted: "In the United States, I will be one of very few directors who knows what Marxism and 
communism are." 
7 
In the denial notice, the Director determined that the Petitioner had not met his "burden to explain why 
the regulatory criteria are not readily applicable to his occupation and how the evidence submitted is 
'comparable' to the [criteria] at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x)." 
On appeal, the Petitioner acknowledges that "the evidentiary standards ... generally apply to directors," 
but he asserts that he has "submitted . . . in-depth evidence that [he is] one of the very few unique 
individuals in the field of directing." But the Petitioner also asserts that the "work of each director is 
unique." Explaining how his work is different from that of other directors does not explain how he 
qualifies for the highly restrictive extraordinary ability classification. 
We agree with the Director that the Petitioner has not shown that specific evidentiary criteria do not 
readily apply to the Petitioner's occupation, and has not explained how his assertions are comparable 
to such criteria. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten lesser criteria. As a result, we need not provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for aliens already at the top of 
their respective fields, rather than for aliens progressing toward the top. U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not 
automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree of recognition of his work that 
indicates the required sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of 
acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 
1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate 
that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not established the impact of his one short film and one feature-length film. While 
letters from two industry figures include praise for those films, the record as a whole does not show that 
the Petitioner's work has achieved significant attention from the industry, critics, or the public. Details 
about the nature of, or motivation behind, the Petitioner's work does not show that he has reached the top 
ofthe field of film production. The availability ofthe Petitioner's feature on a streaming platform shows 
that the public has the opportunity to view his film, but the Petitioner has not shown that this availability 
has led to the level of recognition that the statute and regulations require. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
