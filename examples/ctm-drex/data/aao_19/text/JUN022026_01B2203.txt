Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner satisfied the initial 
evidentiary requirements for this classification but did not establish, as required, that he has sustained 
national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of his field. The 
matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376.

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed seven criteria relating to memberships, 
published materials, judging, contributions, scholarly articles, leading or critical role, and salary. 
SCOPS determined he satisfied three of the seven criteria relating to judging, scholarly articles, and 
salary. Therefore, SCOPS proceeded to do a final merits determination. 
Given the structure of the regulations and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, the satisfaction 
of three evidentiary criteria alone does not automatically or presumptively establish eligibility. In a 
final merits determination, we evaluate whether a petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
2 
the evidence, their sustained national or international acclaim, 1 that they are among the small 
percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor, and that their achievements have been recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation. Therefore, we analyze an individual's accomplishments 
and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.2 Here, SCOPS concluded that, although 
the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements, he did not show he garnered sustained national or 
international acclaim and that he is among that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the 
field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends the record "[d]emonstrates sustained national acclaim through the 
convergence of industry adoption, commercial impact, peer selection, market compensation, and 
expert endorsement." He asserts SCOPS made procedural and legal errors in the decision. We 
evaluate the totality of the evidence, based on the documentation presented to SCOPS, in the context 
of the final merits determination. After reviewing the Petitioner's appellate claims and the remaining 
record, we agree with SCOPS that he has not shown sustained national or international acclaim or that 
he is eligible for this immigration benefit. 
As a preliminary matter, the Petitioner has provided inconsistent information regarding his occupation. 
On the Form 1-140, the Petitioner indicated his job title as "Lead Application Developer" and he 
explained that he is a leading expert in the field of software development. However, in response to 
SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE), he stated that he is a senior software engineer and he has 
sustained national and international recognition in the field of software engineering and enterprise 
systems architecture. Moreover, on appeal, the Petitioner states that he is an enterprise software 
architect. The Petitioner must resolve this ambiguity in the record with independent, objective 
evidence pointing to where the truth lies. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988). 
As discussed further below, the Petitioner belongs to associations, authored material, conducted 
review work, made contributions, served in a critical role, and earned a salary for his work. However, 
in considering the totality of the evidence, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements 
to date reflect a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress at H.R. Rep. No. 
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). Nor has he shown that he has risen to that small percentage at the very 
top ofthe field ofendeavor. See 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). The commentary for the proposed regulations 
implementing section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high 
standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the 
petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 
Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). Here the Petitioner has not established that he meets these 
very high standards. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 386-87 (5th cir. 2022) (stating that the narrow 
definition reflects the stringency of the extraordinary ability standard).

III. CONCLUSION 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 5 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary 
ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an 
exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has 
not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or international 
acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of 
the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for 
the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage 
at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons. In visa petition 
proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The 
Petitioner has not met that burden here.
