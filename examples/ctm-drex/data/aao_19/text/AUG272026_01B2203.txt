Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an information technology service delivery manager, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Petitioner filed his Form I-140 petition in August 2023. USCIS denied the petition in March 2025, 
concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either received a major, 
internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three often initial evidentiary criteria, as required. 
The Petitioner filed a motion to reconsider in April 2025, which USCIS dismissed in August 2025. The 
Petitioner appealed that dismissal in September 2025. The matter is now before us on appeal under 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
A. Invalid Signature 
According to USCIS regulation and policy, "[i]fUSCIS accepts a benefit request and determines later 
that the request was not submitted with a valid signature, USCIS may reject or deny the request." See 
8 C.F.R. § 103.2(a)(7)(ii)(A)(2).1 
We notified the Petitioner in a notice of intent to dismiss (NOID) that the Petitioner's signature on Form 
I-290B appeared to be deficient, because it appeared to be a copy of an earlier signature that was 
electronically added to a new form. The NOID further notified the Petitioner that this basis alone was 
enough to dismiss the appeal and the signature could not be cured. In response, the Petitioner asserts that

B. Extraordinary Ability 
Even without the issue of the invalid signature on the September 2025 Form I-290B, the record does not 
indicate that the Petitioner has met his burden of proof to establish eligibility for the highly restrictive 
immigrant classification that he seeks. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the next step is 
a final merits determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part 
review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination). 
In this instance, the Petitioner claimed to directly satisfy three regulatory criteria, and to have submitted 
comparable evidence relating to two other criteria that, he claimed, do not readily apply to his occupation. 
USCIS concluded that the Petitioner had established that he satisfied the criterion relating to leading or 
critical roles for organizations or establishments with distinguished reputations under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii), and had submitted evidence comparable to display at artistic exhibitions or showcases 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). USCIS did not grant the other three claimed criteria, relating to

The record consists of information about the Petitioner's employers; examples ofwork that the Petitioner 
performed for customers; internal work documents such as email messages and a 2023 performance 
evaluation; and letters from employers, customers, and professors who have worked directly with the 
Petitioner in various ways. Collectively, this evidence shows that the Petitioner has worked, as a 
contractor, for some significant customers such asl Ibut the record does not show that 
the Petitioner has earned recognition beyond the schools where he studied, the companies that employed 
him, and the customers that benefited from his work. Whatever his reputation within these groups, the 
Petitioner has not established the national or international acclaim that the statute and regulations demand. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia 
v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131 (D.D.C. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the 
extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland 
Sec., No. l 9-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021 ), aff'd, 2023 WL 115680 I (D.C. 
Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) ( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of 
prospective immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't of Homeland Sec., No. l 9-cv-2534, 2020 WL 
2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial of petition ofa 
published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that 
"[ c ]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. 
Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914,918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball 
players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree ofrecognition ofhis work that rises to the level of sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
