I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to an immigrant with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics 
which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner intends to use his "skills and experience" in "training professionals, developing new 
techniques, and strengthening the smoked barbecue market in the United States." The Petitioner plans 
to partner with interested American companies and investors and also start his own venture in this 
field. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed before SCOPS that he met all ten ofthe regulatory criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and SCOPS determined he did not meet any of the claimed criteria. 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintain he meets seven of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii)-(v), (vii)-(ix), which respectively, relate to membership in associations in the field, 
published material in a professional or major trade publication or other major media, participation as 
a judge of the work of others in the field, original contributions of major significance in the field, 
display of work, performance in leading or critical role for organizations or establishments with a 
distinguished reputation, and commanding a high salary or significantly high remuneration. After 
reviewing all the evidence of record and finding the Petitioner has not met five of the seven claimed 
evidentiary criteria, we conclude he has not satisfied at least three ofthe regulatory criteria as required 
and therefore does not qualify as an alien of extraordinary ability.

The Petitioner contends that even ifthese events are non-artistic, they are still appropriately considered 
for this criterion under the comparable evidence clause, "due to the substantial nature ofthe showcase, 
the public exposure, the artistic relevance ofthe products displayed ... the recognition received," "the 
culinary artistry inherent to the barbecue field and the manner in which [Petitioner's] work was 
displayed and presented." The regulation at 8 C.F.R § 204.5(h)(4) states "[i]f the above standards do 
not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to 
establish the beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision provides petitioners with the 
opportunity to submit comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, if it is determined the 
evidentiary criteria described in the regulations do not readily apply to the person's occupation. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). When evaluating such comparable evidence, officers 
must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if 
not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. Id. 
Here, the Petitioner does not assert or explain how this criterion does not apply to his occupation. 
Rather, the Petitioner focuses on specific events, Meatstock and Beef Tour, in asserting they include 
"artistic relevance," "culinary artistry," and allow for substantial exposure. 
For the reasons discussed above, we concur with SCOPS that the Petitioner has not met this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
7 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -­
F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes 
a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25 (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability.
