Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a life sciences and digital solutions executive, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Service Center Operations (SCOPS) 
denied the petition concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied the 
initial evidentiary requirements, he did not establish in a final merits determination that he has 
sustained national or international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top ofhis 
field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized

The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied five of these criteria, summarized below:

• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media; 
• (iv), Participation as a judge of the work of others;

SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met three of the criteria, pertaining to judging; scholarly articles; 
and leading or critical role. SCOPS discussed the remaining claimed criteria, relating to receipt of 
lesser awards, published material about the alien in professional or major media, original contributions 
of major significance, and high remuneration for services and concluded the Petitioner did not meet 
them. The Petitioner asserts on appeal that he meets the criteria SCOPS concluded he had met as well 
as the remaining criteria SCOPS concluded he failed to meet. Because there is no dispute that the 
Petitioner has met three or more criteria, we need not address the remaining criteria directly. Instead, 
we will consider the Petitioner's role in the broader context of the final merits determination. 
As such, we will evaluate whether he has demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, sustained 
national or international acclaim; that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
2 
endeavor; and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh 
the totality of the evidence to determine if his or her successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he or 
she has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (stating that USCIS officers should then evaluate the 
evidence together when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has 
established, by a preponderance of the evidence, the required high level of expertise for the immigrant 
classification). In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not established eligibility. 
On appeal, the Petitioner cites a district court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. 
Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), in which the court objected to USCIS' reliance on Kazarian to justify its use of 
the final merits determination as the basis for denying a petition. The Petitioner contends that Mukherji 
v. Miller is a "controlling decision" and "governing legal authority" in the present case. 
We disagree with the Petitioner's reliance on this non-precedent district court decision. In contrast to 
the precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are not bound to follow 
a decision of a United States district court, even in cases arising within the same district. See Matter 
of K-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). The Mukherji decision was issued by a district court in 
Nebraska. In the present case, the petition was denied at the Texas Service Center, outside the 
jurisdiction of the Nebraska court that decided Mukherji. 
Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, unpublished 
opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att'y Gen. United States, 
147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non-precedential 
opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 n.5 (5th Cir. 
2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 ( considering the reasoning of district court decisions). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). 
The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
The final merits determination hinges on whether the Petitioner has established sustained national or 
international acclaim and has reached the small percentage at the very top of the field. This 
determination is related to, but separate from, the antecedent question of whether the Petitioner has 
satisfied at least three of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). In a final merits 
3 
determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to 
determine if his or her successes are sufficient to by a preponderance of the evidence demonstrate that 
he or she has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor through sustained national or international 
acclaim, that he or she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that 
his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
Upon our de novo review of the record we conclude that the Petitioner did not demonstrate eligibility 
for the classification because the evidence does not indicate he is one of the small percentage at the 
very top of his field of endeavor. 
The record as a whole does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's achievements are reflective of a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990). The Petitioner claimed an award received by his employer and that he was a member 
of the team that received it. But the record does not indicate that the receipt of the commendation 
reflects sustained acclaim in his field nationally or internationally such that we can ascertain if he has 
risen to the level of the small percentage of individuals at the very top of the field of endeavor. For 
example, it is not sufficiently manifest from the record how the award is recognized in the Petitioner's 
field and if such recognition reflects acclaim reflecting attainment of status amongst those at the top 
of their field. 
As stated above, the Petitioner claims to have served as a judge of the work of others. But an 
evaluation of the significance of his experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence indicates 
the required extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 
1121-22. 1 The Petitioner listed several occasions where he served in a judging capacity and so met 
the plain language of the criterion. However, the Petitioner did not establish that these judging 
opportunities contribute to a finding that he has a career of acclaimed work in the field or indicate his 
sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not show, for example, how his judging 
experience compares to others at the very top of the field. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner 
apart from others in his field, such as evidence that he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging 
recognized, acclaimed experts in his field, the Petitioner has not shown that his judging experience 
places him amongst that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Hamal v. United States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding the 
petitioner did not show that he served as a judge of renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists 
or the competitions were considered prestigious or attracted significant attention by the field). 
Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The record 
reflects the Petitioner presented evidence showing that he has published articles in scholarly 
publications. But the Petitioner did not show the significance ofhis authorships or how his publication 
record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Jafarov v. United 
States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan.

And the submitted documentation does not reflect that the Petitioner has somehow impacted the field 
through his work demonstrating attention at a level commensurate with those at the very top of the 
field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). In addition, the Petitioner's evidence does not 
distinguish his performance in his role to demonstrate that it reflects a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field." H.R. Rep. No. 101-723 at 59. The Petitioner claimed his role at his current and previous 
employers as evidence of his critical role in organizations or establishments with distinguished 
reputations. But the evidence in support consisted mainly of verification of his employment with the

institutions and some background information about the institution he was employed with. He also 
submitted several letters which described his completion of employment objectives in a 
complimentary manner. But the Petitioner did not adequately identify ifthe actual organizational units 
within which his role resided had a reputation of a sufficiently distinguished nature such that his 
service demonstrated a career of acclaimed work or other recognition demonstrating that he was 
amongst that small percentage at the very top of his field. 
Additionally, the Petitioner's salary does not establish that he is amongst that small percentage at the 
very top of his field or describe that his salary recognizes a career of acclaim stemming from his work 
in the field. The Petitioner's assertions and evidence emphasize the factor by which his income 
exceeds benchmark figures he provided. But the evidence does not support that the benchmarks he 
provided apply to his occupation, his level, or to those individuals who have sustained acclaim such 
that they can be considered amongst the very top oftheir field of endeavor. The evidence in the record 
does not set forth any minimum factor by which a Petitioner could establish his sustained acclaim or 
ascension to a level occupied by the small percentage at the top of his field based on comparison of 
his salary to the benchmarks. So, the record does not convincingly establish that the Petitioner's total 
salary is to compensate him in recognition of his stature in his field such that it reflects that he enjoys 
an acclaimed career in his field or consideration amongst those of that small percentage occupying the 
apex of his field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. , No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 
3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial ofpetition ofa published theoretical physicist 
specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even 
highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" 
did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree ofrecognition ofhis work that rises to the level ofsustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(l)(A) 
ofthe Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small 
6 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).2 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
