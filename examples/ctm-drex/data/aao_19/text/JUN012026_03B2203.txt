Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, who works in the field of marketing, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
the initial evidentiary requirements, it did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner bas sustained 
national or international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. 
The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner earned bachelor's and master's degrees in finance in Ukraine and worked in various 
marketing management positions since 2011. She worked in positions of increasing authority for a 
photography software company in Ukraine from 2015 to 2023. The Petitioner has been in the United 
States since September 2023, working inl Ias a contractor, as head of product marketing for 
a company that makes photograph editing software using artificial intelligence (AI). The Petitioner is 
also head of marketing and communications for local hackathon events associated with a company that 
organizes such events worldwide. The Petitioner also co-hosts "a podcast on leadership and innovation." 
She filed the Form 1-140 petition in September 2025. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that she received a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied eight of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards; 
• (ii), Membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media; 
• (iv), Participation as a judge of the work of others; 
• (v), Original contributions of major significance; 
• (vi), Authorship of scholarly articles; 
• (viii), Leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• (ix), High remuneration for services. 
USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met four of the criteria, pertaining to awards, judging, authorship 
of scholarly articles, and leading or critical roles. On appeal, the Petitioner disputes many of USCIS' 
conclusions, asserting that she had satisfied additional criteria. Because USCIS had concluded that the 
Petitioner had satisfied at least the minimum of three criteria and proceeded to a final merits 
determination, we will consider the evidence in the broader context ofwhether it establishes the sustained 
national or international acclaim that section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act requires.

B. Final Merits Determination 
Because the Petitioner submitted the required initial evidence, we will evaluate whether she has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, sustained national or international acclaim; that 
she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor; and that her achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, 
we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual 
( stating that USCIS officers should then evaluate the evidence together when considering the petition 
in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has established, by a preponderance of the evidence, the 
required high level of expertise for the immigrant classification). 
In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not established eligibility. 
On appeal, the Petitioner cites a district court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. 
Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), in which the court objected to USCIS' reliance on Kazarian to justify its use of 
the final merits determination as the basis for denying a petition. We disagree with the Petitioner's 
reliance on this non-precedent decision. In contrast to the precedential authority of the case law of a 
United States circuit court, we are not bound to follow a decision of a United States district court. See 
Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). 
Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, unpublished 
opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att'y Gen. United States, 
147 F. 4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non-precedential 
opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 n.5 (5th Cir. 
2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 (considering the reasoning of district court decisions). 
Importantly, we note that the Mukherji decision was issued by a district court in Nebraska. In the 
present case, the Petitioner is in California and the petition was denied at the Texas Service Center, 
outside the jurisdiction of the Nebraska court that decided Mukherji. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). 
The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id.

The final merits determination hinges on whether the Petitioner has established sustained national or 
international acclaim and has reached the small percentage at the very top of the field. This 
determination is related to, but separate from, the antecedent question of whether the Petitioner has 
satisfied at least three of the initial criteria at 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Therefore, we review the record with an eye toward the broader issue of sustained acclaim rather than 
the narrower focus of those initial criteria. 
The Petitioner had claimed receipt of several awards, fulfilling the regulatory requirements at 8 e.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). users concluded that the Petitioner's evidence regarding most of these awards was 
deficient. users acknowledged that the Petitioner had submitted satisfactory evidence regardingc=J 
I I in 2021 and 2022, but concluded that the Petitioner had not shown that receipt of these 
awards placed the Petitioner at the very top ofher field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner stated that users "ignor[ed] extensive evidence of additional nationally and 
internationally recognized awards ... , spanning more than a decade." The Petitioner also asserts that 
users did not acknowledge or consider further documentation that she submitted in response to a request 
for evidence (RFE). 
Many of the awards went to the Petitioner's employers. The Petitioner has shown that her marketing 
work benefited those employers, and users took that evidence into consideration when it granted that 
she had performed in leading or critical roles for organizations with distinguished reputations. But in 
terms of whether a particular award satisfies the requirements of 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), the focus 
should be on the person's receipt of the awards, as opposed to the employer's receipt of the awards or 
prizes. See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Apart from thel I 
the Petitioner has not shown that the awarding entities publicly acknowledged her individually as an 
award recipient. 
A number of the awards appear to be for factors such as software or product design rather than for 
marketing. It is not readily apparent that awards based primarily on product features rather than marketing 
would significantly affect the Petitioner's reputation in her field. 
The Petitioner submitted letters from officials of various awarding entities, asserting that the entities 
consider the Petitioner to be part of the teams whose work resulted in the awards. These letters were 
written specifically for submission to users. The record does not show that the awarding entities 
publicly acknowledged the Petitioner as one of the award recipients. Without such an announcement 
or other publicity or coverage, it is not evident how a given award contributed to the Petitioner's 
acclaim and recognition among those in the field who did not already know she worked on the award­
winning project. 
Furthermore, while the letters indicate that marketing and product presentation are factors that play a role 
in selecting award winners, public materials such as printouts from the awarding entities' websites do not 
include that information. Letters of support should be corroborated by documentary evidence in the 
record. See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3).

Even if an individual is not a named award recipient, such awards can be relevant in the broader context 
of acclaim, provided the petitioner can show that his or her involvement in the award-winning project 
resulted in widespread recognition. In this case, the Petitioner has submitted printouts of social media 
posts expressing thanks and congratulations, but has not shown that her participation in such projects 
received wide recognition at a level that would indicate sustained national or international acclaim. 
The Petitioner also indicated that she was on teams that won prizes at various hackathon competitions. 
Much of the evidence relating to these events consists of social media posts from teammates, which do 
not show that the prizes or awards, and the Petitioner's involvement, received a level of attention 
consistent with sustained national or international acclaim. 
The regulation at 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) relates to published material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought. Among the evidence the Petitioner submitted under this criterion is a transcript 
of a podcast interview with her. users stated: "The petitioner did not submit evidence that establishes 
that this criterion should be so broadly interpreted to include podcasts as a publication." users also 
concluded that the Petitioner did not submit statistical evidence to show that the "podcast has a widespread 
viewership or traffic rate on a national or international level." 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that podcasts are broadcast media and ought to qualify as published 
material. We agree that published material should not be disregarded out of hand simply because it took 
the form of a podcast. But the burden remains on the Petitioner to show that a particular podcast meets 
the applicable regulatory requirements. We need not explore that specific issue here in more detail, 
because the question at hand is not whether the Petitioner had satisfied at least three initial criteria, but 
rather whether she had shown sustained national or international acclaim. In the context of a final merits 
determination, an important issue appears to be that the podcast focused not on the Petitioner's field of 
marketing, but on photography. The Petitioner worked for a photography software company at the time 
of the interview, and much of the podcast interview revolved around features of that software. 
On appeal, the Petitioner cites a letter from the host ofthe podcast, who asserted that his podcast featured 
the Petitioner "not as a company representative promoting a product, but as a recognized expert in 
marketing within the imaging technology field." The record contains a transcript ofthe podcast, in which 
the word "marketing" appears two times, including when the host stated the Petitioner's title. There is 
some discussion of the Petitioner's promotional work, such as "photography ... camps in Iceland" and 
events "with ... influencers, and ambassadors and afiiliates," but most of the host's questions are about 
the Petitioner's employer and its products. 
Regarding the Petitioner's judging activity, users concluded: "The evidence does not indicate that these 
events only invited judges with demonstrated extraordinary ability in the field. The petitioner did not 
demonstrate, for example, how this judging experience compares to others in the field, or how either the 
quality or quantity of the reviews she conducted stands out from her peers." users also concluded that 
the judging activity took place during only a short fraction of the Petitioner's career. 
The Petitioner, on appeal, asserts that users did not "analyz[ e] the nature, selectivity, or stature of the 
events" at which the Petitioner served as a judge. The Petitioner also contends that "[t]he decision fails

The record includes evidence ofTechstars' global reach as a startup accelerator, but the Petitioner has not

USCIS determined that the Petitioner's "valuable contributions" amounted to leading or critical roles 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). But USCIS concluded: "While the petitioner has been successful 
in her work, the record does not demonstrate that the petitioner's work ... has resulted in the petitioner 
receiving widespread acclaim from the field." 
The Petitioner states, on appeal, that USCIS did not "address[] the distinguished reputation[ s] of the 
organizations involved." But the organizations' distinguished reputations are a core requirement of 
the criterion. By concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied the criterion, therefore, USCIS stipulated 
that the Petitioner had established those reputations. It remains that leading or critical roles for 
distinguished organizations do not always result in recognition outside those organizations, which is 
why the criterion is only one piece of a larger whole. 
The Petitioner submitted information about a podcast that she co-hosts. Screenshots indicate that the 
podcast's channel has "219 subscribers" on YouTube and "31 followers" on Linkedln. YouTube view 
counts for the individual videos, also visible in the screenshots, indicate that one video had been viewed 
5,000 times in a year; the remaining episodes had accumulated between 250 and 2,200 views during that 
period. The Petitioner did not establish that these numbers indicate sustained national or international 
acclaim at the top of her field. The podcast's other co-host wrote an article in a newsletter distributed to 
alumni of a business training program that the Petitioner attended. The newsletter article appears to 
indicate that the guests on the podcast are faculty and other participants in the same training program. 
The Petitioner asserts on appeal that "the final merits discussion also omits any meaningful 
consideration of' her published scholarly articles, letters from independent experts, and her 
"proprietary original.__ _______________, documented and adopted beyond [her] 
employers." Below, we will examine these three issues. 
USCIS stated that, while the Petitioner's authorship of scholarly articles satisfies 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)(vi), "the petitioner did not establish that publishing in these journals is noteworthy or 
reserved only for the best in the field." On appeal, the Petitioner states that USCIS did not sufficiently 
consider her "academic publications in selective scholarly venues." The Petitioner does not elaborate 
or directly dispute USCIS' conclusions. The Petitioner's passing reference to insufficient 
consideration of her scholarly articles identifies no specific error that could be remedied on appeal. 
Cf Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230,234 (D.C. Cir. 1997).

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
7 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that 
EB-1 visas are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of individuals with 
extraordinary ability); see also Hamal v. Dep 't o_fHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, 
at * 1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published 
theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories ofgravitation) (stating that"[c ]ourts have 
found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. 
Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in 
Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree of recognition of her work that rises to the level of 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
