Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a professional soccer player who intends to work as a soccer coach, seeks classification 
as an individual of extraordinary ability. Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant 
visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as 
required. In making this determination, SCOPS observed that the evidence the Petitioner provided in 
support of the petition almost exclusively relates to and documents his career as a competitive athlete, 
not as a coach. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. Aliens requesting this classification must establish that they seek to enter 
the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the 
United States will substantially benefit the country. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition oftheir achievements in the field through a one-time achievement in the form 
of a major, internationally recognized award. Alternatively, a petitioner must submit evidence that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those standards do not readily 
apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows the submission of 
comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner meets the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits determination, 
in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The record shows that the Petitioner played soccer professionally for various teams in Brazil and Saudi 
Arabia between 2008 and 2022. A "personal plan" provided in support of the petition indicates he 
"intends to teach soccer in the U.S. as well as to open his soccer academy" and provide "high-level 
coaching sessions and programs for athletes." In a separate personal statement, the Petitioner indicated 
he was working on obtaining the licenses required to work as a soccer coach in the United States and 
mentioned his plans to open a soccer school for children. Although the Petitioner indicated that he 
was still "active" as a professional athlete as of February 2023, he did not mention any specific plans 
or commitments to continue playing soccer competitively in the United States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner initially claimed to have satisfied six of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards;

In evaluating this criterion, USCIS must determine whether a petitioner has demonstrated that their

B. Final Merits Determination 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets the initial evidence 
requirements for this classification based on the submitted evidence related to his career as a 
professional soccer player. Therefore, we need not make a final merits determination, as described in 
Kazarian, to evaluate whether he has achieved the high threshold of showing sustained national or 
international acclaim that places him among that small percentage of athletes at the very top of the 
field. See section lOl(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Further, separate from the issue of acclaim, the Petitioner must establish that he seeks to enter the 
United States to continue work in the area of claimed extraordinary ability. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii) 
of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). Like sustained acclaim, intention to continue work in the area of 
claimed extraordinary ability is a core eligibility requirement. 
6 
SCOPS concluded, and we agree, that the evidence the Petitioner provided in support of the initial 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) exclusively relates to and documents his career as a 
professional soccer player between 2008 and 2022. Evidence ofacclaim as an athlete does not establish 
acclaim or extraordinary ability as a coach. See Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 917 (N.D. Ill. 2002) 
(finding that the "distinction between extraordinary ability as a coach and a player is a reasonable one, 
entitled to deference," because playing and coaching "are markedly different roles"). 
Because the Petitioner has identified that he seeks employment in the field of coaching, and the statute 
requires that he "seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability," 
the athlete to coach transition necessarily relies on demonstration of extraordinary ability, and thus 
sustained national or international acclaim, as a coach. If the evidence of record does not demonstrate 
that the Petitioner has sustained acclaim at the national or international level as a coach, then he cannot 
demonstrate that he meets all eligibility requirements for this classification. This would be the case even 
ifhe had met his burden to show that he met the initial evidentiary requirements and achieved the required 
level of sustained national or international acclaim as an athlete. 
The Petitioner must meet eligibility requirements at the time of filing. See 8 C.F .R. § 103 .2(b)(1 ). Here, 
the record does not demonstrate that, at the time of filing in December 2023, he had any formal coaching 
experience. Rather, the record shows that he obtained coaching licenses in 2023 and 2024 in preparation 
for future work as a coach. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that his "exceptional abilities extend beyond his accomplishments as a 
player to include his potential as a top-tier coach," emphasizing that he is equipped with the "tactical 
knowledge and leadership qualities necessary for coaching." He further emphasizes that he holds al I 
'-------------------'coaching license, noting that this credential is "universally recognized as the pinnacle qualification for soccer coaches," and that he has skills that are "directly 
transferable to a coaching role." Finally, the Petitioner maintains that"[ c ]oaching is a natural progression 
for an elite athlete of [his] caliber and aligns seamlessly with his extraordinary abilities as a player." 
While the Petitioner may show potential as a coach, he cannot rely on such potential to demonstrate his 
eligibility for this highly restrictive classification. Rather, it is his burden to demonstrate that he has 
already personally achieved sustained national or international acclaim as a coach, the field in which he 
intends to work in the United States. Claims that other professional athletes have successfully made the 
transition and achieved acclaim as coaches do not establish that he has done the same. 
With respect to the coaching licenses the Petitioner has attained to date, the documentation in the record 
identifies no requirements for thel Ilicense or other licenses other than completion ofthe required 
training course. The Petitioner has not supported his claim that the I I license he obtained is "the 
pinnacle qualification for soccer coaches" or that attainment of a specific license reflects his existing 
acclaim as a coach or contributes to such acclaim. The Petitioner has not established extraordinary ability 
as a coach, which is the occupation he intends to pursue in the United States. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed, SCOPS was correct to draw a distinction between the evidence regarding 
the Petitioner's career as an athlete and his intended future work as a coach. Even if the Petitioner had 
7 
met the initial evidence requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) based on his career as an athlete, 
the record shows he has not yet worked as a coach, and therefore his coaching activities cannot support 
a conclusion that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for classification 
as an alien of extraordinary ability as a soccer coach. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals who have already 
reached, and remain at, the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top, 
or who are embarking on new careers related to ones in which they had previously earned more 
recognition. Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree of recognition ofhis coaching work that rises 
to the level of sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed 
work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also 
section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the 
Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
