Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is an athlete who seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability as an athlete. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did she demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met four of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to prizes or awards and 
published material but that she had not satisfied the criteria associated with membership and 
performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that she meets the 
evidentiary criteria that SCOPS declined to grant. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we 
conclude that she has only met one criterion. 
l. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, the 
Petitioner must establish the circulation statistics are high relative to other similar forms of media. 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
2 
The Petitioner provided several print and online articles. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met 
the requirements of this criterion without providing any analysis. But we observe that even though 
the articles are about the Petitioner and her performances in the field, she relies on counsel's assertions 
in the request for evidence (RFE) response that the articles appeared in one of the three publication 
types. Without documentary evidence to support the claims, counsel's assertions in a brief do not 
constitute evidence nor will they satisfy the Petitioner's burden of proof. Matter of Y-H-L-, 29 I&N 
Dec. 698, 700 (BIA 2026) ( citing Matter ofMariscal-Hernandez, 28 I&N Dec. 666, 673 (BIA 2022) 
and Matter ofJJ Rodriguez, 27 I&N Dec. 762, 765-66 (BIA 2020)). See also Matter ofM-K-, 29 
I&N Dec. 556, 569 (BIA 2026); Matter of Khan, 28 I&N Dec. 850, 862 (BIA 2024); Matter of 
Arambula-Bravo, 28 T&N Dec. 388, 396-97 (BIA 2021 ). 
Lacking from the record is any objective and probative evidence demonstrating the material appeared 
in "either a widely circulated print newspaper or a specialized sports media platform" as counsel 
asserts in the RFE response. For example, the Petitioner did not include circulation and distribution 
information about the newspapers and magazines or the viewership of the websites. Additionally, 
even though the Petitioner provided translation certificates reflecting the publication's name in which 
the articles appeared (the print version), the majority of the original evidence in a foreign language 
does not contain the publication's name. The Petitioner, therefore, did not offer probative evidence 
corroborating her claims and this diminishes the evidentiary value of that material. 
Accordingly, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination as it relates to this criterion. 
2. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that she is a member of an association in her field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 
2780815, at *3--4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility under this criterion based on three memberships. SCOPS determined 
that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements ofthis criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner only claims 
one membership in the Venezuelan Athletics Federation (FVA). First, she provides letters with her 
appeal that postdate the petition filing date and the denial. SCOPS requested this type of evidence in 
the RFE and while she provided letters in response to the RFE, that correspondence was inadequate to 
demonstrate outstanding achievements were required as a condition of membership, or that the 
associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized experts to judge the achievements of 
prospective members to determine if the achievements are outstanding. 
Because the governing regulations and SCOPS' RFE provided the Petitioner with both formal notice 
and a reasonable opportunity to submit the necessary documentation, we decline to consider new

evidence proffered for the first time on appeal. Under 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l l), all requested evidence 
must be submitted together at one time. It is well-established that an administrative appellate body 
will not review or consider evidence first introduced on appeal when the filing party was put on notice 
of the requirement and given a meaningful opportunity to provide it before the denial. Matter of 
Furtado, 28 I&NDec. 794, 801-02 (BIA2024) (citing Matter ofSoriano, 19 I&NDec. 764,766 (BIA 
1988)); Matter ofIzaguirre, 27 I&N Dec. 67, 71 (BIA 2017) (same); Matter ofJin, 29 I&N Dec. 441, 
449 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter of Obaigbena, 19 I&N Dec. 533, 537 (BIA 1988)). Additional 
precedents reinforce this restriction, noting that the appellate process is not a venue for the primary 
submission of evidence. See Matter ofN-P-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 347, 349 (BIA 2025) (quoting Matter of 
German Santos, 28 I&N Dec. 552, 561 n.4 (BIA 2022), aff'd, No. 22-2072, 2025 WL 269410 l (3d 
Cir. Sept. 22, 2025) ("[w]e do not consider new evidence for the first time on appeal")); Matter of 
D-J-L-, 29 I&N Dec. 485, 489 (BIA 2026); Matter ofD-G-E-A- & N-G-G-E-, 29 I&N Dec. 570, 577 
n.5 (BIA 2026) ( citing Matter ofFedorenko, 19 I&N Dec. 57, 73-74 (BIA 1984)). 
But she also continues to rely on the FV A bylaws in this appeal. That is the same evidence SCOPS 
concluded fell short of demonstrating the FV A requires outstanding achievements to become a 
member. While the bylaws reflect membership on the relevant team is based on certain standards, 
they are silent regarding a specific international ranking threshold for membership eligibility, what 
"the minimum marks of the approved year" must be, nor has the Petitioner identified what section of 
the bylaws-or any other evidence in the record-establish that the achievements of prospective 
members are judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. As a 
result, two of this criterion's requirements remain unmet and the Petitioner has not demonstrated that 
SCOPS erred when they made an adverse determination under this criterion. 
B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner satisfies one criterion, she does not meet the criteria regarding 
published material and membership. While she argues and submits evidence for one additional 
criterion on appeal relating to performing in a leading or critical role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), it 
is unnecessary that we make a decision on this additional ground because she cannot numerically meet 
the required number of criteria. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of 
three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the remaining issue. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 
328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); 
see also Matter ofF-B-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 456,460 (BIA 2026) (declining to reach alternative issues on 
appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-
4 
(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 
WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026) (concluding the two-step framework was adopted without 
required notice-and-comment rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report­
which serves solely to explain how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two 
versions of the bill-as being more authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the 
purpose of which includes guiding the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the 
judicial branch in its interpretation. That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before 
and since the Mukherji court made it. Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final 
merits determination in this matter. Patel, 596 U.S. 332. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
