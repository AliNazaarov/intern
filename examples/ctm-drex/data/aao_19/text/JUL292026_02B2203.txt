Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish that he is sustained national or 
international acclaim as one ofthe small percentage at the top of his field to demonstrate his eligibility as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien' s entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immig1: Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 20 l 0) ( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US 
Citizenship & lmmigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), aff'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

C. Final Merits Determination 
A final merits determination requires analyzing all of a petitioner's accomplishments and weighing 
the totality of the evidence to determine if they are among the small percentage at the top of their field 
and have sustained national or international acclaim sufficient to demonstrate that they have 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2) and (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 1 
As noted above, SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not establish that he has sustained national 
or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of his field. On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that SCOPS erred in its final merits determination and did not fully account for the 
evidentiary weight and cumulative impact of the documentation submitted. 
We will remand the matter because SCOPS' decision does not sufficiently apprise the Petitioner of the 
deficiencies in the evidence of record and offers unclear, contradictory, and conclusory statements 
without discussing the evidence. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l)(i); see also Matter ofM-P-, 20 I&N Dec. 
786 (BIA 1994) (finding that a decision must fully explain the reasons for denying a motion to allow 
the respondent a meaningful opportunity to challenge the determination on appeal). 
For example, pertaining to the published material about the Petitioner, SCOPS states that while articles 
featuring the Petitioner praise his work, "the evidence does not establish that [he] received national or 
international acclaim from the published material." However, SCOPS does not discuss which articles 
they considered and why they do not establish sustained acclaim. On appeal, the Petitioner emphasizes 
that the chronology of the published materials demonstrates his sustained acclaim from 2022 through 
2024. On remand, SCOPS should consider the chronology and any other evidence submitted in 
support of the published material, and explain why it does or does not establish sustained acclaim and 
that the Petitioner is at the very top of the field.

Similarly, in discussing the criterion concerning authorship of scholarly articles, SCOPS provides an 
overview ofwhat the evidence should demonstrate, concluding that the evidence does not indicate that

if the evidence does not show that others have relied upon the author['s] findings." SCOPS advises

Concerning the Petitioner's roles for organizations having distinguished reputations, although SCOPS 
states that the Petitioner established that he "has worked in a leading/critical role," SCOPS determined, 
without discussion of the evidence, that the evidence does not show whether his professional 
achievements have garnered him national or international acclaim or demonstrate his recognition on a 
national or international scale. And without further elaboration, SCOPS concludes that he "did not 
establish how his roles resulted in widespread acclaim from his field, that he drew significant attention 
from the greater field, or that [the] overall field considers him to be at the very top of the field of 
endeavor." 
Further, the decision includes statements that contradict SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner met 
certain criteria. Of the Petitioner's original contributions to his field, SCOPS states that "they may 
have been successful overall and USCIS does not question that [his] contribution has value." SCOPS 
then concludes, without explanation, that the record does not contain evidence to support his claim 
that he is one of the very few at the top of his field. Confusingly, SCOPS follows this conclusion with 
what appears to be guidance on Kazarian's two-part approach to assessing an individual's 
extraordinary ability. While SCOPS concludes that the Petitioner has made original contributions of 
major significance to his field, SCOPS emphasizes that the major significance ofcontributions "should 
also be discussed in Part Two [ a final merits determination] since many of the same arguments as to 
why the original contributions are not of 'major significance' can also be articulated to show that they 
are NOT indicative of [extraordinary ability]." SCOPS does not include an analysis ofthe Petitioner's 
contributions. 
Similarly, although SCOPS initially found that the Petitioner met the criterion demonstrating that he 
has commanded a high salary in relation to others in his field, SCOPS states in the final merits 
determination that the Petitioner has not shown how his "salary for his role compares to others in the 
field as high" and that the "record does not indicate he has received a high salary in relation to others 
in the field." SCOPS also included a confusing explanation for its determination that the "amount" of 
his salary is not indicative of sustained national or international acclaim, stating that "there is no 
explanation or evidence as to why the petitioner's services would lead to a higher salary amount and 
if the elevated amount was based on his extraordinary ability to demonstrate that he is considered at 
the upper echelon of his field" (quoted as written). SCOPS does not articulate deficiencies concerning 
the Petitioner's salary and contradicts its initial conclusion that he met the requirements of the 
criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
Because SCOPS did not sufficiently address the Petitioner's claims and evidence, providing a decision 
with an incomplete and unclear analysis of the evidence, we will remand the matter for issuance of a 
new decision. SCOPS must evaluate the totality of the evidence in the context of a final merits 
determination. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115. SCOPS must determine whether the Petitioner has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, that 
he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe 
Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; and see generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2). SCOPS may request any additional evidence considered pertinent 
to the new determination. As such, we express no opinion regarding the ultimate resolution of this 
case on remand. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
