Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur, requests classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for 
U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner met two of ten 
initial evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that he also satisfied the following seven evidentiary criteria: 
• Membership in associations in his field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Published material about himself and his work; 
• Participation as a judge of other's work in the field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), (iv), (v), (vii), (viii), (ix). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we reverse some of SCOPS' findings and conclude that the Petitioner met the requisite number 
of evidentiary requirements. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for 
a final merits determination and entry of a new decision consistent with the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )( I )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that Colombian universities awarded the Petitioner a bachelor of science degree in 
aeronautical administration and a master's degree in operational safety management. He served as a 
pilot in the Colombian air force for about 12 years, including as a: squadron group commander; 
military teacher; and training director. 
While in the air force, the Petitioner founded a mobile application/digital platform for peer-to-peer 
car, boat, and airplane rentals in Latin America. Now retired from the military, he states that he seeks 
to expand his business by offering shared charter flights in the United States.

1. Lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards 
This criterion requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines whether petitioners - as opposed to their 
employers, for example-received prizes or awards. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 1. Ifso, the Agency then determines whether the awards honored excellence in the applicable 
fields and have national or international recognition. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he won a business innovation award m 2021 and an 
"entrepreneur of the year" award in 2022. 
Contrary to SCOPS' finding, the record does not demonstrate that either award has national or 
international recognition. The Petitioner submitted evidence that a Colombian government ministry 
annually issues the business innovation award to promote new ideas and increase business productivity 
and competitiveness in the country. A national government agency's issuance of an award, however, 
does not alone establish a prize's national recognition. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 
183 (D.N.J. 2021) ("[A]s reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not the 
awarding organization.") ( emphasis in original). The Petitioner must show how others beyond the 
Colombian government ministry viewed his award. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 
2 Even if we could review the prior decision, it does not specify its reasonings for its evidentiary findings. Rather, the 
decision discusses all the evidence together in a final merits dete1mination. 
3 
(D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence of how a larger audience viewed Plaintiffs awards, there was no 
way for the agency to evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this 
criterion.") 
The Petitioner submitted copies of online news articles about the business innovation award. But the 
record lacks evidence demonstrating that the publications have national or international readerships. 
The record therefore does not establish the award's receipt of national or international recognition. 
Also, the record lacks evidence that the 2022 entrepreneur of the year award received recognition. 
The Petitioner's evidence does not demonstrate that his awards for excellence in his field have national 
or international recognition. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary decision. 
2. Membership in Associations 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a pet1t10ner must submit "[d]ocumentation of the alien's 
membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in two Colombian associations: one for 
businesspersons; and the other for business administrators. SCOPS found insufficient evidence that 
either organization requires outstanding achievements of their members or that recognized experts 
judge their members. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS undervalued documentation and misinterpreted one 
association's member selection process. He claims that, to attain membership in the businesspersons' 
association, "a rigorous process must be completed, reviewed, and judged by the members ofthe board 
of directors and [the association's] president, [who is an] international recognized expert in the field 
of business." 
The record, however, contains inconsistent descriptions ofthe association's member selection process. 
In response to the Director's request for additional evidence, the Petitioner submitted a letter from the 
association's president stating that "the selection process and the final decision to grant membership 
to a new businessman are the exclusive responsibility of [the association's] President." (emphasis 
in original). As previously indicated, the Petitioner contends on appeal that review and judgment of 
new association members falls to not only the organization's president but also to members ofits board 
of directors. He further claims that the association's general assembly of members and fiscal auditor 
have roles in selecting members. He states: "Within the process to be a member, each phase of the 
study must be approved by [all] these groups of people." The record, however, does not support any 
of the Petitioner's descriptions of the selection process. A copy of the association's bylaws omits 
member selection among the duties ofthe president, directors, general assembly, or fiscal auditor. See 
Marllantas, Inc. v. Rodriguez, 806 F. App'x 864, 869 (11th Cir. 2020) (citing Matter ofHo, 19 I&N 
Dec. 582, 591 (BIA 1988)) (requiring "the petitioner to resolve any inconsistencies in the record by 
independent objective evidence.") 
4 
Further, if others besides the president judge new association members, the Petitioner has not 
established their recognition as experts in their fields. See Nelson v. USCIS, No. CV 21-10334-LTS, 
2022 WL 20583561, at *5 (D. Mass. June 15, 2022) (finding that this criterion "demands membership 
in an organization that requires 'outstanding achievement' as judged by experts, and not merely 
membership in selective organizations in the field"). 
For the foregoing reasons, evidence does not demonstrate that the associations in the field to which 
the Petitioner belongs require outstanding achievements of their members as judged by recognized 
experts. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' decision regarding this evidentiary criterion. 
3. Published Material About the Petitioner 
This criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the material, and 
any necessary translation." Id. 
When adjudicating this criterion, users first determines whether published material relates to a 
petitioner and their specific work in the field. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), 
Criterion 3. Material covering a broader topic meets the criterion ifthe material includes "a substantial 
discussion of the person's work in the field and mentions the person in connection to the work." Id. 
users then considers whether a publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication. Id. Relevant factors include, for professional and major trade 
publications, their intended audiences and, for major trade publications and other major media, their 
relative circulations, readerships, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies ofonline articles. SCOPS found that the material does not sufficiently 
relate to his work in his field. 
As SCOPS found, some of the articles do not meet this evidentiary criterion because, while they 
discuss the Petitioner's company, they do not mention him. See Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 
3d 837, 847 (C.D. Cal. 2022) (affirming USCrS' finding that articles about a petitioner's water-polo 
team did not satisfy this criterion where they did not reference her); see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 3 (requiring qualifying published material to "mention[] the person in connection 
to the work"). 
The Petitioner, however, also submitted a copy of a January 2024 article that appeared on a major 
Colombian financial website regarding his selection to attend a I tforum. The 
article discusses him, his company, and a school for entrepreneurs that he founded. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B) ("[A]lthough some of the regulatory language relating to evidence occasionally 
uses plurals, it is entirely possible that the presentation of a single piece of evidence in a specific 
evidentiary category may be sufficient.") 
The Petitioner submitted published material about himself in a major medium relating to his work in 
his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding.

4. Judge of Others' Work in the Field 
To meet this criterion, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence ofthe[ir] participation, either individually 
or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which 
classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, as a "jury" member, he evaluated business-related research 
papers of graduate and undergraduate students at a Colombian university. He specifically provided 
documentation that he judged the thesis of a master of business administration student in 2022. 
SCOPS found the evidence insufficient to link the Petitioner's entrepreneurial field to economic 
sciences, the field of the university department that asked him to judge. SCOPS also stated: "USCIS 
recognize[s] judging the work of others for [ doctoral] dissertation only and not at the graduate level. 
Therefore, this criterion has not been met." 
By distinguishing the judging of graduate theses from that of doctoral dissertations, SCOPS imposed 
an evidentiary requirement unstated in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). The regulation requires evidence 
ofonly "participation ... as a judge ofthe work ofothers in the same or an allied field ofspecification." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). Contrary to SCOPS' finding, the regulation does not require judged work 
of any particular level. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121 (barring USCIS from imposing evidentiary 
requirements beyond those stated in the regulation). Under Agency policy, evidence of "[s]erving as a 
member of a Ph.D. dissertation committee that makes the final judgment as to whether a candidate's 
body of work satisfies the requirements for a doctoral degree" would meet this criterion. 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 4. But, like the regulation, USCIS policy does not exclude 
evidence of graduate work evaluation from satisfying this requirement. While the difference between 
judging Ph.D. and graduate work may be relevant in a final merits determination, neither the regulation 
nor USCIS policy require judgment of work at a doctoral level. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121-22 
( finding this criterion met by evidence of an alien's participation as "a judge ofgraduate-level diploma 
works"). 
Also, the regulation specifically allows for the judgment of others' work "in ... an allied field of 
specification for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). The Petitioner submitted 
copies of scholarly articles indicating a close relationship between the business and economics fields. 
The evidence therefore shows that he judged others' work in "an allied field." Cf Sarin v. United 
States, No. 2:22-CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. July 27, 2023) (affirming 
USCIS' conclusion that the judging of a personality contest "was not in the field of acting or in an 
'allied field' to acting"). 
The Petitioner met this criterion by submitting evidence of his participation as a judge of others' work 
in an allied field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
5. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
6 
users first determines whether a petitioner made original contributions in their field. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. If so, the Agency then considers whether the original 
contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
To meet this criterion, the Petitioner relied on evidence that he founded his company and a school for 
entrepreneurs. The evidence indicates that his company, which was established in 2012, employs 26 
people and that the school, founded in 2017, has trained 378 entrepreneurs. SCOPS, however, found 
insufficient evidence that these original contributions have major significance in his field. 
We agree with the Petitioner that SCOPS erroneously disregarded support letters he submitted. 
SCOPS discounted two letters because they lack dates, stating that "is one of the requirements for a 
letter to be considered evidence under users regulations." Contrary to SCOPS' decision, however, 
no applicable regulation requires dating of evidentiary letters. 
SCOPS disregarded a third letter because it was dated after the petition's filing. SCOPS stated: "There 
is no evidence on record explaining why this letter was not available or did not exist prior to [the 
petition's] filing date." 
A petitioner must establish their eligibility "at the time of filing the benefit request." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.2(b )(1 ). But that regulation does not bar evidence that post-dates a petition's filing if the 
documentation relates to the petitioner's eligibility at the time of filing. As the Petitioner states, 
SCOPS should have considered the letters on his behalf because "they all refer to accomplishments, 
activities, and contributions made prior to the initial filing of my [Form] r-140 petition." 
Although SCOPS should have considered the support letters, they do not demonstrate the major 
significance of the Petitioner's original contributions to his field. Support letters: 
should explain in specific terms why the witnesses believe the [petitioner] to be of the 
caliber of a person with extraordinary ability. Letters that merely reiterate users' 
definitions relating to this classification or make general and expansive statements 
regarding the [petitioner] and [their] accomplishments are generally not persuasive. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). 
The support letters for the Petitioner contain conclusory statements and do not specifically explain his 
contributions' significance to his field. For example, a letter from a founder of a start-up business 
accelerator states: "What truly sets [the Petitioner] apart is his ability to solve complex problems with 
creative and viable solutions, his capacity to adapt to fast-changing and challenging environments, and 
his commitment to creating value that is not just economic but also social." But the letter does not 
provide examples of these purported abilities or explain their significance to the field. Also, a letter 
from a Colombian entrepreneur states that "[the Petitioner's] impact on the global entrepreneurial 
landscape, particularly in fostering inclusive and socially responsible business practices, has been 
transformative." But the letter does not detail the Petitioner's contributions or explain how they have 
purportedly transformed his field. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 568850, at 
*9 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) ('[T]he agency may reject supporting letters that lack 'specific evidence' 
about how the plaintiff's contribution was a major contribution.") 
7 
The record lacks evidence that the Petitioner's original contributions have major significance in his 
field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary criterion. 
6. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the[ir] authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
users first determines whether petitioners authored scholarly articles in applicable fields. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 6. "[A] scholarly article reports on original 
research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse. . . . Scholarly articles are also generally peer 
reviewed by other experts in the field of specialization" and contain footnotes, endnotes, or 
bibliographies. Id. 
users then considers a publication's qualifications as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication. Id. As previously indicated, relevant factors for professional 
and major trade publications include their intended audiences and, for major media, their circulations 
or readerships relative to other media in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies ofan online book and various articles he wrote about entrepreneurship 
and business. The record, however, does not establish any of these writings as "scholarly articles." 
They lack footnotes, endnotes, or bibliographies and do not demonstrate their pre-publication review 
by experts in the business field. The website that published most of the writings states that it allows 
writers to submit their own original articles for publication on the site. The Petitioner's book provides 
advice on starting businesses and comprises more than 50 pages. But the remaining articles run only 
a page or two each, and none of the writings report on original research, experimentation, or 
philosophical discourse. The evidence therefore does not establish the Petitioner's writings as 
scholarly articles. 
Also, the record lacks evidence that the writings' publishers are professional or major trade 
publications or other major media. The website containing most of the articles describes itself as "a 
powerful marketing tool for both businesses and webmasters." It further states its publication of 
articles in a variety of fields, from "animals and insects" to "travel," indicating that it targets a general 
audience rather than entrepreneurs and business researchers/experts. 
For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner did not submit evidence of his authorship of scholarly articles 
in his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
7. Display of the Petitioner's Work in Artistic Exhibits or Showcases 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
users first determines whether displayed work constitutes petitioners' work products. See generally 
6 US/CS Policy ManualF.2(B)(I), Criterion 7. The Agency next considers whether the display venues 
8 
- including virtual venues - constitute artistic exhibitions or showcases. Id. The term "exhibition" 
ordinarily means "a public showing (as of works of art, objects of manufacture, or athletic skill)." 
Exhibition, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, in June 2023, he served as a panelist on an episode of a 
business reality television program. The episode involved entrepreneurs pitching ideas for businesses 
to a panel of investors who then decided whether to personally invest in the proposed ventures. 
As SCOPS' found, the evidence does not establish the business reality TV show as an artistic 
exhibition or showcase. The regulation requires display "at artistic exhibitions or showcases." See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) ( emphasis added). In this context, the word "artistic" ordinarily means 
"relating to or characteristic of art or artists." Artistic, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, supra. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated the artistic nature of considering proposals for new businesses. 
See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1122 (finding that self-publishing a textbook, lectures at a community 
college, and presentations at conferences did not constitute displays at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases); see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 7. ("[T]he plain language of the 
criterion includes the modifier 'artistic' and explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases be 
artistic in nature.") 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that the TV show "is not merely an entertainment program; it is a 
platform where real businesses and investment proposals are presented." The entertaining nature of 
the TV show, however, is not at issue. Rather, contrary to the governing regulation, the record does 
not establish the show as "artistic" in nature. "USCIS only considers non-artistic exhibitions or 
showcases as part of a properly supported claim of comparable evidence." 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 7. Contrary to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), the Petitioner has not claimed the 
inapplicability ofthis criterion to his occupation. See Goncharov v. Allen, No. 3:21-CV-1372-B, 2022 
WL 17327304, at **4-5 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 29, 2022) (finding that USCIS properly disregarded proposed 
alternate comparable evidence where a petitioner "did not argue" the inapplicability of the applicable 
evidentiary requirement to his field). 
The Petitioner neither submitted evidence of his work's display at artistic exhibitions or showcases 
nor claimed the inapplicability of this evidentiary requirement to his occupation. We will therefore 
affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this criterion. 
8. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first considers whether a petitioner performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, 
establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is (or was) a leader within 
an organization, establishment, division, or department. Id. In contrast, a critical role means "that the 
person has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities." Id. 
9 
USCIS next considers whether an organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. The word "distinguished" ordinarily means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, supra. Relevant considerations include not only an organization's relative 
size and longevity but also other factors, such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage of it. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his founding and management of his company and service, for a 
three-month period in 2023, as dean of a Colombian university's economic sciences faculty. 
As SCOPS found, the record lacks sufficient evidence that the Petitioner's company and the university 
faculty where he served as a dean have distin uished re utations. He submitted evidence that the 
school is university. He also provided 
evidence that its recent global university rankings have ranged froml I But the record 
shows that the Petitioner led the school's economic sciences faculty, not the entire university. Thus, 
he must specifically demonstrate the distinguished reputation of the university department that he led 
rather than that of the entire school. Even assuming that the university has a distinguished reputation, 
the Petitioner has not demonstrated the distinguished reputation ofthe school's department that he led. 
See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8 ("USCIS determines whether the organization or 
establishment, or the department or division/or which the person holds or held a leading or critical 
role, has a distinguished reputation.") (emphasis added). 
Regarding the Petitioner's company, the record shows that: he founded it in 2012; it generated 
$420,000 in investment funding in 2022; and it recently employed 26 people. An online article 
described the company as "innovative," and a U.S. business research professor stated: "Under [the 
Petitioner]'s leadership, the company has garnered praise for its punctuality, comfort, and customer 
service, continually striving to innovate and improve the shared charter flight experience." But the 
evidence does not sufficiently demonstrate the company's eminence, distinction, or excellence. See 
Distinguished, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, supra ( defining the word "distinguished" as 
"marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence"). 
The Petitioner did not submit evidence that the organizations he led have distinguished reputations. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary criterion. 
9. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, as chief executive officer (CEO) of his Colombian company 
in 2023 and 2024, he received a base monthly salary of25,400,000 pesos (about $6,000). He provided 
2024 salary information listing the average monthly salary for Colombian CEOs as 10,458,460 pesos 
( about $2,500). Thus, the evidence indicates that his salary is more than double that of the average 
Colombian CEO. See Berardo v. USCIS, No. 3:19-CV-01796-SB, 2020 WL 6161459, at *5 (D. Or. 
Oct. 20, 2020) ("[T]he appropriate test compares the petitioner's salary with the average salary of 
10 
others in the same position in the field."); see also Buletini v. INS, 860 F. Supp. 1222, 1232 (E.D. 
Mich. 1994) (finding this criterion met where a physician's salary "was twice the average salary of 
other Albanian doctors"). 
SCOPS found the Colombian CEO wage information inapplicable to the Petitioner because he stated 
his proposed occupation on the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers, as an 
"entrepreneur, consultant, advisor, startup, and business developer." SCOPS also noted that monthly 
paystubs from his company list his job title as "general manager," not CEO, and questioned the 
reliability of the Colombian wage information. 
Although the Petitioner described his proposed occupation as "entrepreneur" on the Form I-140, his 
written statement and business plan indicate that he would continue to serve as his company's CEO 
during its proposed expansion into the United States. We also credit his explanation that the terms 
"CEO" and "General Manager" are synonymous, both referring to a company's highest executive 
position. Thus, the Petitioner properly compares his salary to CEOs. 
Also, the record does not support SCOPS' questioning of the Colombian wage data. Online 
information identifies the information's publisher as a reputable provider of recruitment and payroll 
services and data reports. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence ofhis receipt of a high salary relative to others in his field. We will 
therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met the requested category's requisite number of evidentiary requirements. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Specifically, he submitted documentation of: published material about him 
and his work in his field; his participation as a judge ofothers' work in the field; and his commandment 
of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the field. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (iv), (ix). 
USCIS must now conduct a final merits determination regarding his claimed extraordinary ability. 
SCOPS did not make a final determination in this matter. Rather than make one in the first instance, 
we will remand the matter. 
On remand, SCOPS must determine whether the Petitioner demonstrated sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognized achievements establishing him as one of that small percentage 
who has risen to his field's very top. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)(2). SCOPS should 
consider any potentially relevant evidence ofrecord, even ifit does not fit one ofthe regulatory criteria 
or was not presented as comparable evidence. Id. The Petitioner's burden ofproof includes explaining 
the significance ofhis evidence and how it demonstrates his sustained national or international acclaim 
and recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval or denial should depend on the evidence's 
type and quality. Id.
