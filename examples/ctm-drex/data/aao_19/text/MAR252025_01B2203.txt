Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a martial art athlete and coach, seeks first preference immigrant classification as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
The Director of the Nebraska Service Center denied the petition, concluding that the record did 
not establish that the Petitioner had demonstrated his extraordinary ability in the totality of the 
circumstances. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the 
evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions 
in this matter de novo. Matter ofChrista 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon 
de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, 
or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the 
field through extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit 
prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a pettt10ner can 
demonstrate sustained acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items 
such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th 
Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if 
fulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits determination). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Director determined that the Petitioner established the requirements for at least three of the 
initial criteria. As such, we will proceed to a final merits determination. In these evaluations, we 
analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate that a petitioner has extraordinary ability in the field of 
endeavor. Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. We evaluate whether a petitioner has demonstrated, by 
a preponderance of the evidence, that they have sustained national or international acclaim and if 
they are one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Id. Their 
achievements must be recognized in the field through extensive documentation. Id. In this matter, 
we determine that the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility.

was a member of theI ITeam from 1997 to 2006, first as an athlete and 
then from 2004, as an Assistant Head Coach. He also served as team captain. He received a 
bronze medal at both the 20061 ICham~ionship and the 2005 I I

Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
Although the Petitioner has established that he meets the plain language of several of the criteria,

Petitioner's awards, while the Petitioner has shown evidence he participated and placed in several 
competitions in his career, he has not provided evidence sufficient to show that he participated in 
a competition since approximately 2017. Moreover, he did not provide evidence showing he

Concerning the Petitioner's experience as a coach, while he has presented documentation that he 
has acted as a coach, he has not provided sufficient documentation to show that he has reached the 
top of that profession. As mentioned previously, he served as an Assistant Head Coach for the
I Iteam. Nevertheless, beyond evidence verifying his former position, the 
Petitioner did not submit more detailed elaborating on his work with the team. 
The Petitioner also provided a letter from I l the head coach of the I II lclub. The letter states that the Petitioner was formally employed as a coach at the club 
in 2019, and then worked as a volunteer from 2022 to 2023. He indicates that the Petitioner 
contributed to two students' victories, but did not provide farther details on the specific placement 
of the individuals or the significance of the competitions. The Petitioner presented two letters 
from his students' parents attesting to the Petitioner's coaching skills. The Petitioner additionally 
submitted a check from the club in payment for his services and some other documentation 
confirming his aforementioned experience coaching. This evidence shows that the Petitioner has 
acted as a coach, but does give further detail and is not sufficient enough to establish that his time 
as a coach has been noteworthy or even that he has significant experience coaching. It is not 
enough to demonstrate that the Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very top of the field 
of endeavor. Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115; Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. 
In summary, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals 
already at the top of their respective fields. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at 
the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of 
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). The Petitioner's evidence confirms that the 
Petitioner has acted as an athlete and coach in his field. Nevertheless, he has not submitted extensive 
documentation exhibiting, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, that he has attained a level ofexpertise 
placing him among that small percentage that has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual 
ofextraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons. In visa petition 
proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. 
The Petitioner has not met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
