Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a visual artist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, 
internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that she met at least three ofthe ten regulatory 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a visual artist who specializes in prop design and fabrication for the film and 
television industry. At the time of filing, she was a property assistant intending to continue her work 
in the United States to become a property master and a production designer. 
A. One-Time Achievement 
On appeal, the Petitioner does not address SCOPS' analysis concluding that she did not demonstrate 
international recognition for achievements in her field through receipt of a major, internationally 
recognized award. We consider the Petitioner's prior claims not raised or contested on appeal to be 
abandoned. An issue not raised on appeal is waived. See, e.g., Matter of O-R-E, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 
336 n.5. (BIA 2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M, 25 I&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012)). 
B. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS determined that the Petitioner satisfied the criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii), which concerns the display of work in artistic exhibitions or showcases, 
but that she did not meet any of the other claimed criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she 
meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii) and (ix).

3. Published Material 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner submitted articles about herself and her work from online publications. SCOPS 
acknowledged the articles and requested additional evidence to demonstrate that they originated from 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, such as circulation numbers and 
information about the publications' intended audiences. In response to the RFE, the Petitioner did not 
submit any additional information or evidence concerning the articles or publications. SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner did not address the deficiencies under the criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that"[ q]uantifiable [ a ]udience [ m ]etrics [ d]emonstrate ' [ m ]ajor' [ s ]tatus" 
of the publications, stating that SCOPS ignored the "submitted Media Analysis provid[ing] concrete, 
objective metrics." Review ofthe record does not reveal the presence of additional evidence concerning 
published material about the Petitioner beyond the articles initially submitted. The Petitioner's appeal 
brief attributes readership numbers to each publication, but the record does not include evidence to 
corroborate the numbers cited or other evidence concerning the nature of the publications. Again, a 
petitioner must support assertions with relevant, probative, and credible evidence. See Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. The record does not include evidence to demonstrate that the articles 
originate from professional or major trade publications or other major media. Further, as explained above, 
we are not obligated to consider new evidence on appeal. 5 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not established that she meets the requirements of this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish that she has met the three categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although she also asserts eligibility under the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), which concern high

salary or remuneration, we need not reach this additional ground because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the 
initial evidentiary requirement to meet at least three criteria under 8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3). 6 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (stating that 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) establishes a necessary, 
not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 7 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
