Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a petroleum reservoir and production engineer, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 
1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner established that he satisfied the 
initial evidentiary requirements, he did not demonstrate his sustained national or international acclaim 
and that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2); see also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and recognition 
ofhis or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F .R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not "readily 
apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable evidence" to establish 
eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F .3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is seeking to be classified as an alien of extraordinary ability as a reservoir and 
production engineer with expertise in reservoir stimulation, hydraulic fracturing in unconventional 
reservoirs, pressure transient analysis, rate transient analysis, water flooding, and artificial lift. He 
indicates that he has worked for more than 14 years in the oil and gas industry, currently working in 
the United States for I ~ as a lead perf01mance reservoir engineer. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
USCIS dete1mined the Petitioner met five of the seven claimed evidentiary criteria. However, USCIS 
concluded the Petitioner did not show he garnered sustained national or international acclaim and his 
achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating he is one of that small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contests USCIS' decision and argues his eligibility for two additional criteria. 
Because USC IS concluded that the Petitioner met at least three criteria, we need not dete1mine whether 
the Petitioner fulfills any further criteria. See Churyumov v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 
2:18-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 WL 5802041, at *3 (W.D. Wash. Nov. 7, 2019), ajj"d, No. 19-36094, 2022 
WL 1657356 (9th Cir. May 25, 2022) (finding that regardless of the plaintiff's claim of meeting seven 
criteria, the plaintiff met the threshold evidentiary burden when he met at least three criteria); see also 
2 
Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 819 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (stating that the petitioner's challenge to 
USCIS' finding at step one does not move the needle because USCIS determined that he prevailed at

determination below. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2),

B. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, the Petitioner provides multiple alternative arguments that USCIS erred in its final merits 
determination. In a supplemental appeal brief, the Petitioner cites a district court decision, Mukherji 
v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), in which the court objected to USCIS' reliance 
on Kazarian to justify its use of the final merits determination as the basis for dismissing an appeal. 
We disagree with the Petitioner's reliance on this non-precedent decision to argue that our decision 
was incorrect to conduct a final merits determination. 
In contrast to the precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are not 
bound to follow a decision of a United States district court. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 
(BIA 1993). Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, 
unpublished opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Atty Gen. United 
States, 147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non­
precedential opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 
n.5 ( 5th Cir. 2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 ( considering the reasoning of district court 
decisions). Notably, the Mukherji decision was issued by a district court in Nebraska. Whereas, in 
the present case, the petition was denied at the Texas Service Center and the Petitioner indicates his 
physical address is in Texas, outside the jurisdiction of the Nebraska court that decided Mukherji. In 
addition, the two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the 
Administrative Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 810 
(E.D. Pa. 2025); Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 
2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) 
(citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015)). 
In addition, the Petitioner argues on appeal that the Mukhe,ji decision acknowledged the recent United 
States Supreme Cout decision in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 144 S.Ct. 2244 (2024), which 
he claims diminished the validity of deference to an agency's interpretation of a statute. With limited 
deference, the Petitioner argues that the validity of the final merits determination outlined in Kazarian 
is a question of law which should be dete1mined by the court and the MukheFji court found it to be 
unlawful. 
While we acknowledge the Petitioner's assertions, the Petitioner has not provided any suppmi for his 
assertions and appears to misinterpret Loper's reach. Loper overturned Chevron USA v. Natural 
Resources Defense Council, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), in holding that federal courts need not defer to 
agencies' reasonable interpretations of ambiguous federal laws. Therefore, Loper provides the 
judiciary with the sole prerogative to "say what the law is," stating: "[c]ourts must exercise their 
independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority." Loper, 
144 S. Ct. at 2257, 2273. While Loper guides federal courts that review our interpretation of 
ambiguous statutes, it does not modify our own adjudication of immigrant petitions. 
3 
Moreover, Loper does not change how we interpret such laws or mandate, as the Petitioner suggests, 
an immediate change in how USCIS adjudicates immigrant petitions for aliens of extraordinary ability. 
Similarly, Loper involved an agency's interpretation of a statute. Id. at 2254-56. USCIS' denial of 
this petition, and our adjudication of this appeal, rest on a determination of whether the Petitioner met 
his burden to demonstrate his sustained national or international acclaim, he is one of the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and his achievements have been recognized in the 
field through extensive documentation under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2); it is not based on the agency's 
interpretation of a statute. Even if the regulations are ambiguous, our interpretation of them would 
merit deference under Auer v. Robbins, 519 U.S. 452 (1997), which Loper did not reverse. See, e.g., 
United States v. Boler, 115 F.4th 316, 322 n.4 (4th Cir. 2024) (declining to apply Loper to an issue 
involving an agency's interpretation of its own regulations). Accordingly, we are not persuaded by 
the Petitioner's assertions and will conduct a de novo review of the record considering the two-part 
analysis described in Kazarian and in the USCIS Policy Manual. 
In the alternative, the Petitioner argues that USCIS' final merits determination erred in the analysis of 
the evidence by imposing novel requirements in contradiction to the law, applied an incorrect 
evidentiary standard, and misinterpreted the law. When considering the totality of the evidence, he 
claims it clearly supports his sustained national or international acclaim in the field and that he is one 
of that small percentage at the very top of his field. As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the 
Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, his sustained national or international 
acclaim, 1 he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and his 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits 
determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to 
determine if his successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of 
endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 
F.3d at 1119-20.2 Here, the record reflects USCIS properly analyzed the submitted documentation 
and applied the correct evidentiary standard. Upon de novo review, the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated his eligibility. 3 
Because extraordinary ability is an elite level ofaccomplishment whose recognition necessarily entails 
a judgement call, it cannot be established through meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
alone. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394-395. Congress sets a very high benchmark for aliens 
of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been 
recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) ofthe Act.

Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained

for the classification sought, we find the record insufficient to demonstrate that he has sustained 
national or international acclaim in his field and is among the small percentage at the top of his field 
of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
