Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a ballroom dance choreographer, requests his classification under the employment­
based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; see also Sands v. U.S. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 308 F. App'x 418, 419 
(11th Cir. 2009) (holding that decisions on extraordinary-ability petitions under section 203(b )( 1 )(A) 
of the Act are discretionary). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
one often initial evidentiary criteria-two less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that, in finding that he did not satisfy six other requirements, SCOPS 
overlooked evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he met at least three evidentiary criteria. But, because he did not establish 
acclaim or recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage at his field's top, we will 
dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) then makes a final merits determination. To merit final approval, the 
record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and 
recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step review process to be 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
In his home country of Russia, the Petitioner earned a bachelor's degree in state/municipal 
administration and a master's degree in economics/marketing. He also established two businesses. 
But the record shows that his passion is for ballroom dancing. He began dancing at age 7 and has 
since served as a dance coach, choreographer, and judge. 
Now in the United States, the Petitioner states that he seeks to work as a choreographer at U.S. dance 
schools. He also hopes to develop an online dance course and eventually open his own network of 
U.S. dance schools. 
B. The Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He must therefore meet at least three of the evidentiary requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner met one initial evidentiary criterion by 
submitting evidence of his performance in leading or critical roles for organizations with distinguished 
reputations. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he satisfied six additional evidentiary requirements by 
submitting evidence of his: 
• Receipt ofnationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in his 
field; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements as judged 
by nationally or internationally recognized experts;

• Published materials about him and work in his field in major professional publications, 
trade publications or other major media; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in the field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; and 
• Display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v), (vii). 
To satisfy an evidentiary criterion, the Petitioner's documentation must objectively meet the 
parameters of an applicable regulatory description. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B). 
"[N]either USCIS nor [the] AAO [Administrative Appeals Office] may unilaterally impose novel 
substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010). 
1. Published Materials About the Petitioner 
This evidentiary criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is 
sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." 
When adjudicating this evidentiary requirement, USCIS first determines whether published material 
relates to a petitioner and their specific work in their field. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F .2(B )( 1 ), Criterion 3. The Agency next considers whether a publication qualifies as a professional 
publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. Id. Relevant factors for professional 
and trade publications include their intended audiences. Id. For major trade publications and other 
major media, relevant factors include their relative circulations, readerships, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies oftwo articles interviewing him about the benefits ofchildren learning 
to ballroom dance. Russian newspapers published the articles online in 2024 and 2025. SCOPS 
concluded that he did not demonstrate that either ofthe publications qualify as a professional or major 
trade publication, or other major media. 
We disagree. The Petitioner submitted online information showing that an independent survey ranked 
the newspaper that published the 2025 interview as the most popular Russian Internet medium in 2023 
in the "News and Media" category. A preponderance of the evidence therefore shows that the 
newspaper is a major medium. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 185 (D.N.J. 2021) 
(stating that "courts generally agree that the phrase ['other major media'] can mean a publication with 
significant reach and recognition"); see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B) ("[ A ]lthough some of the 
regulatory language relating to evidence occasionally uses plurals, it is entirely possible that the 
presentation ofa single piece ofevidence in a specific evidentiary category may be sufficient.") Thus, 
the 2025 online article meets this evidentiary criterion. 
The Petitioner submitted published material in a major medium about him and his work in the field. 
We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding.

2. Participation as a Judge of Others' Work 
To satisfy this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the[ir] participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge ofthe work ofothers in the same or an allied field ofspecification 
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner's evidence met this criterion. He submitted a copy of his 2018 license as a dance judge 
from a Russian dance federation and a letter from the federation's president stating the Petitioner's 
participation as a judge at various ballroom dancing competitions in the "Children" and "Juniors" 
categories. The letter specifically identifies the Petitioner as a judge at a 2019 competition, and he 
submitted a copy of a "final protocol" for the event listing him as a member of the judging panel. 
SCOPS erred in finding that: 
[t]he weight given to evidence submitted to fulfill the criterion at 8 CFR 205.5(h)(3)(iv) 
... depends on the extent to which such evidence demonstrates, reflects, or is consistent 
with sustained national or international acclaim at the very top of the alien's field of 
endeavor. ... For example, judging a national contest of professionals is of far greater 
probative value than judging a competition for novices. 
As previously indicated, USCIS may not "unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121. This criterion 
requires only that a petitioner judge others' work in the petitioner's or an allied field. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 205.5(h)(3)(iv) Thus, SCOPS erred in requiring that the Petitioner's evidence demonstrate or reflect 
sustained national or international acclaim. Rather, USCTS does not consider acclaim until a final 
merits determination. See Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346--47 (W.D. Wash. 2011) 
("USCIS may not conclude that evidence does not satisfy an evidentiary criterion by looking beyond 
that criterion to the final merits determination"), aff'd, 683 F .3d 1030 (9th Cir. 2012); see generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(2) (stating that, in a final merits determination, "the petition must 
demonstrate that the person has sustained national or international acclaim and that their achievements 
have been recognized in the field ofexpertise, indicating that the person is one ofthat small percentage 
who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor"). 
The Petitioner satisfied this evidentiary criterion by submitting evidence that he judged others' work 
in his or an allied field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
The Petitioner has met the requisite three evidentiary criteria. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Thus, we 
need not decide whether he satisfied any other evidentiary requirements. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 
429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" 
on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
SCOPS did not make a final merits determination. So, we will next do so.

C. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field - that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. USCIS must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
The record shows the Petitioner's journey from a young, award-winning ballroom dancer to a talented 
choreographer, dance coach, and judge. Considering the evidence in total, however, he has not 
established his receipt of acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage 
at his field's very top. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his receipt of 21 first-place dancing awards as a teenager. But 
the record indicates that the awards generated little acclaim or recognition for him beyond the dance 
organizations that administered the competitions. See Donskoy v. USCIS, No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 
2021 WL 5240224, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 11, 2021) ("[W]hat matters for the purpose of the visa 
classification is whether the award brought [ the petitioner] individual acclaim.") He submitted a letter 
from the president of a Russian dance federation indicating that Russian or other European television 
stations broadcast some of the competitions. But the letter lacks details about the TV coverage and 
whether it included the Petitioner's awards. The record also lacks corroborating evidence. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.(2)(B)(3) ("[S]tatements made by the witnesses should be corroborated by 
documentary evidence in the record.") 
Also, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his awards fall under his stated endeavor's field: 
ballroom choreography. The record indicates his receipt of the awards for ballroom dancing, not 
choreography. See, e.g., Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 843-45 (C.D. Cal. 2022) 
(holding that awards given to a petitioner as a water polo player did not reflect excellence in the water 
polo coaching field); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(2) ("Competitive athletics and 
coaching rely on different sets of skills and in general are not in the same area of expertise.") The 
Petitioner asserted that the ballroom dancing community does not distinguish between coaches and 
athletes, and that choreography "is an extension of the same creative and technical skillset developed 
through years of elite performance." But he did not substantiate his assertions with independent, 
corroborating evidence. 
The Petitioner also submitted evidence of his membership in an international choreographers' union 
and, in his capacity as a dance judge, a Russian dance federation. But he did not establish that these 
memberships required outstanding achievements or otherwise garnered him acclaim or recognition. 
See Donskoy, 2021 WL 5240224, at *6 (affirming USCIS' decision that a petitioner's membership in 
an association did not "demonstrate the requisite level ofachievement or sustained acclaim to establish 
extraordinary ability").

As previously indicated, two Russian newspapers each published online news interviews with the 
Petitioner regarding the benefits of introducing children to ballroom dancing. These 2024 and 2025 
publications garnered him some national acclaim. But he has not demonstrated that two newspaper 
articles reflect someone among the small percentage at his field's top. See Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 
119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (holding that a "few" 
publications about a petitioner's accomplishments "is not consistent with national or international 
acclaim"). 
The Petitioner also has judged others' work in his field and has had his choreographic works displayed 
at artistic exhibitions and showcases in Russia. But he did not establish that these activities generated 
significant amounts of acclaim or recognition for him. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he wrote a manual used to teach young Russian dance students 
and developed a curriculum for a network of dance schools across the country. The record also shows 
that he performed in a critical role for the dance schools. But the record still lacks independent 
evidence placing him among the small percentage at his field's top. Letters on the Petitioner's behalf 
are from those who personally know him, such as former employers, former co-workers, former 
coaches, and dance federation officials. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 
WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (noting that the "plaintiff submitted letters only from 
people within his circle of professional acquaintances, and none of the letters point to evidence of 
'extensive documentation' of plaintiff's accomplishments to support his assertion that he has enjoyed 
sustained national or international acclaim"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("It is 
generally expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international 
acclaim would have received recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the circle of their 
personal and professional acquaintances.") 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). The legislature indicated 
that this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the 
very top of their field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner has met the minimum number of evidentiary 
criteria and has demonstrated his receipt of some acclaim and recognition in his field. But, considering 
the evidence as a whole, he has not established his claimed place among the small percentage at his 
field's very top. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary ability 
classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a petitioner] 
was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (holding that 
even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not automatically meet the extraordinary 
ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner satisfied the required amount of evidentiary criteria. But he did not establish sustained 
national or international acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage 
at his field's top. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial.
