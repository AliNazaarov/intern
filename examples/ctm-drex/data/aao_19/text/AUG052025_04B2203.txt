See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish

appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review,

I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a tattoo artist with over twenty years of experience working in Russia and the United 
States. The Petitioner claimed to qualify based on his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award and, in the alternative, by meeting six ofthe alternate initial evidentiary criteria relating to lesser 
awards, published material, judging, contributions, display, and high salary at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii), (iv), (v), (vii), and (ix). The Petitioner also claimed the criteria relating to 
membership in associations and commercial success at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and (x) did not apply 
to his occupation and submitted comparable evidence of his brand sponsorships and high 
compensation. SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not establish that the criteria relating to 
membership and commercial success did not apply to his profession. SCOPS concluded the Petitioner 
met the criteria relating to judging and display, but not the remaining four criteria he claimed to meet. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts SCOPS misapplied the regulatory standards and failed to assess the 
documentation under the preponderance of the evidence standard. 
A. One-Time Achievement 
A petitioner may satisfy the initial evidence requirement through evidence of a one-time achievement, 
"that is, a major, international[ly] recognized award." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Neither the Act nor the 
relevant regulations define a "major, internationally recognized" award or differentiate between such 
an award and a "lesser" award under the first, alternate evidentiary criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). However, given Congress' intent to restrict this category to "that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor," the regulation permitting 
eligibility based on a one-time achievement must be interpreted very narrowly, with only a small 
handful of awards qualifying as major, internationally recognized awards. See H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at *6739. The House Report 
specifically cited the Nobel Prize as an example of a one-time achievement. Other examples which 
enjoy major, international recognition may include the Pulitzer Prize, the Academy Award, and an 
Olympic Medal. Further, we must look to Congress' intent that "admission under this category is to 
be reserved for that small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of 
endeavor." Id. Thus, consistent with legislative history, a one-time achievement must be interpreted 
very narrowly, with only a small handful of awards qualifying as major, internationally recognized 
awards.

nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor."

or international significance of the awards or prizes in the field, the number of awardees or prize

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in

High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Comparative wage 
or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location should be submitted to 
establish that a petitioner's compensation is high relative to others working in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner claims to meet this criterion through his high compensation and rates of $2,000 a day 
or $333 an hour. The Petitioner's 2023 federal income tax return shows his wages or self-employment 
earnings were $160,200 and his gross receipts or sales were $130,872. The Petitioner also submitted 
bank statements showing he received numerous payments for tattoo deposits, work, and appointments 
for between $300 to $1,700. In addition, the Petitioner submitted: evidence from the Occupational 
Outlook Handbook that the median pay for craft and fine artists was $25.44 an hour or $52,910 a year; 
a 2022 Tattoo Smart Tattooist Hourly Rate Survey stating the average hourly rate in California where 
the Petitioner now works as $176.70 and as $106.67 in Russia where he used to work; a printout from 
Indeed stating the average salary of a tattoo artist in Los Angeles, California is $57,678: a printout 
from the Economic Research Institute stating the average salary of a tattoo artist inI I 
California is $41,747: and a printout from Salary .com stating the 90th percentile of salaries for tattoo 
artists inl ICalifornia is $88,131. The preponderance of this evidence shows that the 
Petitioner has commanded a high salary in relation to other tattoo artists and he meets this criterion.

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has established that he meets the evidentiary criteria relating to published material, 
judging, original contributions, display, and high salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (iv), (v), (vii) 
and (ix). As the Petitioner submitted the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether he has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, that 
he is among the small percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor, and that his achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, 
we analyze petitioners' accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims a final merits determination shows he has extraordinary ability 
through consideration of his awards, media presence and publicity, judging and peer recognition, 
professional endorsements and sponsorships, and his significantly high remuneration. We have 
considered all of this evidence in our assessment.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing towards the top. The Petitioner has 
not shown that the significance and recognition of his work are indicative of sustained national or 
international acclaim as one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of his field, as 
extraordinary ability is described at section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
Petitioner is consequently ineligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
