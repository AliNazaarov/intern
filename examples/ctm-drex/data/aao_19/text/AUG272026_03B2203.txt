Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner1 seeks the employment-based, first-preference immigrant visa category (EB-1) for the 
Beneficiary, who is a classical music composer, as an alien with "extraordinary ability." 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
EB-I classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Beneficiary had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the Beneficiary is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that the beneficiary: 
• Has "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• Seeks to continue work in his or her field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
1 The Petitioner appears to be an attorney at the law firm that has assisted the Beneficiary in filing this petition. 
can demonstrate international recognition ofthe beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
As the Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). USCIS concluded that the Beneficiary met three criteria: 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (receipt of 
lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards), 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) (participation as a 
judge of other's work) and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) (display of work at artistic showcases). 
Upon review of the record, we will not disturb USCIS' determination that the Petitioner has satisfied 
at least three initial regulatory criteria. The record contains evidence that the Beneficiary received 
awards by participating in music composition competitions, judged other's work in selecting new 
music to be played by his chamber music group, and displayed his music composition at recitals and 
concerts. However, in reviewing the totality of the evidence in the context of the final merits 
determination, 2 we conclude that the Petitioner has not shown the Beneficiary garnered sustained 
national or international acclaim and his achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, 
demonstrating he is one ofthat small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). 3 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that: 1) USCIS erred in concluding that the Beneficiary did not meet 
three additional criteria (membership, published materials, and leading or critical role); 2) the final 
merits determination is "unlawful, arbitrary, and capricious, and requires reversal under Mukherji v. 
Miller"; and 3) USCIS failed to articulate specific or objective standards for showing sustained 
acclaim and being at the very top of the field and imposed extra-regulatory requirements. We will 
address each of the Petitioner's claims in the order we list here.

First, as we conclude that the Beneficiary has met the required three initial criteria 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iv), and (vii), we need not address the Petitioner's appellate claims that the 
Beneficiary also meets three other criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), and (viii). See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Secondly, we acknowledge that the recent U.S. District Court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), and its finding that USCIS' adoption of the 
two-tiered system under Kazarian is not valid because it was implemented without the proper notice 
and comment rulemaking process. However, the Mukherji decision issued by a district court in 
Nebraska is not a binding precedential authority in the present case which arises out of New York. 
See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993) (in contrast to the precedential authority of the case 
law of a U.S. circuit court, the Board of Immigration Appeals is not bound to follow a decision of a 
U.S. district court). Therefore, we decline to address the substantive issues in Mukherji until it is 
properly before us. Instead, we proceed to the second step of the Kazarian analysis and focus on the 
Petitioner's specific contentions that USCIS erred in applying the final merits determination. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine ifhis successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability 
in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2),(3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. The commentary for the proposed regulations implementing section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high standard be set for 
aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more 
extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991 ). Consequently, courts have applied this high standard in interpreting "extraordinary 
ability." See, e.g., Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) (upholding agency's 
decision to deny EB-1 classification to a competitive ballroom dancer who had won world junior 
championship); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 915, 918 (N.D. Ill. 2002) (upholding agency's 
decision to deny EB-1 visa to "arguably one ofthe most famous baseball players in Korean history"); 
Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 1115, 1117, 1122 (9th Cir. 2010) (upholding 
agency's decision to deny EB-1 classification to Ph.D.-holding theoretical physicist working with 
researchers at Caltech). Therefore, the Petitioner's claim that the standards of the initial criteria alone 
govern whether the Beneficiary is an individual of extraordinary ability is not persuasive. 
The Petitioner's appellate claims do not acknowledge other persuasive case law that have consistently 
imposed high standards in adjudicating this extraordinary ability visa. The Petitioner asserts that the 
evidence satisfies the plain language of the initial criteria and that USCIS introduced ultra vires 
requirements by its "demand for comparison" to those at the top of the field. However, courts have 
held that comparison to others in the field to demonstrate one's extraordinary ability is proper. 
See e.g., Nakos v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 3:25-CV-0985-D, 2026 WL 2042845, at *8 
(N.D. Tex. July 15, 2026) (finding USCIS did not impose a novel evidentiary requirement when it 
stated that the record lacked sufficient information about others in the field and his place in the field 
relative to them); Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) 
(upholding the final merits determination in which the petitioner did not show how his publications 
compared to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field).

The evidence overall depicts a promising young composer, but the record as a whole does not reflect 
recognition of an individual who has reached the top of his field with sustained acclaim and does not 
establish the Beneficiary's eligibility for the benefit sought. 7 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly 
restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top oftheir respective fields, rather 
than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994); 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of

consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished than the Beneficiary to qualify 
for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that the Beneficiary is among the 
small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to 
establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
