The Petitioner, a talent management business, seeks to classify the Beneficiary, a fashion designer, as a 
foreign national of extraordinary ability in the arts. To do so, the Petitioner seeks 0-1 nonimmigrant 
classification, available to foreign nationals who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 101(a)(15)(O)(i), 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(O)(i). 
The Director of the California Service Center denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did 
not provide evidence sufficient to support a finding that it would act as an agent performing the function 
of an employer pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(l), and to explain the terms of the proposed 
employment and the nature of the activities and events in which the Beneficiary will participate 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(ii). 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that it submitted sufficient explanation as to why it should be considered 
an agent performing the function of an employer, and of the terms of the proffered employment and 
the nature of the Beneficiary's proposed activities and events. 1 The Petitioner submits a brief and 
evidence previously submitted into the record. 
In these proceedings, it is the Petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the requested benefit. 
Section 291 of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1361. Upon de nova review, we will dismiss the appeal.

specific events as detailed on the initial petition." 59 Fed. Reg. 41818, 41828 (Aug. 15, 1994). Here, 
the Petitioner has not detailed sufficiently what work, over what period of time, and where the Beneficiary 
will perform her proposed events or activities.

In addition, we agree that the Petitioner has not established that it is "performing the function of an 
employer" within the meaning of 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(1). The 0-1 nonimmigrant visa 
regulations distinguish between a petition filed by an "employer" and an "agent performing the 
function of an employer." Pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E), an agent performing the function 
of an employer may file a petition in cases involving workers who are traditionally self-employed. 
The 0-1 regulations require a petitioner claiming to be acting as such an agent to provide the 
contractual agreement that specifies the wage offered and the other terms and conditions of 
employment of the beneficiary. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(1). This requirement recognizes that the 
terms and conditions of the contractual agreement are critical to making a case-by-case factual 
determination as to whether an agent is functioning as an employer for 0-1 purposes. 
The present contract did not include such critical elements as guaranteed pay, exclusivity, and a high 
degree of control over the Beneficiary's performance of her duties, that would indicate the Petitioner 
exercises a degree of control similar to that exercised by an employer. As noted previously, although 
the employment agreement indicates that the Petitioner would pay the Beneficiary a monthly wage of 
$3,950, in its initial letter counsel for the Petitioner asserted that the Beneficiary "will be compensated 
on a per project basis." It is incumbent upon the Petitioner to resolve any inconsistencies in the record 
by independent objective evidence. Any attempt to explain or reconcile such inconsistencies will not 
suffice unless the Petitioner submits competent objective evidence pointing to where the truth lies. 
Matter of Ho, 19 l&N Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988). The record also does not indicate that the terms 
and conditions of the Beneficiary's fashion design assignments are primarily determined by the 
Petitioner, and not by the Beneficiary. We note that the Petitioner did not submit evidence to establish 
that it had actually arranged any of the fashion shows or other events I isted on the Employment Agreement 
and Itinerary and the updated itinerary. Regarding two of proposed events, the record shows that the 
Beneficiary has an existing relationship with thel land previously promoted her work 
through! tsl lprogram, and the record does not indicate that the Beneficiary has 
historically utilized an agent to market and promote her work. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the Director erred in denying the petition on this basis without 
including in the RFE a request for evidence that the Petitioner "will exercise significant control of [the 
Beneficiary's] career." However, within its RFE response the Petitioner did address this issue, asserting 
that it "will have significant control of [the Beneficiary's] U.S. employment, as it will directly make 
payments to [the Beneficiary] for the projects listed on the itinerary of events in the employment 
agreement. ... " However, as discussed previously, the Director concluded that the Petitioner's contract 
did not include critical elements to support a finding that the Petitioner exercises a degree of control 
similar to that exercised by an employer, such as exclusivity and a high degree of control over the 
Beneficiary's performance of her duties. Moreover, the most efficient remedy for any alleged error in 
failing to request specific additional evidence would be to consider any additional evidence that might 
have been submitted in response to such a request on appeal. In the matter before us, the Petitioner 
submits no further evidence relating to the Director's concerns. 
Finally, we note that the Petitioner has not been consistent in its explanation of its role in relation to the 
Beneficiary. As discussed previously, the initial evidence suggested that the Petitioner would be the 
5 
Beneficiary's direct employer, as the Petitioner indicated in the employment agreement that it would pay 
the Beneficiary a specific wage for work that it had scheduled that would take place on its premises. 
Within its RFE response, the Petitioner stated that it can be categorized as "a U.S. agent performing the 
function of an employer" for the purposes of this visa petition, similar to what is described at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(1), because it "will have significant control of [the Beneficiary's] U.S. employment, 
as it will directly make payments to [the Beneficiary] for the projects listed on the itinerary of events in 
the employment agreement." Again, it is incumbent upon the Petitioner to resolve any inconsistencies 
in the record by independent objective evidence. Any attempt to explain or reconcile such 
inconsistencies will not suffice unless the Petitioner submits competent objective evidence pointing to 
where the truth lies. Matter of Ho, 19 l&N Dec. at 591-92. Due to the discrepancies, the record does 
not contain evidence sufficient to support a finding that the Petitioner would act as an agent under the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(1). 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence on appeal to overcome the Director's finding that the Petitioner 
did not provide evidence sufficient to support a finding that it would act as an agent performing the 
function of an employer pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 214.2(o)(2)(iv)(E)(1) and to meet the requirements at 
8 C.F.R. 214.2(o)(2)(ii). Consequently, the Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary is eligible 
for the 0-1 visa classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for 
the above stated reasons, with each considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
