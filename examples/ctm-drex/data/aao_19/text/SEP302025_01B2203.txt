Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a journalist and editor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability under 
section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Service Center Operations (SCOPS) 
denied the petition, concluding that the evidence did not satisfy at least three of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We dismissed a subsequent appeal and this matter 
is now before us on a motion to reconsider. Upon review, we will dismiss the motion. 
A motion to reconsider must show that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of law 
or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record ofproceedings at the 
time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior 
decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). 
We may grant motions that meet these requirements and establish eligibility for the requested benefit. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of establishing her eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
The Petitioner claimed to have met nine of the ten regulatory criteria before SCOPS: receipt a lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in her field; membership in associations 
in the field requiring members' outstanding achievements, as judged by recognized experts; published 
material about herself; judging others' work; original contributions of major significance in the field; 
display of her work at artistic exhibitions or showcases; leading or critical role for distinguished 
organizations; a high salary or remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field; and 
commercial successes in the performing arts. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(v), (vii)-(x). SCOPS 
concluded that the record satisfied only two criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) (participation as a 
judge of the work of others) and (viii) (leading or critical role for distinguished organizations), and 
denied the case as the record did not satisfy at least three of the nine regulatory criteria she claimed. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claimed and provided arguments for only four of the nine regulatory criteria 
she previously claimed below, comprising 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) (i), (iv), (viii) and (ix), and we agreed 
with SCOPS she has met two ofthese four criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) (judging others' work) 
and (viii) (leading or critical role). We further determined and explained why the evidence did not 
satisfy the two remaining criteria the Petitioner asserted on appeal at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (receipt 
of a lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in her field) and (ix) (a high 
salary for services, in relation to others in the field). 1 
With respect to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (lesser recognized awards for excellence in the field), we 
determined that although she submitted evidence of a 2015 I IAward 
from 'I land letters of appreciation from the I Ifor the I ID award, her assertions and the submitted documents were unclear as to whether she received one 
award or two distinct awards. Even if they were two different awards, we farther determined that the 
record lacked objective evidence of their claimed national or international renown, contrary to her 
claim. W~onsidered the various support letters attesting to her receipt of the 2015 I I 
award andl__Jletters of appreciation, but determined that the support letters lacked probative detail 
as to their claimed widespread reputation and acclaim, and the record did not otherwise contain 
independent evidence of their national or international recognition as regulation requires. 
Additionally, we noted that two support letters, purportedly written by two differentOofficials 
five years apart in 2019 and 2024, were nearly identical, calling into question their evidentiary weight. 
We also explained why the evidence of record did not demonstrate that she has "commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field" as set 
forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). We specifically determined that her comparative salary data for 
"journalists" and "senior journalists" in Nepal ( consisting of former employer letters and letters from 
the Minimum Wage Fixation Committee) did not closely relate to the occupation in which she received 
her earnings as a "senior global editor." Although she also provided evidence of a job offer with a 
$60,000 annual salary in the United States as a "consultant editor," the record lacked objective 
comparative salary data that may help determine whether the offered salary is high compared to other 
consultant editors in this country. We thus determined that the record did not show a high salary or 
other remuneration for services in relation to others in the field, which is interpreted as permitting 
comparisons to those employed in the same occupation at a similar level. See, e.g., Berardo v. USCIS, 
No. 3:19-CV-01796-SB, 2020 WL 6161459, at *5 (D. Or. Oct. 20, 2020) ("[T]he appropriate test 
compares the petitioner's salary with the average salary of others in the same position in the field."); 
see also Skokos v. US. Dept. ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding 
salary information of those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field). 
Because the above two evidentiary criteria have not been met, and the record thus did not satisfy at 
least three of the four regulatory criteria she claimed on appeal, we could not conclude she is an alien 
of extraordinary ability and dismissed the appeal without farther analysis of the merits under the Act. 
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119 (9th Cir. 2010); see also Nagaiah v. Allen, F.Supp.3d, 
2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, 
not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").
