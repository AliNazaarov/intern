In Re: 43930344 Date: AUG. 24, 2026 
Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a machine learning engineer who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
After earning his foreign Master of Computer Science degree, the Petitioner has worked in various 
roles and as a machine learning engineer since 2019. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met five of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to membership and performing 
in a leading or critical role but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes or awards, 
membership, original contributions, or a high salary or remuneration. On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains that he meets the same criteria. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude 
he has not demonstrated eligibility here. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that he is a member of an association in his field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 
2780815, at *3--4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
Relevant factors that may lead to a conclusion that the person's membership in the association(s) was 
not based on outstanding achievements in the field include, but are not limited to, instances where the 
person's membership was based solely on the following factors (by themselves or in the aggregate): a 
level of education or years of experience in a particular field; the payment of a fee or by subscribing 
2 
to an association's publications; and a requirement, compulsory or otherwise, for employment in 
certain occupations, as commonly seen with union membership or guild affiliation for actors. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner argued that his "senior membership" with the Institute of Electrical and Electronics 
Engineers (IEEE) fulfilled this criterion. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the requirements 
ofthis criterion, but we do not agree that the Petitioner has shown this level ofmembership is adequate 
here. The Petitioner submitted his IEEE membership certificate, and three partial printouts from the 
IEEE website about senior level membership requirements. The first printout reflects four 
requirements for membership: 
• Be engineers, scientists, educators, technical executives, or originators in IEEE-designated 
fields; 
• Have experience reflecting professional maturity; 
• Have been in professional practice for at least 10 years (with some credit for certain degrees); 
and 
• Show significant performance over a period of at least five of their years in professional 
practice. 
In the second partial printout, the Petitioner highlighted the portion illustrating that "candidates for 
Senior Member grade must supply three references from current IEEE members holding the grade of 
Fellow, Senior Member, or Honorary Member." And in the third partial printout, the Petitioner 
highlighted the statement that only eight percent ofIEEE' s members hold the grade of senior member, 
which according to their own figures amounts to more than 34,000 members. 
None of these materials demonstrate that this association requires outstanding achievements as a 
condition for this level of membership, and the Petitioner did not offer the organization's bylaws that 
might have provided additional information. Having professional maturity and being in practice for 
at least 10 years fall far short of that standard. And the Petitioner did not offer evidence of what IEEE 
considers "significant performance" that a candidate must demonstrate for at least five years of their 
professional practice. 
The requirements for the "Senior Member Grade" on the IEEE website-the portion of the website 
printout that the Petitioner cut off and failed to offer for the record-further specify that "significant 
performance" does not require "special awards, patents, or other extremely sophisticated technical 
accomplishments," but can include "substantial job responsibilities such as team leader, task 
supervisor, engineer in charge of a program or project, engineer or scientist performing research with 
some measure of success (papers), or faculty developing and teaching courses with research and 
publications." Requirements for IEEE Senior Member Grade, IEEE Advancing Technology for 
Humanity (Aug. 21, 2026), https://www.ieee.org/membership/senior/senior-requirements. 
The record, therefore, does not demonstrate that the IEEE's standards for the senior grade membership 
rise to the level of "outstanding achievements" as contemplated by the plain language of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). While this membership level does not appear to be available to all professionals 
with 10 years of experience in the field, the Petitioner must establish that IEEE requires senior 
members to have "outstanding achievements" as an essential condition of membership to satisfy this 
3 
criterion. Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 845 (C.D. Cal. 2022) (determining that the 
record did not include evidence that the claimed associations required outstanding achievements as a 
condition of membership). 
In addition, the Petitioner did not establish that recognized national or international experts judge the 
outstanding achievements for senior membership within IEEE as required by 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). The partial printout about supplying three references does not meet this requirement. 
Because the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of this 
criterion, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination. 
2. "Documentation of the alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: ( l) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner provided a team award that named him as one ofits team members. SCOPS determined 
that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. While we do not agree with SCOPS 
that the Petitioner is not sufficiently identified as one of the award's recipients, we do agree that the 
record lacks adequate evidence that it was national or internationally recognized for excellence in the 
field. Before SCOPS the Petitioner relied on material about the methodology the team used during 
the competition, how the competition was structured, later reliance on the methods, and the fact that 
I !sponsored the conference when his team received the recognition. 
On appeal, the Petitioner identifies some of the same evidence, claims SCOPS' denial did not 
substantively analyze the material, and failed to engage with it. But what remains lacking is his 
explanation of how this form of recognition was nationally or internationally recognized. 
National and international recognition results, not from the entity that issued the prize or award, nor 
from the issuing event's sponsors, but through the awareness of the accolade in the eyes of the field 
nationally or internationally. Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (finding 
"the focus is on the award, not the awarding organization"); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 
3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013). Agency policy addresses the requisite level of a prize's or award's 
recognition noting we consider the "national or international significance of the awards or prizes in 
the field" and states "national or international recognition is most often associated with awards given 
to individuals at the highest level in a given field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l). See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 
2021) (upholding the determination that awards must be nationally or internationally recognized). 
A national or international level competition may issue lesser awards that also receive lesser 
recognition, which do not meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. The requisite 
recognition should be evident through specific means; for example but not limited to, national or 
international-level media coverage. Courts have recognized other factors when considering whether 
4 
an award was nationally or internationally recognized such as the number of entrants who competed 
in the same category or their level of experience. Nakos v. USCIS, No. 3:25-CV-0985-D, 2026 WL 
2042845, at *4 (N.D. Tex. July 15, 2026). The fact that the Petitioner's team recognition occurred in 
connection with a reputable conference establishes the institutional setting in which the challenge 
occurred, but it does not demonstrate that when the team won the challenge, that this constituted 
winning a conference award or that the award itself possessed the same level of recognition as the 
conference. 
Additionally, unsupported conclusory letters from those in the Petitioner's field are not sufficient 
evidence that a particular prize or award is nationally or internationally recognized. Probative 
evidence that an award is nationally or internationally recognized is generally material from sources 
besides the issuing entity. Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021). 
It remains that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements 
of this criterion. 
3. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner to submit 
evidence of a "high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others 
in the field." That means a petitioner must provide comparative evidence for those performing similar 
work, during the same approximate time and geographic location, within the field. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). And the presentation of only average salaries in the field is 
generally inadequate to demonstrate a petitioner's earnings are high, or their other types of 
remuneration are significantly high, in relation to the rest of the field. 
The Petitioner claimed his remuneration from his current employer and he asserted his annual 
remuneration consisted of his base salary and restricted stock units. His evidence consisted of his 
2025 tax documents, government-based wage data, and to compare with his own remuneration, public 
crowdsourced job boards and data aggregators. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet 
the requirements of this criterion. 
We note the Petitioner did not submit evidence from his employer verifying the breakdown of his 
wages, tips, and other compensation to corroborate his assertions relating to his base salary and 
restricted stock units comprising his remuneration. So at the outset he has failed to demonstrate what 
made up the figure on his tax documentation and this has an adverse effect on the value of his claims 
under this criterion. However, his initial supporting evidence in which to compare with his own 
reflected only base salaries and bonuses, and did not include restricted stock units or other 
documentation consistent with his claimed compensation structure. 
In the request for evidence (RFE) response, the Petitioner claimed that some of the salary survey 
evidence he initially provided included restricted stock units in the total pay figures, but he did not 
corroborate that with any evidence and we were unable to verify those claims. Unsupported assertions 
have little evidentiary value and are insufficient to establish a filing party has satisfied their burden of 
proof; such assertions do not even make a prima facie eligibility showing. See Matter of G-C-1-, 29 
5 
I&N Dec. 176, 178 (BIA 2025). Also accompanying the RFE response, the Petitioner provided 
material from levels.fyi, but that evidence related to a "Software Engineer" and should have instead 
been for a "Machine Leaming Engineer." We reviewed the data for both a "Software Engineer" and 
a "Machine Learning Engineer" on that website and the "Machine Learning Engineer" demands 
considerably higher remuneration. Additionally, the Petitioner's earnings data was from a different 
year than much of the comparative evidence he submitted, limiting its value as a direct comparison. 
The Petitioner further notes "the regulation requires evidence demonstrating a high salary or other 
significantly high remuneration 'in relation to others in the field.' It does not require mathematically 
perfect subgroup matching ...." Again we reiterate the levels.fyi evidence was for software engineers 
when they have a category for machine learning engineers, and within the initial filing the Petitioner 
included a separate section titled "My Field of Endeavor" in which he stated in the opening paragraph 
that he is "an established machine learning engineer." 
Finally, the Petitioner notes that agency policy references various U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) 
resources that may be helpful when evaluating this criterion. However, the DOL resources he 
submitted relate to the software developer occupation, not to a more appropriate comparator 
occupation, such as data scientists or computer and information research scientists. Further, although 
he asserts that SCOPS did not meaningfully address this evidence, the DOL data has limited relevance 
because it reflects only base salary information and does not include bonus structures, restricted stock 
units, or other forms of compensation relevant to his claimed remuneration. 
As a result, they do not provide an adequate basis for comparison with the compensation structure he 
claims. Comparing an alien's total compensation-which includes various bonuses and stock 
options-against even the upper percentiles of other base salaries is comparing apples to oranges. It 
artificially conflates guaranteed earnings with highly variable conditions that lean higher, thus creating 
an illogical, inflated, and fundamentally invalid benchmark of earnings. To make a legally sound 
comparison, one must measure like with like. Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 
5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 
2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding a petitioner must show that salary reports reflect an 
apples to apples, rather than an apples to oranges). 
Here, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. 
B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner satisfies the leading or critical role criterion, he does not 
meet any of the remaining claimed criteria discussed above. While he argues and submits evidence 
for one additional criterion on appeal relating to contributions of major significance at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v), it is unnecessary that we make a decision on this additional ground because he cannot 
numerically meet the required number ofcriteria. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary 
requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the remaining issue. Patel v. 
Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
6 
ultimate decision)); see also Matter of Delcarmen-Lara, 29 I&N Dec. 830, 837 n.2 (BIA 2026) 
( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
ITT. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act. In 
creating the "extraordinary ability" visa, Congress hoped to "draw the world's best and brightest to 
American shores" by giving "preferential treatment to highly talented immigrants." Branski v. USC IS, 
No. 25-CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at *11 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) (quoting Amin v. Mayorkas, 
24 F.4th 383, 386 (5th Cir. 2022)). Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial 
evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of 
extraordinary ability and it is unnecessary that we provide farther analysis of the merits under the 
statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798,814 (E.D. 
Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 
28, 2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
That view has been directly rejected by numerous federal courts before and since the Mukherji court 
made it. Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. Patel, 596 U.S. at 332. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
