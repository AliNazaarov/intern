Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is an entrepreneur who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant 
classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner owns a brokerage business and has work experience in the areas of mergers and 
acquisitions and financial services. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met seven of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to prizes or awards and 
performing in a leading or critical role but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with 
membership, published material, original contributions, the authorship of scholarly articles, or a high 
salary or remuneration. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he meets the same evidentiary criteria. 
After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we do not agree with him. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: ( l) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner claimed four awards under this criterion. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met 
the requirements here, although they made that determination without any analysis and we do not 
agree with that favorable outcome. 
USCIS determines whether a prize or award is a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize 
or award for excellence in the person's field of endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. In making this determination, USCIS focuses on the 
recognition of the particular prize or award, rather than merely on the reputation of the issuing 
2 
organization, the prominence of the individual who signed the award, or the reputation of the 
competition or institution associated with it. 
National or international recognition does not automatically transfer from a well-known competition, 
institution, organization, or individual to every prize, certificate, or honor issued under its name. See 
Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (explaining that "the focus is on the 
award, not the awarding organization" and finding that evidence concerning a festival did not establish 
the recognition of the particular award); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 
2013). Instead, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular accolade is recognized nationally or 
internationally as an award for excellence in the field. So, an entity or event may be nationally or 
internationally known while particular accolades it issues are not themselves nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence. 
The same principle applies when a competition includes the word "National" in its title or otherwise 
has a national or international profile. The national or international scope or reputation of the 
competition does not, by itself, establish that every prize or award issued in connection with the 
competition is nationally or internationally recognized. A competition may issue different awards that 
vary substantially in their level of selectivity, prominence, and recognition. Some may receive 
national or international recognition, while others may receive only local, regional, internal, or 
otherwise limited recognition. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 
(N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) (upholding the determination that awards must be nationally or 
internationally recognized). 
USCIS policy reinforces the requirement that the focus is on the award and not the issuer ( explaining 
that the agency considers the "national or international significance of the awards or prizes in the 
field") and it addresses the requisite level of recognition noting that "national or international 
recognition is most often associated with awards given to individuals at the highest level in a given 
field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). The Policy Manual further provides 
that examples of qualifying awards may include, but are not limited to, "[ c ]ertain awards from 
well-known national institutions or well-known professional associations." Id. The qualifiers 
"certain" and "well-known" reinforce that the focus is on the prize or award and that the inquiry is 
case-specific. The fact that an award is issued by a well-known national institution, professional 
association, competition, or event may be relevant, but it does not by itself establish that the particular 
award is nationally or internationally recognized for excellence. 
In some circumstances, receipt of a top prize or honor from a nationally or internationally recognized 
competition or institution may provide evidence that the particular award is nationally or 
internationally recognized. A petitioner, however, must submit evidence establishing the significance 
and recognition of that specific award, rather than relying solely on the existence or reputation of the 
compettt10n, institution, or issuing organization. Relevant evidence may include national or 
international media coverage of the particular award, material demonstrating that the award is widely 
known or regarded as prestigious within the field, or other independent evidence demonstrating that 
the accolade carries national or international recognition for excellence in the field. The weight of 
such evidence depends on its specificity and probative value. 
3 
Conversely, unsupported or conclusory statements from individuals in the petitioner's field ordinarily 
are insufficient to establish that a particular prize or award is nationally or internationally recognized. 
Evidence from sources independent of the issuing entity may be particularly probative because it can 
demonstrate how the particular award is viewed beyond the organization that conferred it. See 
Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 183. Evidence merely establishing that the issuing organization, 
competition, event, or institution is nationally or internationally known does not necessarily establish 
that the particular prize or award itself enjoys comparable recognition.

2. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations 
or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
4 
qualifying role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization 
or establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). Evidence of a leading role should show that the alien was a leader for the 
claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help to establish that a role was leading. 
A leading role should be apparent by its position in the overall hierarchy and the role's matching 
duties. Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *7 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 
2025). 
For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l ). In determining whether the organization, establishment, department, or division has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. Id. Organizations, establishments, divisions, or departments that enjoy a 
distinguished reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Id. The Petitioner 
must submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024). 
The Petitioner offered three claims under this criterion and SCOPS decided he met the requirements. 
Below we explain why we are withdrawing that favorable determination. 
His first claim is based on his own business. We begin noting that the record lacks an explanation of 
how his particular company has a distinguished reputation versus the reputation ofthe larger franchise 
entity. He provided promotional materials relating to the business, corporate documents, and other 
material that does not delineate between the larger corporate franchise and the Petitioner's particular 
business. As it relates to the role the Petitioner performed, ifhis intention was to indicate he performed 
in a leading or critical role for the broader franchise, while he provided a letter from the franchise's 
chief executive officer, that letter simply confirmed his performance as a business broker when 
compared to the network of franchise businesses. The letter's author did not describe how the 
Petitioner's role was leading or critical for the overall franchise.

the relevant entity is generally inadequate to meet his burden of proof. Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 
21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *5 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 21, 2023) (finding self-promotional

3. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner to submit 
evidence of a "high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others 
in the field." That means a petitioner must provide comparative evidence for those performing similar 
work, during the same approximate time and geographic location, within the field. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l ). And the presentation of only average salaries in the field is 
generally inadequate to demonstrate a petitioner's earnings are high, or their other types of 
remuneration are significantly high, in relation to the rest of the field. 
The Petitioner provided evidence of his earnings as an owner of a business brokerage firm and 
comparative evidence relating to business brokers as employees. SCOPS determined that the 
Petitioner did not meet the requirements ofthis criterion. Part of SCOPS' determination reflected that 
for the Petitioner to demonstrate his remuneration was high in relation to others in the field, he must 
provide evidence comparing his remuneration to his peers in specific positions. 
We note that the Petitioner's comparison here is not apt because he submitted evidence of his earnings 
as an owner of a business brokerage firm, but compares those earnings to compensation data for 
business brokers working as employees. Earnings derived from business ownership may reflect 
factors other than compensation for personal services. Therefore, employee wage data for business 
brokers does not provide a reliable basis to determine whether the Petitioner has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services in relation to others in the field. This 
artificially conflates guaranteed earnings with highly variable conditions that lean higher, thus creating 
an illogical, inflated, and fundamentally invalid benchmark of earnings. To make a legally sound 
comparison, one must measure like with like. Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 
5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 
2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding a petitioner must show that salary reports reflect an 
apples to apples, rather than an apples to oranges). 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements ofthis criterion. 
4. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that he is a member of an association in his field. Second, the Petitioner must

demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized

outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility

language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL

https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/outstanding. Dictionaries are not of themselves

5. "Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary elements the Petitioner must demonstrate. The first 
element is that the Petitioner is an author of scholarly articles in his field in which he intends to engage 
once admitted to the United States as a lawful permanent resident. 
We consider these articles to fall within two distinct areas. One area is within the academic arena in 
which a scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse. It 
is written by a researcher or expert in the field who is often affiliated with a college, university, or 
research institution. In general, it should have footnotes, endnotes, or a bibliography, and may include 
graphs, charts, videos, or pictures as illustrations of the concepts expressed in the article. The next 
area lies outside of the academic arena in which a scholarly article should be written for learned 
persons in that field. "Learned" is defined as "having or demonstrating profound knowledge or 
scholarship." Learned persons include all persons having profound knowledge of a field. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1 ). 
The second evidentiary element this criterion requires is that the scholarly articles appear in one of 
the following: (1) a professional publication, (2) a major trade publication, or (3) in a form of major 
media. Relevant factors a petitioner should demonstrate include the intended audience (for 
professional and major trade publications) and the relative circulation, readership, or viewership are 
high relative to similar publications (for major trade publications and other major media). See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1). The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying each of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
USCIS policy differentiates between scholarly articles in the academic arena and those in 
non-academic fields of endeavor. As the Petitioner's field is not within the academic arena, USCIS 
policy provides that this article should be written for learned persons in the field of business brokers, 
mergers and acquisitions, or financial services. The policy recognizes that "learned" is defined as 
"having or demonstrating profound knowledge or scholarship." As it relates to this case, learned 
persons include all those having profound knowledge in the field of business brokers. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1). 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. The Petitioner 
provided several informational or advisory publications consisting of one to two pages in length and 
he has not demonstrated that they are for learned persons in his field, as agency policy requires. 
Although several of the publications addressed legitimate business and mergers and acquisition 
subjects, the content was presented primarily as practical educational material for business owners, 
prospective buyers and sellers, and other members of the general business public. The articles 
explained basic concepts, provided practical advice, and frequently assumed little or no specialized 
background in business brokerage or mergers and acquisitions. 
For example, the material addressing SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportumttes, and threats) 
analysis explains the basic meaning and components of SWOT and then instructs prospective buyers 
and sellers of how to apply it to a mergers and acquisitions transaction. Similarly, the material 
concerning franchises, selling a business, timing a sale, and preparing a business for sale is framed as 
9 
guidance to individuals contemplating or participating in a transaction rather than as an exchange of 
specialized knowledge among persons who already possess profound knowledge of the field. So the 
fact that the subjects themselves may not be basic or simple does not establish that the articles were 
written for a learned audience. This goes directly against the Petitioner's claims that the "subjects 
remain objectively exclusive to a specialized audience." 
We conclude that the Petitioner's claims and evidence do not satisfy the plain language requirements 
of this criterion or the agency's policy on the matter of the scholarly nature of his evidence. 
B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner claims he meets two additional criteria, because his 
arguments fail on any single criterion discussed above, that means he cannot numerically meet the 
required number of at least three criteria and it is unnecessary for us to make a decision on these 
additional grounds. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve his additional claims or issues. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 
328, 332 (2022) ( citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); 
see also Matter ofW-F-D-, 29 I&N Dec. 854,858 n.5 (BIA 2026) (declining to reach alternative issues 
on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring a petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. "In creating the 'extraordinary ability' visa, Congress hoped to 'draw the world's best and brightest 
to American shores' by giving 'preferential treatment to highly talented immigrants."' Branski v. USC IS, 
No. 25-CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at *11 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) (quoting Amin, 24 F.4th at 386). 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and it is unnecessary that 
we provide farther analysis of the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F .3d at 1119-20; see 
also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification.
