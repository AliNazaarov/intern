Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a computer engineer who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 20 I 0) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner earned his Ph.D. in Electrical and Computer Engineering from a U.S. university in 2023 
and since then has been working as a computer engineer for a U.S. semiconductor company. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met three of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied the criteria relating to judging and the authorship of 
scholarly articles but that he had not satisfied the contributions criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains that he meets that criterion. After reviewing all the evidence, we conclude the record does 
not support the Petitioner's eligibility. 
1. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect 
and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or 
to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion and we agree 
with their ultimate determination. 
2 
The Petitioner alleges an error because SCOPS quoted from the initially offered support letters but not 
from those he provided in the request for evidence response. But that is not an error and SCOPS is 
not required to quote from any of the letters. The lack of recounting the precise language in a set of 
support letters is not an indication that those letters were not considered. It is not erroneous that a 
USCIS decision does not cite from each and every letter in support of a petition. Chen v. US. Dep 't 
ofJustice, 471 F.3d 315,338 n.17 (2d Cir. 2006)); Yan Lan Wu v. Ashcroft, 393 F.3d 418, 424-25 (3d 
Cir. 2005); Morales v. INS, 208 F.3d 323, 328 (1st Cir. 2000). Especially where SCOPS addressed 
the contents of any claimed letter, they do not have to directly discuss every one of them. Amin, 24 
F.4th at 394. 
Considering the Petitioner's citation record, he questions how SCOPS treated his record by deciding 
it individually did not show he met this criterion's requirements by itself as opposed how it fit into the 
overall totality of the evidence under the preponderance of the evidence standard. First, under step 
one (the regulatory criteria) the USCIS Policy Manual chapter covering this immigrant classification 
discusses "the preponderance of the evidence standard" of proof and it does not expressly permit a 
totality of the evidence analysis under any of the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
as opposed to mandating that methodology under the final merits determination. 
In the event that an alien's individual contributions are inadequate to satisfy the requirements, USCIS 
does not permit petitioners to cobble together the entirety of their life's work to aggregately qualify as 
a contribution in the field. The Petitioner cannot transform this step one criterion into a mini-final 
merits determination. But that appears to be what the Petitioner seeks to do here. Neither the 
regulation nor any other authority allows an alien to effectively establish points system in which his 
contribution may be added together to equal major significance. Instead, the regulatory question is 
whether the evidence establishes original contributions that are of major significance in the field. 
USCIS has repeatedly treated originality and major significance as substantive elements that must be 
demonstrated rather than as characteristics that can be accumulated through quantity. That is an 
important distinction because otherwise the term "major" loses much of its meaning. 1 Virtually any 
productive researcher could assemble a lengthy list of ordinary or modest contributions and claim that 
their aggregate quantity necessarily becomes "major." 
Second, the preponderance standard of proof is the degree, or level, of proof demanded in a specific 
case. We are ultimately deciding whether the petitioner's claims are sufficiently supported by relevant, 
probative, and credible evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376 reiterated the longstanding 
principle for the preponderance standard of proof. Here, the Petitioner failed to explain how SCOPS 
held his evidence to a higher than the appropriate standard of proof. 
And even if we set the above aside, in proposing this construct, the Petitioner doesn't expand on how 
the assemblage of his contributions to the field should be collectively weighed in favor of satisfying 
this criterion's major significance mandate. Because we find no error in SCOPS' analysis here, we are 
not persuaded by the Petitioner's allegation.

Then, the Petitioner turns to his claims of influence of his findings on other researchers' work. In his 
first example, in the paper's paragraph the Petitioner identified, his work is cited one time among 18 
other cites, and his work is only cited one time while some others were cited as many as three times. 
It is not apparent that the Petitioner's work was as he claims: "a critical benchmark in advancing 
circuit-integrated actuation and motion control for next-generation robots." 
As it relates to the second example, the Petitioner received two citations to his work in a closing 
paragraph that conveyed some newer ways of processing data. The paper mentioned that AI-based 
methods and brain-inspired computing have been used in other flexible electronic systems but have 
not yet been widely tested with a widely used semiconductor material for flexible electronics. The 
authors theorized that using those methods could help the technology work more reliably and make it 
easier to connect many sensors without needing as many wires or physical connections. Serving as a 
possibility at the end ofthe material does not corroborate the Petitioner's characterization that his cited 
work here "is highly influential and used as a foundation for their" article. Again, the two citations to 
his work were among over 100 citations and he has not shown how the article's reliance on his work 
was influential. 
As the final example, the Petitioner characterizes the single citation to his work as demonstrating that 
his "method provides an invaluable baseline for similar and adjacent research on the modeling and 
control of intelligent piezoelectric soft robots." The supporting citation provided: "Piezoelectric 
actuators are categorized into stack actuators and bending actuators, characterized by the diversity of 
layers forming the actuator. The multilayers can either be double mounted or single-ended as a 
cantilever" and it cited to the Petitioner's paper and another paper primarily to support the general 
proposition that piezoelectric actuators-devices that tum electrical energy directly into mechanical 
movements or physical force-are used in soft robotic systems, but not to establish a foundational 
control method or a field-wide baseline. The article cites the Petitioner's work in the introductory 
discussion immediately after explaining how the actuators can be set up in different ways. Then, it 
uses the Petitioner's work to show that layers can be mounted in two ways: mounted on both sides, 
or held on just one side like a shelf. The Petitioner has not established that this citation to his work 
warrants substantial evidentiary value in these proceedings. 
Next in the appeal, the Petitioner highlights three support letters and he asserts the authors benefitted 
from his research. We can summarize them quickly as they simply indicate they placed some general 
reliance on his research findings within their own work. But this brings us back to our above 
discussion about the level ofinfluence a petitioner's work must have in the field to meet this criterion's 
requirements. Agency policy requires that published material has "been highly cited relative to others' 
work in that field," and that it "has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others 
working in the field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
And as it relates to letters from experts in the field, they should explain "the nature and significance 
of the person's contribution" and "specifically describe the person's contribution and its significance 
to the field." Id. While these letters may exhibit general reliance on the Petitioner's findings in their 
work or reflect that these individuals built upon the Petitioner's work in their own research, they do 
not show the significance of his research in the overall field beyond their own research and written 
findings. Such a showing of incremental progress to the common repository of knowledge does not 
demonstrate that his work has "provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others 
5 
working in the field." Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *20 (D. Neb. Sept. 
12, 2025) (upholding that it must be objectively demonstrated that the field considers the work to be 
majorly significant); Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134 ("[T]he regulatory requirement that the petitioner 
demonstrate the 'major significance' of any original contributions means that the petitioner's work 
must significantly affect [his] field of endeavor"). 
The remainder ofthe Petitioner's appellate claims relate to his citations, funding, and peer review. For 
his citation record, the Petitioner notes his collective number of citations for all of his work, as well 
as how the citations to one of his papers in 2024 placed him in the top percent of most cited articles in 
engineering for that year. His arguments regarding the collective or total number of citations to his 
published work fails to meet this criterion's requirements. In general, the comparison of a petitioner's 
cumulative citations to those of others in the field in an attempt to draw a conclusion of their 
comparative impact in the field, is often more appropriate within a final merits determination after 
they have satisfied at least three regulatory criteria. Such a comparison may assist in determining 
whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that they are 
among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. We view a comparison ofcitations 
to individual scientific articles to be more relevant for this criterion in order to establish the overall 
field's general view of a contribution of major significance. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l ). 
Turning to his individual published work in 2024, he notes his 1 7 citations place him in the top 10 
percent most cited articles in the engineering field for that year with the average number of cites at 
2.78. The comparative ranking the Petitioner presents to baseline or average citation rates does not 
automatically establish majorly significant contributions in the field. For example, according to the 
data from Clarivate Analytics, engineering papers published in 2024 receiving seven citations were in 
the top ten percent, and 24 citations were in the top one percent. The Petitioner has not demonstrated 
that papers with such citation counts have necessarily had a major, significant impact or influence in 
the field as evidenced by being among the top percentage of most highly cited articles according to 
year of publication. 
The issue for this criterion is whether the Petitioner has made original contributions of major 
significance in the field rather than where his citation rates rank among the averages of others in his 
field. A more appropriate analysis, for example, would be to compare the Petitioner's citations to 
other similarly, highly cited articles that the field views as having been of major significance, as well 
as factoring in other corroborating evidence. The Petitioner has not demonstrated, as he asserts, that 
his articles at the time of filing, using the methodology found in the Clarivate Analytics evidence 
through citation numbers and percentiles, resulted in original contributions of major significance in 
the field. We also note that the Petitioner's documentation from Clarivate Analytics reflects that 
"[ c ]itation frequency is highly skewed, with many infrequently cited papers and relatively few highly 
cited papers. Consequently, citation rates should not be interpreted as representing the central 
tendency of the distribution." Stated differently, the Petitioner did not sufficiently show the reliability 
of these figures. 
The Petitioner also places substantial weight on the funding received for some of his research. We 
distinguish between evidence that research was sufficiently meritorious to attract funding versus 
evidence that the work actually resulted in a contribution of major significance in the field. The fact 
6 
that some entity elected to support research may demonstrate that the work was considered 
worthwhile, original, or potentially useful. But without more it does not establish the subsequent 
impact in the Petitioner's field. Research ordinarily must offer some new or useful information to 
obtain funding or other scholarly attention, but it does not follow that every researcher whose work 
adds to the general pool of knowledge has also made a contribution of major significance. 
Agency policy further speaks to the issue of funding: "Evidence that the person's work was funded, 
patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily 
establish, on its own, that the work is ofmajor significance to the field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The burden is on the Petitioner to provide adequate evidence to support his claims. And we note that 
funding is most probative when the record connects the funding to demonstrated field-wide 
consequences. For instance, evidence that the funded work was subsequently adopted, was widely 
implemented, it materially influenced other researchers or practitioners, or otherwise produced a 
measurable impact beyond the funding institution. In his appeal, the Petitioner has not even explained 
how the funded projects resulted in any measurable impact in the broader field. Absent that type of 
evidence, funding establishes at best that an institution or sponsor considered the research sufficiently 
promising or meritorious to warrant investment. But it does not illustrate that the research ultimately 
achieved the level of major significance in the field that the regulation here requires. 
And finally, the Petitioner identifies his peer review service for distinguished or reputable journals as 
evidence under this criterion. We agree that his service for the listed journals may reflect his expertise 
and professional recognition. But we do not agree that those factors demonstrate selection for peer 
review even amounts to a contribution to the field, let alone one of major significance. Performing 
peer review is almost exclusively an evaluative process rather than reflecting some level of creativity 
or innovation. And even if we were to agree that peer review was more than an evaluative 
process-which we do not outside of some exceptional set of circumstances-it is the filing party's 
responsibility to demonstrate their service was more than just a contribution. They also must show 
the degree to which that contribution impacted the field, and that it was significant. 
Lacking is an indication that to be selected to perform peer review for the relevant journals and 
conferences, one must have received a reaction from colleagues that would be viewed as a major 
significance in the field. To sum up, while performing peer review may highlight an individual's 
professional standing, it does not meet the standard for demonstrating original contributions of major 
significance in the field. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not established that he made original contributions of major 
significance in the field. The record shows that his work has received some citations, funding, peer 
review opportunities, and garnered favorable support letters, but this evidence does not demonstrate 
that his contributions significantly affected the field as a whole. Rather, the evidence reflects 
respectable scholarly activity, incremental research progress, and limited reliance by other researchers. 
Because the Petitioner has not shown that his work provoked widespread commentary, was broadly 
adopted, materially influenced the field, or otherwise rose to the level of major significance, he has 
not satisfied this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. Patel 
v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) ( citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision)). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal, 2025 WL 
2879217, at *12; Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 
2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an 
exhaustive historical review of the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the 
comprehensive regulatory scheme intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive 
this classification. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
