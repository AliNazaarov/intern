Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a chemical engineer, materials scientist, and chemical technologist, seeks classification 
under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with 
"extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l l 53(b )(1 )(A). This EB-1 classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 
203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner 
meets at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification and does not intend to 
come to the United States to continue her work in the field. The matter is now before us on appeal. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS

"a chemical engineer, material scientist and chemical technology." SCOPS issued requests for

B. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that she received a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied six of these criteria, summarized below: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards or prizes; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• Commanding of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v), (vi), (viii), (ix).

8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v), and (viii), as discussed below. As the Petitioner did not satisfy four

1. Lesser Recognized Prizes or Award for Excellence in the Field 
In order to fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that she received prizes or awards, and 
they are nationally or internationally recognized for excellence in the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). We first determine whether the alien is the recipient of prizes or awards, and then 
whether the award is nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence 
in the alien's field of endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims that she was awarded a presidential scholarship from the .___________. 
in February 2012. The Petitioner submitted evidence that her ~-------------~ name appeared on the list of scholarship recipients and contends that the scholarship has "stringent 
selection process" and "demonstrates ... national significance" as the recipients are selected based on 
their high academic performance or other outstanding achievements. The selection criteria for the 
presidential scholarship submitted in the record indicates that scholarships are limited to students of 
"Russian universities (up to 700 recipients per year) as well as students studying abroad (a quota of 
40 scholarships is allocated for them" and they must "have been enrolled in a foll-time government­
fonded program at a university for more than two years with high academic performance." 
However, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the presidential scholarship award is "nationally or 
internationally recognized" in the field of her endeavor or the field of chemical engineering. The 
record lacks sufficient independent, objective evidence corroborating the scholarship's purported 
recognition. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence of 
how a larger audience viewed [ a petitioner's] awards, there was no way for the agency to evaluate 
whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion"). The scholarship's 
issuance by a national ministry does not automatically prove the grant's receipt ofnational recognition. 
The record does not show, for example, that the Petitioner received the scholarship publicly or that 
news or professional organizations reported the grant's issuance. Additionally, the record does not 
sufficiently show that this scholarship is in the field of chemistry engineering as it appears to be open 
generally to students who are studying in a variety of fields. The Petitioner also has not demonstrated 
that her receipt of the scholarship is for excellence in the field as she competed with fellow students 
who are generally in training for future careers, not with experienced and recognized experts in the 
field. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner did not show that she meets this criterion.

membership in the Russian Chemical Society (RCS) fulfills this criterion.

4. Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). A critical role means an alien contributed in a significantly important way 
to the activities ofthe organization, establishment, division, or department. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 8. Next, the Petitioner must establish that the organization, 
establishment, division, or department has a distinguished reputation. The word "distinguished" 
ordinarily means "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." 
Distinguished, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant 
considerations in determining whether an organization is "distinguished" include not only its relative 
size and longevity but also other factors, such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage of it. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 8.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need 
not farther analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also 
Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F. Supp. 3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the 
regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks 
an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility 
for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
