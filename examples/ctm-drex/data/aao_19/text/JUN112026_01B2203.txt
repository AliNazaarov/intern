Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a human resources (HR) manager, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of ten lesser criteria or comparable evidence. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and ( 4). 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met two of the criteria, pertaining to judging and leading or 
critical roles. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she also meets the other claimed criteria. 
We take particular notice of the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), which calls for published 
material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to 
the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. 
The Petitioner submitted English translations of two Chinese-language online articles, with background 
information about the publications in which they appeared. A translated D 2025 article from China 
Enterprise News indicates that the Petitioner "has plard a key role" in "develop[ing] a new 'Human 
Resource Decision Management Model -I " A second article, published inc=]2025 on 
dicn.china.com.cn, features "a Dialogue with [the Petitioner]" concerning "the Innovation in Human 
Resources Management with Digital Transformation." The Petitioner submitted evidence including 
Chinese government materials about the publications. 
USCIS acknowledged the Petitioner's evidence, but concluded: "the submitted published materials are 
not about the petitioner" because they do "not dive into the petitioner's works or provide specific details 
about the petitioner['s] works, innovative projects, or methodologies." USCIS also determined that the 
background materials do "not include the criteria or process used to determine the listed publishing 
entities rankings and readership or circulation." USCIS concluded that "the information submitted does 
not demonstrate that the petitioner has received the level of media attention or interest to rise to the very 
top of their field."

On appeal, the Petitioner disputes these conclusions. We agree with the Petitioner that the articles are 
about the Petitioner, relating to her work. The denial decision added requirements not found in the 
regulations. Also, the articles go into reasonable detail about the Petitioner's work, and quote the 
Petitioner about that work. The Petitioner also submitted sufficient evidence to establish that the articles 
appeared in major Chinese media. 
We also agree with the Petitioner that the question of whether the media coverage places her at the top of 
her field is a matter for the final merits determination. 
We will withdraw USCIS' decision, because that decision was based on the determination that the 
Petitioner had satisfied fewer than three of the initial evidentiary criteria. As noted above, where a 
petitioner meets these initial evidentiary requirements, USCIS then considers the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assesses whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115, and section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) 
of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3). We will therefore remand the matter for USCIS to make the 
final merits determination in the first instance. 
Because we have determined that the Petitioner satisfies a third initial criterion, we will not examine 
the remaining claimed criteria in depth. The satisfaction of three criteria is enough to proceed to a 
final merits determination. The final merits determination considers the totality of the evidence in the 
broader context of sustained national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that 
"are not relevant to the antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least 
three types of evidence" "might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is 
at the very top of his or her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations 
that might impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of 
an initial criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
In rendering the final merits determination, USCIS may wish to consider certain factors when 
determining the extent of the Petitioner's recognition and acclaim. For example, USCIS can consider 
whether the publication of two articles about the Petitioner, a few weeks before the petition's filing date 
in June 2025, are strong evidence ofthe required level of recognition and sustained acclaim. 
The Petitioner asserts that she developed a widely-implemented HR model. USCIS can consider whether 
the evidence shows implementation of this model outside of the company that employed the Petitioner. 
There is also the related but broader question of how widely the Petitioner has earned lasting recognition 
and sustained national or international acclaim outside of companies that have employed her. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
