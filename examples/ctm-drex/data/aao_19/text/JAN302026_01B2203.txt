Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an engineer in the sustainable energy field, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
two initial evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
he contends that he satisfied two other evidentiary criteria and demonstrated sufficient acclaim and 
recognition to establish his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that he did not meet the minimum number of evidentiary criteria to 
qualify for the requested immigrant visa category. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) then makes a final merits determination. To merit final approval, the 
record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and 
recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step review process to be 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
The record shows that an Indian university awarded the Petitioner a bachelor of engineering degree in 
mechatronics, a field that integrates mechanical engineering, electronics, computer science, and 
control systems to create smart products and automated systems. He continued his studies in the 
United States, earning a master of science degree in energy systems. While in school, he designed a 
portable thermoelectric heating and cooling fan for refugee camps and low-income communities, and 
a modular waste-to-energy microgrid generator for disaster-prone areas. Since August 2019, he has 
worked in this country as an energy engineer for an energy consulting company. 
If allowed to remain in the United States, the Petitioner states that he seeks to establish a "public­
interest technology enterprise focused on energy access, disaster resilience, and equity-driven climate 
innovation." He states that his enterprise would: commercialize thermoelectric and waste-to-energy 
systems; support government grant programs; collaborate with state energy offices; develop 
educational pipelines; and contribute to national resilience, clean infrastructure, and equity design 
priorities. He states that "[h ]is continued presence in the United States will result in significant and 
demonstrable national benefits and will contribute directly to federal climate and equity goals, 
accelerate commercialization of public-interest technology, and support resilience infrastructure 
serving at-risk populations." 
B. The Initial Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
SCOPS' found that the Petitioner submitted evidence of: 
• Published materials about himself and his work; and 
• His original contributions of major significance to the field.

To satisfy an evidentiary criterion, the Petitioner must objectively meet the parameters ofan applicable 
regulatory description. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B). "[N]either USCIS nor [the] 
AAO [ Administrative Appeals Office] may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121

We will first review SCOPS' favorable evidentiary findings. 
1. Published Material 
This evidentiary criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is 
sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." 
When adjudicating this evidentiary requirement, USCIS first determines whether the published 
material relates to a petitioner and their specific work in their field. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. The Agency next considers whether a publication qualifies as a 
professional publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. Id. Relevant factors 
for professional and trade publications include their intended audiences. Id. For major trade 
publications and other major media, relevant factors include their relative circulations, readerships, or 
viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies ofpublications about his selection as one of five students from among 
thousands of worldwide applicants to present their inventions onstage at a 2018 meeting addressing 
global problems. He and his team developed the thermoelectric heating and cooling fan for refugee 
camps and low-income communities. He submitted copies of articles about his selection in 
publications of the U.S. university that issued his master's degree. The articles include photographs 
of him and others on stage with a former U.S. president who founded the organization that holds the 
annual meetings. The record also contains copies of the organization's 2019 e-mail message to its 
subscribers, which contains similar photos. 
Despite describing the Petitioner as part of a team, the published materials sufficiently relate to him 
and his work in his field. 
[O]fficers may consider material that focuses solely or primarily on work or research 
being undertaken by a team ofwhich the person is a member, provided that the material

mentions the person in connection with the work or other evidence in the record 
documents the person's significant role in the work or research. 
6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. The published materials mention the Petitioner and 
describe him as the team's leader. Also, a copy of a 2020 Indian patent certificate identifies him as 
the thermoelectric fan's inventor. Thus, the materials satisfy the first part ofthis criterion's evaluation. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that the submitted published materials qualify as 
professional or major trade publications or other major media. A letter from a former deputy director 
ofthe organization that honored the Petitioner's thermoelectric fan states that "the selected participants 
were announced during the plenary session [before an audience of about 2,000 people], promoted in 
official [organization] media channels, and featured on websites and publications viewed by global 
audiences." But the Petitioner did not submit evidence that the publications' intended audiences were 
professionals or others in his field or of the publications' relative circulations, readerships, or 
viewership. See Kumar v. Dir. of USCIS, No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. 
Apr. 1, 2024) (ruling that, because a senior data analyst did not "submit sufficient evidence to establish 
that the material qualified as professional or major trade publications or other major media", he did 
not meet this evidentiary requirement). The Petitioner therefore did not document his materials' 
appearance in professional or major trade publications or other major media. 
The Petitioner did not establish his submission of published material about himself and his work in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' 
contrary finding. 
2. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner submitted evidence of original contributions 
of major significance in his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). We will therefore proceed to review 
SCOPS' findings regarding the additional evidentiary criteria that the Petitioner claims to have met. 
3. Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards for Excellence 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a petitioner must submit "[d]ocumentation of the[ir] receipt of 
lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field ofendeavor." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
USCIS first determines if a petitioner - as opposed to their employer, for example - received prizes 
or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 1. If so, USCIS next considers 
whether an award is nationally or internationally recognized and given for excellence in the 
appropriate field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, at a U.S. student innovation expos1t10n, he won an 
"outstanding research" award regarding his waste-to-energy generator. Based on that research, he was 
selected to participate in a U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF) program that matched student 
inventors with mentors to help the students' develop their ideas. During the program, the Petitioner 
further developed his waste-to-energy system, and, at the program's end, the NSF chose his project to 
4 
receive protype funding. He also submitted evidence of his selection to present the thermoelectric fan 
invention on stage and that, based on his waste-to-energy generator, he placed within the top 50 in an 
international entrepreneurship competition. 
As SCOPS found, however, the Petitioner did not sufficiently demonstrate that these honors received 
national or international recognition. He submitted evidence that the U.S. university where he studied 
published articles about his awards. But the record does not establish that the university publications 
have sufficient audiences to reflect national or international recognition. He also provided evidence 
that the organization that honored his thermoelectric fan publicized his award on the organization's 
social media and in email messages to its subscribers. But these materials are insufficient to show that 
people nationally or internationally beyond the awarding organization learned of the Petitioner's 
honor. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence of how a 
larger audience viewed Plaintiffs awards, there was no way for the agency to evaluate whether those 
awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion.") 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS improperly required national or international media 
articles about his awards. He contends that, under USCIS policy, "awards conferred by national or 
international institutions ... are inherently nationally or internationally recognized. Media coverage 
is not required under the regulation." 
We agree with the Petitioner that, to show an award' s national or international recognition, he need 
not necessarily have provided examples of media coverage. Also, we agree that the NSF and the 
organization that honored his thermoelectric fan are nationally or internationally recognized 
institutions. "But, as reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not the awarding 
organization." Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) ( emphasis in original). 
Thus, the Petitioner had to show that his specific awards - not the organizations that issued them -
garnered national or international recognition. He submitted insufficient evidence to show that the 
awards received such recognition beyond their awarding organizations. 
The Petitioner also argues that, because SCOPS credited his published materials as meeting 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii), SCOPS must also accept the same evidence to meet this awards criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). He stated: "USCIS accepted national/international media coverage of these awards 
but then declined to credit them as national/international media coverage of those awards under the 
Awards criterion. This is logically inconsistent and contrary to law." 
As previously discussed, however, SCOPS erred in its decision on the published materials under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Thus, the premise of the Petitioner's argument is no longer true. 
The Petitioner did not submit evidence ofhis receipt ofnationally or internationally recognized awards 
for excellence in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary 
criterion.

This evidentiary criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the 
field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi).

The Petitioner's evidence included documentation indicating that he first-authored a 2015 scholarly 
article on thermoelectric configurations in an international journal on energy and statistics. In response 
to SCOPS' request for additional evidence, he submitted printouts of additional online information

SCOPS questioned the journal's reputation, stating that "some sources classify[] it as a predatory 
journal with a lack of clear information on the journal's review process and its adherence to standard 
academic publishing practices." SCOPS concluded: 
Given the concerns surrounding the [journal]'s reputation and considering that the

SCOPS did not identify its sources. Our online search indicated that the journal stopped publishing in 
201 7. But we found no evidence disparaging its reputation or academic review practices. Thus, a 
preponderance of the evidence indicates that a scholarly journal in the Petitioner's field published his 
article using a peer-review process. We therefore find that he met this evidentiary criterion. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B) ("[A]lthough some of the regulatory language relating to evidence

The Petitioner submitted evidence that he authored scholarly articles in his field in professional 
publications. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met two initial evidentiary criteria. Because he did not meet a requisite third, we, 
like SCOPS, need not conduct a final merits determination. But, even if he qualified for a final merits 
determination, he would not have demonstrated his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. USCIS must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
6 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
The Petitioner's strongest evidence of his claimed extraordinary ability in his field are his inventions. 
The U.S. entrepreneurship professor who ran the NSF program in which the Petitioner developed the 
waste-to-energy generator stated that this invention "can be a game-changer." A mentor in the 
program stated that "[t]he potential of his work was not just theoretical - it carried profound 
humanitarian and environmental implications." Also, United Nations agencies and government 
organizations expressed interest in the generator and the thermoelectric fan he developed. 
Nevertheless, the Petitioner's evidence as a whole would not have placed him among the small 
percentage at his field's very top. While he submitted several recommendation letters on his behalf: 
most of them are from people who personally know him, such as former teachers, co-workers, and 
mentors in the NSF program. "It is generally expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered 
sustained national or international acclaim would have received recognition for their accomplishments 
well beyond the circle of their personal and professional acquaintances." 6 USCJS Policy Manual 
F.2(8)(3). In general, the evidence identifies the Petitioner as a rising star in his field but would fall 
short of placing him among the small percentage who are already at the field's very top. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991 ). The legislature indicated 
that this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the 
very top of their field of endeavor." Id. Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner would not 
have established his claimed place among the small percentage at his field's very top. See Amin, 
24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary ability classifications, we cannot say that the 
agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very 
good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' 
premier leagues do not automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not meet the required number of alternative evidentiary criteria. We will therefore 
affirm the petition's denial.
