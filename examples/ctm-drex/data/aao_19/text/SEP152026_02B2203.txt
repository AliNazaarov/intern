Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a television director and producer, seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidence 
requirements for this classification, he did not demonstrate his sustained national or international 
acclaim and establish that he is among the small percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor. 
The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the claimed criterion 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) for original contributions of major significance to his field, nor did he 
meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) for having performed in leading or critical role for a 
distinguished organization. However, USCIS found that the Petitioner met the evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii), (iv), and (x), relating to prizes or awards, published material, judging, 
and commercial success. Because the Petitioner initially demonstrated that he met at least three of the 
initial evidentiary requirements, USCIS proceeded to a final merits determination. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that USCIS' use of the Kazarian final merits test is unlawful based 
on Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3 l 70, 2026 WL 895708 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026). The U.S. district 
court in Mukheji concluded that USCIS' adoption ofthe two-tiered system under Kazarian is not valid 
because it was implemented without the proper notice and comment rulemaking process. However, 
we note that Mukheji is an unpublished decision. While we may consider the court's reasoning when 
it is properly before the AAO, unpublished opinions are not considered binding precedent. See 
Suchite-Salguero v. Att'y Gen. United States, 147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have 
cautioned over and over again that non-precedential opinions carry little to no weight for future 
cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531,538 n.5 (5th Cir. 2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715, 
719 (BIA 1993) (considering the reasoning of district court decisions). Further, we note that other 
U.S. district courts have affirmed USCIS's final merit determination. For example, in Taneja v. 
Miller, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 1734266, at *4-8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026), the court 
found that USCIS' final merits determination comports with the governing statute and the agency 
regulations and it did not require notice and comment rulemaking. See also Branski v. Seng, No. 25-
2 
CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) (finding plaintiff did not show that 
USCIS exceeded its statutory authority by engaging in the step two, "final merits determination" 
analysis). 
The Petitioner also asserts that USCIS did not assess the totality of the evidence to determine whether 
it demonstrated his sustained national or international acclaim as an individual among the few at the 
top of his field. The Petitioner avers that USCIS instead applied an impermissibly fragmented analysis 
that did not assess the combined weight of the evidence of record. However, in final merits 
determination, USCIS considers all evidence in the totality. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(8)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Likewise, we will review the totality of the 
evidence in the context of a final merits determination. Id. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner 
has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he has sustained national or international 
acclaim and is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits 
determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to 
determine if his successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of 
endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2),(3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility. 
The Petitioner is an independent producer and the creator of several television series, including some 
for which he has also served as director and host. His programs include internationally distributed 
shows focused on enthusiast culture, outdoor adventure, and travel. As stated above, the record 
includes documentation demonstrating that he met the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), 
(iii), (iv), and (x). He has won awards for his work, he has served as a judge of others in the field of 
televised and streaming programming, he has been the subject of published material discussing his 
work, and his work has had commercial success in his field. 
The record, however, does not contain sufficient evidence to show that the Petitioner has risen to that 
small percentage at the very top ofhis field ofendeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The commentary 
for the proposed regulations implementing section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent 
of Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this 
regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required" for 
lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). Approval or denial of a petition is 
based on the type and quality of evidence submitted, and a petitioner must explain the significance of 
the submitted evidence and state how it demonstrates that they have achieved sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognition in their field ofexpertise. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(8)(2). While there is no "quantitative threshold" the quantity and quality of evidence is 
a consideration in a final merits analysis if it reflects that the Petitioner is one of that small percentage 
who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and has sustained national or international 
acclaim. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121-22. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386-87 (5th Cir. 
2002) (stating that the narrow definition reflects the stringency of the extraordinary ability standard). 
Here, although the Petitioner has submitted evidence indicating that he has achieved a level of success

The Petitioner asserts that 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) requires only that the petitioner has "participated, 
either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the field." However, meeting the 
plain language of a criterion concerns only the first step ofKazarian 's two-step analytical framework. 
The second step of Kazarian requires evaluation of the individual's participation as a judge to 
determine whether it was indicative of the person being one of the small percentage who have risen to

producers, executives, educators, and past award-winning professionals." Based on this description

not clear how the Petitioner's service places him at the very top of the field. While the letter 
demonstrates the initial evidentiary criteria of serving as a judge, it does not provide sufficient 
information to establish that his service as a judge shows that he is among the small percentage at the 
top of his field.

Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at 
the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 
I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of 
accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24-CV-83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 
l 0, 2025) (stating that EB- I visas are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of 
individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, considering all of the evidence, we conclude that 
the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. 
See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more 
accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he 
is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the reasons stated above, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
