Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a human resources consultant, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy any of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, of which he must establish at least three. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien bas extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which bas been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner indicates that he is a manager and partner at an e-commerce business development 
company. Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established that he has received a major, 
internationally recognized award as defined at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), he must satisfy at least three of 
the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
In denying the petition, SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy any of the ten alternate 
regulatory criteria and therefore did not establish eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. On 
appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he has established eligibility for seven criteria. After reviewing 
the evidence, we conclude that the record does not reflect that the Petitioner meets the requirements 
of at least three criteria. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) requires a petitioner to provide "[d]ocumentation of the 
alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the 
field of endeavor." 
In the initial filing, the Petitioner acknowledged that he "has not received any nationally or 
internationally recognized awards or prizes for excellence," but asserted that he establishes this 
criterion "by demonstrating exceptional performance and recognition in his professional career." On 
appeal, the Petitioner repeats the claims made in response to SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE), 
asserting that "while traditional awards may not apply, the combination ofISO certifications, detailed 
professional roles, and industry influence supports [the Petitioner's] exceptional qualifications and 
achievements" and therefore satisfies this criterion. 
2 
To fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that he has received prizes or awards, and that 
they are nationally or internationally recognized for excellence in the field of endeavor. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The record does not 
demonstrate that the Petitioner has received a nationally or internationally recognized prize or award 
for excellence in his field, and he does not assert that he has received such an award. Instead, the 
Petitioner seeks to rely on a combination of other factors that he contends support his "qualifications 
and achievements." But this is not what the regulation requires. Moreover, on appeal, the Petitioner 
does not identify any factual or legal errors in SCOPS' decision that this criterion does not apply. 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not show that he meets this criterion. 
2. Membership in Associations Which Require Outstanding Achievements. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based on receiving training certificates in ISO 
9001 :2000 and ISO 9001:2001. The Petitioner acknowledges that ISO certifications "are not 
traditional membership-based organizations" but asserts that they are "highly esteemed international 
standards for quality management systems." The Petitioner contends that these certifications reflect 
his "outstanding professional qualifications and adherence to rigorous standards within the field of 
human resources" and that they therefore "serve as evidence of his membership in entities with high 
standards and significant prestige." 
On appeal, the Petitioner repeats the same claims previously presented to SCOPS that his training 
certificates are relevant to his field, reflect his expertise, and demonstrate his significant 
accomplishments. However, the Petitioner did not provide evidence to establish that his ISO training 
certifications constitute membership in an association, as required by the plain language of the 
regulation. Additionally, the Petitioner did not provide evidence to establish the requirements to 
obtain these certifications, in order to demonstrate whether receiving them requires outstanding 
achievements, as judged by recognized national or international experts in the field. The record 
therefore does not establish that the Petitioner is a member of an association that requires outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized experts in the field. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfies this criterion. 
3. Display at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." 
The Petitioner claims eligibility under this criterion based on 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), which states "[i]f 
the above standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit 
comparable evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision provides 
3 
petitioners with the opportunity to submit comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, if 
it is determined that the evidentiary criteria described in the regulations do not readily apply to the 
person's occupation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. When evaluating such 
comparable evidence, we consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's 
occupation and, ifnot, whether the evidence is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
Id. 
The Petitioner claims that the "impact of the Petitioner's work, practices, and protocols in human 
resources has been far-reaching, influencing his peers and colleagues to adopt similar strategies;" that 
his approach "has been emulated by peers in the industry" who "have sought to replicate similar 
initiatives;" and that"[o ]ther HR professionals have referred to his work." The Petitioner asserts that 
his "innovative work, industry influence, and public recognition all serve as comparable evidence that 
satisfies the requirement ... for having his work displayed in an artistic exhibition or showcase ...." 
The Petitioner does not cite any evidence in the record in support of these assertions. The burden of 
proof rests with the Petitioner, who should submit "relevant, probative, and credible evidence" to 
establish his claims. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. Additionally, the plain language 
of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) permits the petitioner to submit comparable "evidence" to establish 
eligibility. The Petitioner's unsupported statements are insufficient to establish the claimed impact of 
his work and insufficient to establish that this impact would be truly comparable to the display ofwork 
at artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfies this criterion, including through the 
submission of comparable evidence. 
4. Leading or Critical Role for Distinguished Organizations or Establishments. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for an organization, establishment, or a division or 
department of an organization or establishment. Evidence of a leading role should show that the alien 
was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help to establish that 
a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way 
that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or 
those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In determining whether the 
organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof: has a distinguished reputation, 
relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and relevant media coverage. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based on his employment with .________... as 
a head of human resources, and his employment with I Iin their human resources 
department, as evidenced by reference letters from colleagues within these organizations. 
Additionally, the Petitioner submitted copies of training certificates related to human resources 
management and asserts that the institutions from which he obtained these certificates are prestigious. 
On appeal, the Petitioner, again, does not identify legal or factual errors in SCOPS' determination that 
4 
the Petitioner did not establish this criterion, and instead repeats the same claims previously presented. 
For the following reasons, we conclude that the Petitioner has not established this criterion. 
The reference letters speak highly but generally of the Petitioner's performance in his roles, stating 
that his work was "crucial in implementing the necessary actions to comply with government 
regulations" during the COVID-19 pandemic, that the Petitioner's efforts "added value" to the 
company's processes, and that the Petitioner led a project to implement a new employee evaluation 
system. However, the letters do not provide sufficient specific information or details to establish that 
the Petitioner's role within these organizations was either leading or critical. 
Additionally, one of the letters claims that the Petitioner was employed as a "Head of Human 
Resources for the productive [e]stablishments that the company owns in the province ofl land 
as a legal proxy to represent the company in labor matters within the province ofi I" However, 
the letter does not provide context regarding the organizational structure of the company, nor did the 
Petitioner provide other evidence to establish the company structure, such that we are able to determine 
whether this was a "leading" role within the organization. Moreover, the job duties provided in the 
letter do not help establish that the role was leading. For example, the letter states that this role required 
the Petitioner to "[ c ]oordindate" and "[p ]articipate" in activities and does not indicate whether the 
Petitioner led, supervised, or managed other employees and, if so, how many. We therefore conclude 
that the letter does not provide evidence of a job title with matching duties to help establish that a role 
was leading. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. 
This regulatory criterion also requires that the organization or establishment, or the department for 
which the person holds or held a leading or critical role, have a distinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii). The Petitioner's counsel stated in the RFE response, and repeats on appeal, that 
these organizations are "prominent" and have a "distinguished reputation," but the record does not 
contain evidence in support of these claims. Counsel's unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute 
evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("statements in a brief, motion, 
or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight."). 
Finally, the training certificates submitted by the Petitioner do not establish the Petitioner's leading or 
critical role in an organization or establishment that has a distinguished reputation, as required by the 
plain language of the regulation. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he fulfills this criterion. 
5. Commercial Success in the Performing Arts. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) requires "[e]vidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 
The Petitioner claims eligibility under this criterion based on the "flexibility allowed under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)( 4)," asserting that this regulation "allows for a flexible interpretation of these criteria, 
permitting the use of analogous evidence to establish eligibility." As stated above, the regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) permits the submission of comparable evidence if an evidentiary criterion's 
standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation. 
5 
Here, the Petitioner asserts that his contributions to his prior employers as a human resources manager 
"can be seen as equivalent to commercial success in the performing arts because his leadership led to 
substantial financial and operational benefits for these organizations." In support of this claim, the 
Petitioner refers to his reference letters from prior colleagues. The Petitioner asserts that these letters 
establish that the Petitioner's work meets this criterion under the comparable evidence regulation 
because "his leadership substantially impacted the company's commercial performance" and they 
establish the "direct commercial success resulting from [the Petitioner's] HR management." 
These reference letters, also discussed above, state the Petitioner's dates of employment and job title, 
describe the Petitioner's prior job duties, and discuss projects to which the Petitioner was assigned. 
However, they are not specific or detailed enough to establish the Petitioner's claim that his work led 
to "substantial financial and operational benefits" for these organizations. Additionally, the Petitioner 
has not established how these reference letters are truly comparable to "box office receipts or record, 
cassette, compact disk, or video sales" that demonstrate commercial success in the performing arts. 
General claims that USCIS should accept witness letters as comparable evidence are not persuasive. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfies this criterion, including through the 
submission of comparable evidence. 
B. Remaining Claimed Evidentiary Criteria 
Although the Petitioner maintains eligibility for two additional criteria on appeal-those relating to 
judging the work of others at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and commanding a high salary or other 
remuneration at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix)-the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for any of 
the evidentiary criteria discussed above. He therefore cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement 
of meeting at least three of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Therefore, we reserve the issue of 
whether he has satisfied the additional two criteria he claims on appeal. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 
429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
farther analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.
