Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a pole and aerial sport performer and instructor, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 
1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner established that she satisfied the 
initial evidentiary requirements, she did not demonstrate her sustained national or international 
acclaim and that she is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. We 
dismissed the Petitioner's appeal and two combined motions to reopen and reconsider. We 
subsequently reopened the matter on service motion under 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(5) in response to 
litigation.' Following the Petitioner's response to our service motion, the matter is again before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(3). 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation,

to submit a brief in support of the petition. Her response has been incorporated into the record. 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable 
evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USC IS, 596 F .3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation").

B. Final Merits Determination 
While we have concluded that the Petitioner has not fulfilled the initial evidentiary requirement of 
three criteria, and thus need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian 
v. USCJS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20, we will nonetheless consider the issue ofthe Petitioner's qualification 
under that standard, as it is the focus of both USC IS' decision and the appeal. We have reviewed the 
record in the aggregate and agree with USCIS that the Petitioner has not established eligibility as a 
person of extraordinary ability. 
Because extraordinary ability is an elite level ofaccomplishment whose recognition necessarily entails 
a judgement call, it cannot be established through meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
8 
alone. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394-395. Congress sets a very high benchmark for aliens 
of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been 
recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an alien's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the field 
of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. Here, the Petitioner asserts that she has extraordinary ability in the field of pole 
and aerial sport, submitting evidence ofher experience and achievements in this field. She emphasizes 
having worked as a pole and aerial performer for~-----------~and as a performer 
and model for charity and community events. Currently, she volunteers as a coordinator forl II lin California and works as a pole and aerial sport instructor for children and adults. She 
indicates her intent to help grow pole and aerial sport with the goal of pole and aerial sport becoming 
an Olympic sport. 
As mentioned above, the Petitioner judged the work of others in her field. We have also considered 
evidence related to nationally or internationally recognized awards or prizes; published materials about 
the Petitioner; and display of her work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. The record, 
however, does not demonstrate that her achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
As it pertains to her service as a judge of others, an evaluation of the significance of her experience is 
appropriate to determine if such evidence is indicative of the extraordinary ability required for this 
highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121-22.4 The Petitioner 
provided certificates indicating she served as a judge ofmultiple events at two pole sport competitions, 
one in 2022 and the other in 2023. However, the Petitioner did not establish that her work as a judge 
at the two pole sport competitions resulted in or reflects her sustained international or national acclaim 
in the field, was recognized outside of the competitions, or places her among that small percentage at 
the very top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. §204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner did not demonstrate that her judging experience contributes to a finding that she has a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress or indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim. See H.R. Rep. No. at 59 and section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the 
Act. For instance, she did not establish that she garnered wide attention from the field based on her 
work as a judge of the pole sport competitions. While reference letters generally mention that the 
Petitioner has been a judge at competitions, the letters do not provide details about the Petitioner's 
judging or explain how her judging contributed to a career of acclaimed work. Without evidence that 
sets the Petitioner apaii from others in her field, such as evidence that she has a consistent history of 
reviewing or judging recognized, acclaimed experts in her field, the Petitioner has not shown that her 
narrow judging experience places her among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of 
the field of endeavor. See Hamal v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 
1156801 at *2 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (finding the petitioner did not show that he served as a judge

Beyond the criterion that the Petitioner satisfied, we consider additional documentation in the record

As it relates to awards, the Petitioner provided evidence reflecting her receipt of four pole sport 
competition placements. Although the competition placements may show some recognition of her 
work from the field, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how they indicate sustained national or 
international acclaim or a career of acclaimed work in the field. While the Petitioner submitted letters 
generally mentioning her receipt of competition placements, the letters and other evidence in the 
record do not contextualize the significance of the competition placements and how they place her 
among that small percentage at the very top of the field. See Hamal v. U.S. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 
No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021 ), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. 
United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023)

meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability." Matter of 
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994).

204.5(h)(2).

Finally, the record reflects that the Petitioner provided several recommendations and reference letters. 
However, the letters are not sufficiently persuasive. The letters generally highlight the Petitioner's 
work and express her being a talented aerial sport performer and instructor who would be an asset 
instructing pole and aerial sport in the United States. Letters that make general and expansive 
statements regarding the petitioner and the petitioner's accomplishments are generally not persuasive. 
See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3). The letters do not claim, nor does the 
content show, that the Petitioner achieved national or international acclaim from the field. Moreover, 
the letters do not demonstrate that the Petitioner has attracted a level of attention, placing her at the 
very top of her field. In addition, the letters do not show that she has enjoyed a career of acclaimed

Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's work as a performer and instructor in her field, they do
