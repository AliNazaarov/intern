Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an actor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b0(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as an 
alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, or 
that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
111. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has worked as an actor in television (TV) series and films, some of which were 
showcased at international film festivals. He intends to continue working as an actor in the United 
States. 
As a preliminary matter, we acknowledge that the Petitioner has been the Beneficiary of an approved 
0-1 nonimmigrant petition. Although USCIS has approved at least one 0-1 nonimmigrant visa 
petition filed on behalf of the Petitioner, the nonimmigrant and immigrant extraordinary ability 
categories have different definitions, evidentiary requirements, and standards for persons in the arts. 
"Extraordinary ability in the field of arts" in the nonimmigrant 0-1 classification means "distinction." 
8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii). But in the immigrant context, "extraordinary ability" reflects that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field. Regardless, each petition is 
separate and independent and must be adjudicated on its own merits, under the corresponding statutory 
and regulatory provisions. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). In his initial petition, the Petitioner claimed to meet five criteria: membership 
in associations (ii); published material (iii); original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business­
related contributions of major significance in the field (v); display of work in the field at artistic 
exhibitions or showcases (vii); and commercial successes in the performing arts (x). SCOPS evaluated 
the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the request for evidence (RFE) 
and concluded the Petitioner only satisfied one criterion, the display of his work at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases. On appeal, the Petitioner only claims to meet four criteria and does not assert his 
eligibility under the membership (ii) criterion. Therefore, we deem that issue to be waived and will 
not address this criterion in our decision. See, e.g., Matter ofM-A-S-, 24 I&N Dec. 762, 767 n.2 (BIA 
2009). While we may not discuss every document in the record, we have reviewed and considered

1. Published Material about the Petitioner

professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field

material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy­

2. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) calls for "evidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 
The evidence must show that the volume of sales or box office receipts reflects the alien's commercial 
success in the performing arts relative to others involved in similar pursuits. Recording and releasing 
musical compilations or performing in theatrical, motion picture, or TV productions would be 
insufficient, in and of itself, to satisfy this criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(8)(1 ), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
In support of this criterion, the Petitioner states that he has "showcased his exceptional acting talent 
through a diverse range of roles in both film and television, earning recognition for his contributions to 
critically acclaimed and commercially successful productions." He also states that he has had "impactful 
roles in major productions that garnered significant achievements and audience reach that underscores his 
influence in the entertainment industry." Specifically, the Petitioner states that he played a major role in 
the filml Iasserting that his role "added depth to the narrative, contributing to the film's success." 
He also states that the film grossed $7.5 million worldwide. In support, the film's director submitted 
a letter stating that the Petitioner's portrayal added "essential emotional nuance and authenticity to the 
film's narrative." In another film,I I the Petitioner contends that his "portrayal added layers 
ofcomplexity to the film" and that "the movie achieved a global lifetime box office gross of$3.3 million."

He further asserts that "this success underscores" his ability to "engage with high-profile, internationally 
recognized projects that attract significant attention and resonate with global audiences." The Petitioner 
also states that he portrayed "a major recurring character" in a TV series I Iwhich he claims had 
"an average viewership of 8 million per episode." He maintains that his role contributed to the show's 
widespread success, and that his performance was recognized by the public. 
SCOPS determined that the criterion had not been met because the submitted information does not show 
that he has achieved commercial success relative to others involved in similar pursuits in the performing 
arts. In particular, SCOPS determined that the record did not demonstrate that the Petitioner's works have 
garnered sufficiently critical acclaim or favorable press reviews or otherwise drew a significant level of 
sales in a manner consistent with sustained national or international acclaim. SCOPS also found that the 
record lacked evidence of comparison of the Petitioner's evidence with others who are at or near the top 
of the field, and that he did not demonstrate that he maintained high box office receipts reflecting his 
recognition in the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the regulation does not require that the Petitioner have held a "lead 
role" to qualify under this criterion. He also asserts that he "played critical and integral roles in a series 
of commercially successful film and television productions." Similarly, he maintains that his roles 
were "not minor or incidental," but rather "essential to the productions' success." The Petitioner also 
insists that the "evidence submitted includes box office figures, viewership data, and expert attestation 
clearly establishing that [his] performances contributed meaningfully to the success of these works." 
Although we acknowledge the Petitioner's evidence and assertions, this criterion focuses on volume 
of sales and box office receipts as a measure of a petitioner's commercial success in the performing 
arts. Accordingly, the evidence must show that the volume of sales and box office receipts reflect the 
individual's commercial success relative to others involved in similar pursuits in the performing arts. 
While the Petitioner has appeared in films and TV series, he has not shown that the success of those 
productions was attributable to his specific work as an actor. Moreover, while the regulations do not 
require a petitioner to have exclusively held lead roles, the evidence must still demonstrate that the 
Petitioner's individual participation contributed to commercial success in a manner that distinguished 
him from others in similar pursuits. Here, the regulation has not been met. 
The Petitioner submitted Wikipedia and IMDb webpage printouts of the films and TV series in which 
he appeared. However, these sources do not demonstrate that the Petitioner himself achieved commercial 
success relative to others in the performing arts field. Moreover, the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
that his parts in films or TV series have attracted substantial audiences. The evidence also does not 
establish that his work resulted in significant commercial success relative to others at or near the top 
of the field. Nor is the evidence sufficient to demonstrate that the commercial successes of the 
aforementioned films or TV series in which he appeared were mainly because ofthe Petitioner's work. 
Accordingly, this criterion has not been met.

noted above, the Petitioner's unsuccessful demonstration ofhis eligibility under the criteria we discuss

C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 
429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for 
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
