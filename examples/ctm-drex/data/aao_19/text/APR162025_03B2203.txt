Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an elementary school teacher, seeks classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of the achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner met one of ten 
initial evidentiary requirements - two less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that she met three additional evidentiary criteria by submitting documentation 
of her: 
• Original contributions of major significance in her field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), (vi), (ix). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that she has met the scholarly article requirement. But, because she has not 
satisfied the requisite number of evidentiary criteria, we will dismiss the appeal. 1

I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, 
business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).2 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )( I )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, USCIS must make a final merits determination. To merit approval, the record- as a whole 
- must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized 
achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services' 
(USCIS ') two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and 
regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

l. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has made original contributions in their field. See 
generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)(l ), Criterion 5. If so, the Agency then determines whether 
the original contributions are of major significance in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted support letters from others in her field and information about her education­
related activities. On appeal, she cites her contributions in the disciplines of science, technology, 
engineering, and mathematics (STEM)] which she believes have had "lasting impact," especially in 
underrepresented communities like the I She states that she: was one of only eight 
educators selected nationwide to create and implement STEM curricula focusing on engineering and 
health education; served as a mentor to new teachers in her state; and reviewed the state's curricula. 
The Petitioner argues that "advancing STEM and health education have implications for national 
health priorities and workforce development" and that her work "demonstrates my ability to create 
solutions that extend beyond the classroom, impacting broader societal challenges." She contends that 
her mentoring "has contributed to improving educational quality, addressing teacher shortages, and 
ensuring professional development for future educators," which she says "is critical to national efforts 
to support and retain high-quality educators." She states that her contributions "directly support U.S. 
Department of Education priorities for equitable access to education and align with [ digital inclusion] 
initiatives." 
The Petitioner's contributions are admirable. In the context of petitions for national interest waivers 
in the employment-based, second-preference category, see section 203(b )(2)(B)(i) ofthe Act, we have 
found that STEM teaching "has substantial merit in relation to U.S. educational interests." Matter of 
Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884, 893 (AAO 2016). But, as SCOPS' found, the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated that her contributions had "major significance" in her field. 
When determining whether a petitioner's contributions have major significance in a field, USCIS 
typically looks for: 
3 
• Objective documentary evidence of the significance of their contribution to the field; 
• Documentary evidence that people throughout the field consider a petitioner's work important; 
• Testimony or support letters from experts discussing a petitioner's contributions of major 
significance; 
• Evidence that a petitioner's major significant contribution has provoked widespread 
commentary in the field or has been widely cited; and 
• Evidence of the implementation of a petitioner's work by others. 
Golani v. Allen, No. 22-10202, 2023 WL 4874767, at *4-5 (E.D. Mich. July 31, 2023). 
The support letters describe the Petitioner as a talented, passionate, and devoted teacher. They indicate 
that she has improved students' reading and writing skills, provided on-going support to new teachers, 
raised awareness of the importance of public education, created a safe and welcoming school 
environment, and advocated for students' and educators' needs. The letters, however, do not explain 
the significance of the Petitioner's contributions to her broader field. Most of the letters are from 
educational professionals with whom she has worked. 3 The record lacks independent, objective 
evidence that her contributions have had major significance in her field. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 
558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 187-88 (D.N.J. 2021) (citations omitted) (stating that to demonstrate major 
significance, "the impact should extend beyond the petitioner's locality or a handful of participants in 
the field"); Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, No. 3: 18-cv-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. 
May 31, 2019) ( citation omitted) ( affirming users' finding that major significance in the field "requires 
substantial influence beyond one's employers, clients, or customers.") 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that her contributions have had major significance in her field. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary criterion. 
2. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the[ir] authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
users first determines whether a petitioner wrote scholarly articles in their field. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)( I), Criteria 6. If so, the Agency then determines whether a publication 
qualifies as a professional publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. Id. 
Relevant factors include, for a professional journal, the intended audience and, for a major trade 
publication or major medium, a publication's relative circulation or readership. Id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that her co-authorship of a peer-reviewed, international research 
article satisfies this criterion. The record contains an abstract ofthe August 2023 article about distance 
learning changes during the COVrD-19 pandemic and lists the Petitioner as a co-author. Online 
information about the journal that published the article identifies it as an international, peer-reviewed 
publication focusing on education, learning, and teaching. Its intended audience is teachers and other 
educational professionals.

3. Commandment of a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien 
has commanded a high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted a copy of her 2023 IRS Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement, indicating that, 
as an elementary school teacher, she earned $79,166 that year. She also provided U.S. Bureau of 
Labor Statistics wage information showing that her salary exceeded the highest listed wage level for 
her occupation and geographic area in 2023: $69,730 a year. She therefore argues that her salary is 
high relative to others in her field. 
But other evidence of record indicates that the Petitioner's salary is not as comparatively high. In 
response to SCOPS' request for additional evidence, she submitted U.S. Department of Labor 
Occupational Information Network (O*NET) information indicating that, in her geographic area in 
2023, the top 10% of elementary school teachers earned at least $84,010 a year, more than the $79,166 
she earned that year. Thus, this wage information does not establish that the Petitioner has a 
sufficiently "high salary" in relation to others in her field. See Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 
10 (D. Mass. 2023), appeal docketed, No. 23-2029 (1st Cir. Dec. 6, 2023) (affirming USCIS' decision 
that a petitioner's salary "was not comparatively high because his salary was below the 90th 
percentile"). 
The Petitioner also argues that her salary significantly exceeds those of elementary school teachers in 
her home country of the Philippines. Because she works and resides in the United States as only a 
temporary nonimmigrant, she contends that SCOPS should have considered wage data from her home 
country. But, as SCOPS found, USCIS policy requires comparison to foreign wages only when a 
petitioner works outside the United States. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 9 
("Officers evaluate persons working outside of the United States based on the wage statistics or 
comparable evidence relevant to the applicable work location.") Thus, comparison of the Petitioner's 
U.S. wages to wages of those in similar jobs in her home country is inappropriate. 
A preponderance of the evidence does not demonstrate the Petitioner's receipt of a high salary in 
relation to others in her field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary 
requirement. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met two evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits 
determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not reach and hereby reserve 
consideration of her arguments regarding her final qualifications as an alien with extraordinary ability.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner demonstrated her authorship of a scholarly article in her field. But she has not met the 
requisite amount of evidentiary requirements for the requested immigrant visa category. We will 
therefore affirm the petition's denial.
