Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a financial advisor and coach, seeks classification under the employment-based, first 
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate her receipt of a major internationally recognized award or her satisfaction of any of the 
classification's ten alternate evidentiary requirements. On appeal, the Petitioner states that she 
disagrees with SCOPS' decision and reasserts her eligibility. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable 
evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24, F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner sought to provide financial consultancy and coaching services in the United States as 
an individual ofextraordinary ability. Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt 
of a major, internationally recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory 
criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet any 
of the evidentiary requirements for the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, the Petitioner 
contends that she satisfied the following six criteria: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field of 
endeavor; 
• Membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought; 
• Published material about the individual; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in the field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field, and 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments with distinguished 
reputations. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (vi), and (viii). 
1. Documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or 
awards for excellence in the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
We first determine whether the alien is the recipient of prizes or awards, and then whether the award 
is nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence in the alien's field 
of endeavor. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The

record contains certificates ofrecognition for the Petitioner's awards in her managerial positions with her 
employer, a bank branch, and as a team member at her branch. She states the following concerning her 
eligibility under this criterion: 
Chapter II, Part B ofthe USCIS Policy Manual confirms that"[ c ]ertain awards from well­
known national institutions or well-known professional associations" are qualifying 
awards to meet this criterion. The Manual further explains that "[a]n award open to 
members of a well-known national institution or professional organization may be 
nationally recognized." 
All ofthe above indicates the beneficiary's excellence in [the] financial and banking field. 
Accordingly, the beneficiary satisfies "receipt of nationally and internationally 
[recognized] prizes, distinguished reputation and excellence in the field of endeavor" 
criterion. 
The Petitioner appears to assert here that the awards she received, which were conferred by her employer 
and by a financial institution to whom she served as a representative of her employer, should be 
considered nationally recognized because those institutions are nationally recognized. While the record 
indicates that these institutions are nationally recognized, the Petitioner has not sufficiently demonstrated 
that that the awards are nationally recognized for excellence in the field of endeavor. Relevant 
considerations regarding whether the awards were granted based upon excellence in the Petitioner's 
field of endeavor include the criteria used to grant the awards, the national or international significance 
of the awards in the field, the number of awardees, and any limitations on competitors. The Petitioner 
has not provided details concerning the criteria used to confer the awards or their significance within the 
field offinance. Without additional information concerning the Petitioner's awards, it is not clear whether 
they qualify as nationally recognized awards for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that she meets the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
2. Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification is 
sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted documentation showing that she was designated as a Professional Certified 
Coach by the International Coach Federation (ICF), an Accredited Executive Coach by the Association 
for Coaching (AC), and a Practitioner by the EMCC Global Individual Team Coaching Accreditation 
(ITCA). She submitted documentation from these organizations describing instructions for joining and 
training programs to earn coaching certifications, but this material does not reference requirements related 
to outstanding achievements of members, nor does it reference any level of recognized national or 
international expertise ofthose judging a member's qualifications. For example, while the ITCA bylaws 
describe requirements that include paying dues, actively working as a coach, and obtaining credentialed 
membership through an application with the organization, they do not address whether those conferring 
membership are recognized national or international experts in their discipline or field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner reiterates her qualifications based on the evidence ofrecord. She also references 
for the first time her involvement with several financial and business entities in Russia: a financial

4. Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work 
of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought. 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
In response to the RFE, the Petitioner submitted a letter from her employer attesting to her membership 
on a committee involved in the selection of certain employees. The letter states, without further 
explanation, that she "is an active member of the [bank's committee] that involves in [sic] selection 
of talented employees and their inclusion as employee reserve." SCOPS determined that the letter did 
not establish that the Petitioner was invited to judge the work of others or that she actually judged the 
work of others. Although the Petitioner asserts on appeal that "selecting talented co-workers for 
company's [sic] cadre reserve constitutes 'peer review"' and that the evaluation of future employees 
is evidence of her expertise, the letter alone does not include sufficient information concerning her 
capacity as a judge or the specific work of the individuals who were judged in order to demonstrate 
that the Petitioner meets the requirements of the criterion. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-0 1471-
LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) (upholding "that there were no details about 
what event the plaintiff was judging, who he was judging, or the basis for [the plaintiff's] knowledge 
[in her letter] of the Federation's authority regarding the plaintiffs judging history"). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that she meets the plain language requirements of this criterion.

6. Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
In evaluating whether an individual has met the requirements of this criterion, USCIS first determines 
whether the person has performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, establishment, or a 
division or department of an organization or establishment. Second, USCIS determines whether the 
organization or establishment, or the department or division for which the person holds or held a 
leading or critical role, has a distinguished reputation. 7 
The Petitioner submitted a business plan, tax information, and articles of incorporation for her 
proposed business, as well as an undated letter from a company stating that she currently provides 
financial support services to the company and that it is considering offering her a permanent position. 
This documentation, however, does not contain information concerning a leading or critical role that 
the Petitioner has performed or show how either her business or the company that has received her 
services is an organization or establishment having a distinguished reputation. 
The Petitioner also submitted letters of support from two financial entities to which the Petitioner 
purportedly served as a representative of her employer. While the record indicates that these entities, 
as well as the Petitioner's employer, may be establishments with distinguished reputations, these 
letters alone are not sufficient to demonstrate that the Petitioner meets this criterion; the letters 
generally compliment the Petitioner's skills and the positive results of her provision of services to 
them in her role as a representative, but, as SCOPS discussed in denying the petition, the letters do not 
explain how her role was leading or critical for any aspect of their organizations' operations or 
achievements.

a bank that previously employed her early in her career. The letter praises her implementation of a

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 ( 1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
