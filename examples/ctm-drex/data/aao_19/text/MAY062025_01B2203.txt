Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a machine learning engineer, requests classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner met two of the 
requested category's ten evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits 
determination. On appeal, he contends that he satisfied the following additional criteria: 
• Original contributions of major significance in his field; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), (ix). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he has met the requisite minimum number of evidentiary requirements by 
submitting evidence of original contributions of major significance to his field. We will therefore 
withdraw SCOPS ' decision and remand the matter for a final merits determination and entry of a new 
decision consistent with the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )( 1 )( A )(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(implementing a two-step extraordinary ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner, an Iranian native and citizen, earned a bachelor of science degree 
in computer engineering from a university in his home country and worked in Iran for about a year as 
a software engineer. He decided to return to school and attained master ofscience and doctoral degrees 
in computer science in the United States. Since May 2024, a U.S. technology company has employed 
him as a machine learning engineer. Machine learning is an artificial intelligence field enabling 
computers to learn from data without explicit programming. Machine learning involves algorithms 
that can analyze data, identify patterns, and make predictions and decisions. 
The Petitioner specializes in "deep learning," a machine learning subfield using artificial neural 
networks to process and analyze information. He conducts deep learning research, focusing on 
mitigating unintended consequences ofdeep models such as hallucination, toxicity, and non-factuality. 
He works with diffusion models, vision transformers, and model agnostic inversion. He seeks to 
continue his research in the United States. 
B. The Evidentiary Requirements 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner met the following evidentiary requirements: 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in the field; and 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field. 
See 8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi).

1. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has made original 
contributions in their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. If so, the 
Agency then determines whether the original contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
For example, published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its 
importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly 
cited relative to others' work in that field, may be probative of the significance of the 
person's contributions to the field of endeavor. 
Id. 
SCOPS acknowledged the originality of the Petitioner's research. But SCOPS found insufficient 
proof that the work had major significance to his field. SCOPS concluded that citations to the 
Petitioner's research "do not reflect that your work is singled out as a major significance in the field; 
instead, your work is utilized as background information to the authors' work." 
The record does not support SCOPS' findings. The Petitioner submitted evidence of nine published 
research papers in his field that he authored or co-authored between 2022 and 2024 and letters from 
four experts in the field discussing his work. As the Petitioner argues: 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the 
person's contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed 
original contributions of major significance, particularly when the record includes 
documentation corroborating the claimed significance. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. 
One expert, a senior staff research scientist for a U.S. technology company, stated that he and his co­
researcher cited the Petitioner's work on "cold diffusion." He said that they "referred to [the 
Petitioner]'s cold diffusion general model to form the basis ofour study and we also used the results 
of his work to examine and compare the efficacy of our own sampling's method and inference 
algorithm." (emphasis added). Thus, contrary to SCOPS' finding, evidence indicates use of the 
Petitioner's work as more than "background information." 
The same expert, who stated that he has never collaborated with the Petitioner, also discussed the 
Petitioner's development of a new algorithm for computer generative diffusion models, which are 
3 
often used for image generation, restoration, or transformation. He stated that the Petitioner 
introduced a new diffusion model that, unlike prior models, does not rely solely on "I t' 
and supports a broader array of degeneration processes. The expert stated: "This opens new avenues 
for the development of deep learning diffusion models with sampling algorithms that have a stronger 
mathematical foundation." 
Another expert, a U.S. computer science professor who served as the Petitioner's advisor, discussed 
the importance of the Petitioner's research on deep learning models. He stated: 
There is a growing reliance on machine learning systems in people's everyday lives, 
and while there is a great deal of research aimed at developing and optimizing new 
deep learning models and algorithms, limited knowledge regarding these models' 
underlying processes hinders scientists from exploiting their full capabilities. [The 
Petitioner] is dedicated to addressing these gaps in knowledge to elucidate the 
fundamental underpinnings of emerging models and support their optimization and 
application in deep learning systems. 
Thus, the record contains evidence explaining the significance of the Petitioner's research. 
SCOPS also found that, "[ w ]hile the petitioner's citations, both individually and collectively, show 
that the field has noticed the petitioner's work, the evidence does not establish that such rates of 
citation are sufficient to demonstrate a level of interest in the field." 
As previously indicated, documentation that research has been highly cited relative to others' work in 
that field "may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of 
endeavor." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 5. The Petitioner submitted evidence that, at 
the time of the petition's filing, 2023 computer science research papers averaged 1.61 citations. The 
record shows that, at that time, one of the Petitioner's 2023 papers had received 207 citations. Thus, 
his research generated widespread interest in the field and was highly cited relative to others' work in 
the field. 
Further, the Petitioner submitted articles stating that, in computer research, papers accepted by 
academic conferences are as important as articles published in journals. The Petitioner submitted 
evidence that five top-rated conferences accepted his papers. The senior staff research scientist stated: 
"Conferences such as these require all article submissions to undergo an extensive peer-review process 
and only accept pioneering work of the highest quality and integrity." Thus, the Petitioner's 
conference articles also indicate that his research has major importance in the field. 
The Petitioner has submitted evidence of original contributions of major significance in his field. We 
will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
2. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
The 
Petitioner has met the requisite minimum number ofevidentiary requirements. We therefore need 
not consider his arguments that he submitted evidence that he commanded a high salary or 
significantly high remuneration relative to others in his field. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 
4 
25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues 
unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
C. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has met the requisite minimum number of evidentiary criteria, USCIS must now 
conduct a final merits determination. SCOPS did not make such a finding. Rather than make one in 
the frrst instance, we will remand the matter for a final merits determination. 
On remand, SCOPS must determine whether the Petitioner demonstrated sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognition of his achievements in the field identifying him as one of that 
small percentage who has risen to the field's very top. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F .2(B)(2). SCOPS should consider any potentially relevant evidence of record, even if it does not fit 
one of the regulatory criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence. Id. The Petitioner's 
burden of proof includes explaining evidence's significance and how the materials demonstrate his 
sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval or 
denial should hinge on the evidence's type and quality. Id. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of original contributions of major significance in his field. USCIS 
must now conduct a final merits determination. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for a final merits determination 
and entry of a new decision consistent with the foregoing analysis.
