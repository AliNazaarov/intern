Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a researcher in information systems, requests classification under the employment­
based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). 
Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their 
fields . Section 203(b )( I )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner 
satisfied three initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, he did not show as required his 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 
103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met three (published material about the alien in professional or 
major trade publications or other major media relating to the aliens' work in the field under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii), judging either individually or on a panel the work of others in the same or an allied 
field under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), and authorship of scholarly articles in the field in professional 
or major trade publication or other major media under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi)) of the five claimed 
categories of evidence. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he fulfills the three remaining claimed 
criteria. Any ground of ineligibility that is not raised on appeal is waived. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 
l&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 l&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012)). 
We will not disturb SCOPS' determination that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vi). But, for the reasons discussed below, we conclude that the record does not 
demonstrate the Petitioner's eligibility under the criterion contained at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) 
pertaining to published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media relating to the aliens' work in the field. So, we will withdraw it. Additionally we, like 
SCOPS, conclude the record does not support the Petitioner met any of the remaining claimed criteria 
contained at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) pertaining to evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance or at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(x) pertaining to evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other 
significantly high remuneration for services in relation to others in his field. Consequently, we 
conclude the Petitioner has not satisfied at least three of the 10 initial evidentiary criteria listed at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and reserve final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). Nevertheless, for the reasons 
discussed below, the record does not show sustained national or international acclaim or that the 
Petitioner is among the small percentage at the top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d 
at 1110-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) and (3)).

C. Remuneration 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the person's salary or remuneration is 
high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
ManualF.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. USCIS determines whether the USCIS does 
not interpret the phrase "has commanded" to mean that the person must have already earned such 
salary or remuneration in order to meet the criterion. Rather, a credible contract or job offer showing 
prospective salary or remuneration may establish that the person has been able to command such 
compensation. Id. 
Evidence regarding whether a petitioner's compensation is high relative to what of others working in 
the field can include compensation surveys and organizational justifications so long as the description 
ofthe occupation matches, the validity ofthe survey is credible and reliable, and the location, currency, 
and salary rate being measured are comparable. Id. 
In support of his commanded salary, the Petitioner submitted two letters from his employer, his 
paystubs and bank statements, salary data from the Office of Foreign Labor Certification, and Bureau 
ofLabor Statistics, a report from Zippia, advertisements for purportedly similar positions nationwide, 
and an annual survey from cupa-hr. 
We conclude the evidence in the record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's compensation is 
high relative to others in his field. The data from the Office of Foreign Labor Certification contains 
salary information for Business Teachers, Postsecondary and Computer Information Research 
Scientists. The job description for Business Teachers, Postsecondary contemplates teaching courses 
in business administration and management. This is different from the information systems field the 
Petitioner claims he occupies space within. The job description for Computer and Information 
Research Scientists does not provide for teaching. So, it is not evident how the Office of Foreign 
Labor Certification salary data relates as a point of comparison to the salary for the position the 
Petitioner occupies. 
And the Bureau of Labor Statistics data the Petitioner presents relate to wages for the entire state of 
Michigan, and not the narrower metropolitan statistical area within which the Petitioner provides his 
services. Or in other words, a broad location like the whole State of Michigan is not comparable to a 
smaller city or county metropolitan statistical area. The advertisements for purportedly similar 
positions nationwide suffer from the same infirmity. 
The survey reports issues by Zippia and cupa-hr do not contain sufficient evidence of the job 
descriptions accompanying the job titles for which salary data is provided. Moreover, the scant details 
of the survey respondents do not provide sufficient information to evaluate relevant aspects of the 
population for comparison. 
The regulatory requirement that the commanded salary be high "in relation to others in the field" is 
interpreted as allowing comparisons to those employed in the same occupation at a similar level. 
See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a professional golfer's 
earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dept. of Homeland Sec., 420 F. 
App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is 
7 
not a comparison to others in the field); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) 
( considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-
45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) ( comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
So, we are unable to determine if the Petitioner's salary is high compared to other working in the field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -
- F.Supp.3d---, 2025 WL 1508028, at* 10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes 
a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required 
to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Regardless, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 
I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of 
accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087 at *l (stating that EB-1 
visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount ofindividuals with extraordinary 
ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the 
required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work 
in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. Whilst the Petitioner need not establish that there is 
no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to 
demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
