Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, which describes itself as a "technology company," seeks to classify the Beneficiary as 
an individual of extraordinary ability in business. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant 
visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record satisfies at least three ofthe 10 initial evidentiary 
criteria but it does not show sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that the 
Beneficiary is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner, which describes the Beneficiary as an expert in the field of customer experience, 
intends to employ the Beneficiary as its "Sr. Director, Global Merchant Engagement and Growth, 
USCIS concluded that the record satisfies at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x); however, USCIS determined that the record does not show sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrate that the Beneficiary is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of the 
Act; 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2)-(3)). 
Black's Law Dictionary defines "sustain" as "to support or maintain, especially over a long period of 
time" or "to persist in making (an effort) over a long period oftime." Sustain, Black's Law Dictionary 
(11th ed. 2019). 
As USCIS observed in its decision, evidence in this case pertaining to the respective criteria is limited 
to few instances, shortly before the Petitioner filed the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien 
Workers, in June 2025 and the evidence does not show that the Beneficiary has sustained national or 
international acclaim. For example, the Beneficiary was a finalist for one award in 2021 and she was 
included among 25 selectees for recognition in 2025. 1 The Beneficiary became a member of the

Even setting aside the short proximity between the evidence and the Form I-140 filing date, the record

More generally, the Petitioner does not establish characteristics of those at the very top of the field of 
endeavor, to contextualize information about the Beneficiary in the record. For example, the record 
does not elaborate on the number and types of awards that those at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor 
have received, in order to contextualize the small number of awards the Beneficiary received or was 
shortlisted for shortly before she filed the Form I-140. As another example, the record does not show 
that the Beneficiary's membership in one professional association and one business development 
council is significant compared to the number and nature of association memberships that those at the 
very top of the field of endeavor hold, nor does it show that she has sustained acclaim-whether 
nationally or internationally-by virtue of her membership that began shortly before the Petitioner 
filed the Form I-140. Similarly, the record also does not show how the limited extent of media 
coverage about the Beneficiary compares to the extent of media coverage that individuals at the very 
top of the field of endeavor receive. 
Relating to the Beneficiary's participation as a judge of the work of others, an evaluation of the 
significance of her experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence indicates the required 
extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22. The 
Petitioner did not establish that the Beneficiary's few, recent judging instances-for one event in 2022, 
2023, and 2025, respectively-contributed to a finding that she has a career of acclaimed work in the 
field or indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not 
show, for example, how the Beneficiary's judging experience compares to others at the very top of the 
field. In addition, the Petitioner did not establish, for instance, that the Beneficiary garnered wide 
attention from the field based on her evaluation work. 
Based on the foregoing, the record does not establish that the Beneficiary has sustained national or 
international acclaim and that she is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(2)-(3)).

III. CONCLUSION 
The totality of the record does not support a conclusion that the Beneficiary has the acclaim and 
recognition required for the classification sought. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. USCIS has 
long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the 
"extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). The 
Petitioner has not shown that the significance of the Beneficiary's work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered 
national or international acclaim in the field and that she is one of the small percentage who has risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; see also 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
