Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a competitive swimmer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the record establishes that the Petitioner satisfied 
at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required, the totality of the evidence did not show, 
in a final merits determination, that the Petitioner has received sustained national or international 
acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in 
certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

We are not persuaded by the Petitioner's alternate argument that USCIS' use of a two-step analysis in 
extraordinary ability adjudications is unlawful based on Mukherji v. Miller, an unpublished district 
court decision. In contrast to the precedential authority of a published decision of the U.S. Courts of 
Appeals in matters arising in their jurisdiction, we are not bound to follow a decision of a United 
States district court. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). While we may consider a 
court's reasoning when it is properly before us, unpublished opinions are not considered binding 
precedent. See Salamalekis v. Comm. ofSoc. Sec. , 221 F.3d 828,833 (6th Cir. 2000); see also Suchite­
Salguero v. Att'y Gen. United States, 147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned 
over and over again that non-precedential opinions carry little to no weight for future cases.").

Nevertheless, we do agree with the Petitioner's primary argument that USCIS did not sufficiently 
consider the record in its entirety in the second step analysis. USCIS did not discuss the specific 
evidence of the Petitioner's awards, memberships, published material, and claimed performance in a 
leading or critical role and explain why that evidence was insufficient to establish the Petitioner's 
sustained national or international acclaim or that he has risen to the top of the field of endeavor. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3 d at 1115. 
This omission is particularly evident as it relates to the Petitioner's claimed performance in a leading 
or critical role. As noted above, because USCIS concluded that the Petitioner established the other 
three claimed evidentiary criteria, it was not necessary to provide a separate analysis as to whether the 
Petitioner established the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). Nevertheless, USCIS should have 
analyzed the totality of the evidence, including the evidence submitted in support of this criterion, in 
its final merits determination, which it did not. Regarding this criterion, the Petitioner submitted 
evidence that he has had a leading role as team captain ofthe~men's swimming team and that his 
role has been critical because, among other reasons, his performance in particular race events was 
determinative in his team's finishing wins. 
Finally, USCIS primarily rests its denial on the conclusion that the Petitioner's national or international 
acclaim has not been sustained. However, USCIS does not sufficiently explain why the Petitioner's 
five-year career, starting from 18 years of age and appearing to maintain a trajectory of increasing 
recognition and accomplishment through the present, is not sufficient to be "sustained." In 
determining whether an individual has enjoyed "sustained" national or international acclaim, USCIS 
should consider that such claim must be maintained. However, the term sustained does not imply an 
age limit. A petitioner may be very young or early in his or her career and still be able to show 
sustained acclaim. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. 
An officer must fully explain the reasons for denying a visa petition to allow the petitioner a fair 
opportunity to contest the decision and to allow us an opportunity for meaningful appellate review. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(i); see also Matter of M-P-, 20 I&N Dec. 786 (BIA 1994) (finding that a 
decision must fully explain the reasons for denying a motion to allow the respondent a meaningful 
opportunity to challenge the determination on appeal). On remand, USCIS should consider the 
evidence in the record in its totality to determine whether it is supportive of the Petitioner's sustained 
national or international acclaim. 
C. Continuing to Work in the Area of Extraordinary Ability & Substantially Prospectively Benefit to 
the United States 
To qualify as a person with extraordinary ability, he or she must intend to continue to work in the area 
of his or her expertise, and his or her entry must substantially benefit the United States prospectively.

In support ofthe Petitioner's intention to continue to work in the United States in his area of expertise, 
the Petitioner submitted a personal statement. In support of the substantial prospective benefit of the 
Petitioner's entry to the United States, he submitted a letter of support fromc=J, an Olympic gold 
medalist and current member of the U.S. National Swimming team. 
In the Petitioner's statement, he affirms that he is committed to a rigorous U.S.-based competition 
schedule and that following the NCAA season he will focus on qualifying and competing at upcoming 
European and World Aquatics Championships, culminating in the 2028 Olympic Games. He describes 
his intention to continue his career and participate in swim clubs and training camps in the United 
States. The letter of support from~corroborates these plans and describes the increased visibility 
and prestige that a competitive swimmer at the Petitioner's caliber brings to the sport of swimming. 
We acknowledge the Petitioner's intention to continue in the sport; however, the record reflects that 
the Petitioner is in his senior year at~ and he may soon leave collegiate athletics. Therefore, 
USCIS may wish to request additional evidence regarding the Petitioner's plans following his 
collegiate career in the United States. While the requirement to "substantially benefit" the United 
States has been interpreted broadly, USCIS should look at how the Petitioner's post-collegiate plans 
would be of substantial prospective benefit to the United States. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953 (Assoc. Comm. 1994) (a golfer of the individual's caliber will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States given the popularity of the sport), see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at 
F.2(A)(3). 
For example, the record contains letters of support from senior I Istaff and~I---~

their respective swimming programs. However, due to their positions as representatives of these 
organizations, the letter writers do not provide specific support for the Petitioner's post-collegiate 
plans in the United States, their feasibility, and their substantial prospective benefit. On remand, if 
USCIS is not able to determine, based on the evidence in the record, that the Petitioner's entry would 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States, it may request additional evidence to meet this 
requirement. 
III. CONCLUSION 
On remand, USCIS should consider whether the totality of the evidence in the record demonstrates 
the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim and his place among the small percentage 
at the top of his field, consistent with the analysis above. If USCIS needs further information to 
determine whether the Petitioner has established any of the eligibility requirements, including the 
substantial prospective benefit of his entry to the United States, it may request additional evidence. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
