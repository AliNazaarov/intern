Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an information technology manager from Argentina, seeks classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not 
establish either that he made original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field, that he displayed his work in the field at artistic 
exhibitions or showcases, that he performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation, or that he satisfied at least three of the 10 initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics 
which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCJS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010).

who plans to work at a Delaware company where he will work with the company's IT team to test,

The Petitioner was the leader ofa software team that developed software called D for the Argentine

Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business­
related contributions ofmajor significance in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The Petitioner presented letters stating that he led a team that created a system for the Argentine social 
security agency to easily find and evaluate information. We acknowledge a scholarly article praising 
this intellectual property and note that the Petitioner led 40 internal technicians and 60 external 
technicians who collaborated. The provided article states that the digitization technology the

been unusually influential, highly acclaimed throughout the field, or has otherwise risen to the level 
of original contribution of major significance. Original intellectual property is not necessarily 
evidence of a track record of success with some degree of influence over the field as a whole. See 
Matter ofNew York State Department ofTransportation, 22 I&N Dec. 215,221 n. 7, (Comm'r 11998). 
Rather, the significance of the innovation must be determined on a case-by-case basis. Id. In the 
instant case, the Petitioner showed the technology was adopted by three entities and the institution that 
employed him for nearly a decade. However, he has not shown that his work garnered any meaningful 
interest at thel !conferences that would constitute a contribution ofmajor significance in the field.

"did not speak in every conference from 2013 to 2018, but did give presentations at several of the 
conferences during that time."I I does not have specific records of when or what the Petitioner 
presented and there is no evidence pertaining to the reception of his presentations atl I 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of major 
significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed 
significance. Here, the Petitioner's letters do not contain specific, detailed information explaining the 
unusual influence or high impact his work has had on the overall field to establish that it has been of 
major significance. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 
(PAE), 2024 WL 69056, *8 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding the petitioner's testimonial letter mostly 
consisted of conclusory statements about the significance of his work and his potential). The letters 
of references' statements and article relating to the aforementioned intellectual property are not 
sufficient to show that the Petitioner's specific work has widely influenced the digitization of 
government or has otherwise risen to the level of contributions of major significance in the field. 
Without sufficient evidence demonstrating that his particular work constitutes original contributions 
of major significance in the field, the Petitioner has not established that he meets this criterion.

Evidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The Petitioner claims eligibility under this criterion based on 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), which states "[i]f 
the above standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit 
comparable evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision provides 
petitioners with the opportunity to submit comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, if 
it is determined that the evidentiary criteria described in the regulations do not readily apply to the 
person's occupation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. When evaluating such 
comparable evidence, we consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's 
occupation and, if not, whether the evidence is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
Id. 
The Petitioner claims that unless one is an artist, the only way one can meet this standard is through 
comparable evidence. Thus, giving presentations at various conferences and organizations are the 
equivalent of this criterion, as long as one can prove that they are giving a presentation of their own 
original work. 
The Petitioner does not cite any evidence in the record in support of these assertions. The burden of 
proof rests with the Petitioner, who should submit "relevant, probative, and credible evidence" to 
establish his claims. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. Additionally, the plain language 
of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) permits the petitioner to submit comparable "evidence" to establish 
eligibility. The Petitioner's unsupported statements are insufficient to establish that this impact would 
be truly comparable to the display ofwork at artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra. 
Furthermore, as discussed supra, the Petitioner did not produce the original programs of ~I-~ 
conferences where he claims to have presented and the updated letter from the Secretary of the 
organization that organized.___.....lwas non-specific and did not specify when and on what specific 
topic(s) the Petitioner presented. The AAO questioned the authenticity of the evidence submitted 
because the evidence in the record conflicted with online archives for the conferences, specificllly the I 
Petitioner's claims to have been on conference committees, and that he presented his work at the 
conferences in the years 2013, 2014, 2016, and 2018. It is the Petitioner's responsibility and burden 
to resolve any inconsistencies in the record with independent objective evidence and attempts to 
explain or reconcile such inconsistencies will not suffice without competent objective evidence 
pointing to where the truth, in fact, lies. See Matter of Ho, 19 I&N Dec. at 582. The Petitioner's 
response to the NOID does not meet this standard. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfies this criterion, including through the 
submission of comparable evidence.

B. Remaining Claimed Evidentiary Criteria 
Although the Petitioner maintains eligibility for one additional criterion on appeal-relating to 
performing in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished 
reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)-the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for any of the 
evidentiary criteria discussed above. He therefore cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of 
meeting at least three of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Therefore, we reserve the issue of 
whether he has satisfied the additional criterion he claims on appeal. See INSv. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 
24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" 
on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that s/he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa"). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
The Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that he is one of the small percentage who 
has risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act; see also 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2).
