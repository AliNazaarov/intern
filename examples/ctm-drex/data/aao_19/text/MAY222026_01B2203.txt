Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a cricket player, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification 
makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their 
field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at 
least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits 
determination did not show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the 
Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The instant petition was filed on May 12, 2023. The record indicates that the Petitioner is a former cricket 
player of the I !Cricket Team who competed internationally from 2001 to 2007. He 
continued to play cricket at the domestic level inl luntil he formally announced his retirement 
in 2020. The Petitioner claims that his retirement was temporary due to COVID-19 pandemic and he 
resumed his competitive playing after several months. Since 2023, the Petitioner has been playing cricket 
in various I Icricket clubs in New Jersey and New York. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied the following six initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), 
(ii), (iii), (iv), (vii), and (ix): 
• Receipt of lesser recognized awards or prizes for excellence in the field; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Published materials about the person; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work; 
• Display of work at artistic showcases or exhibitions; and 
• Commanding a high salary in relation to others in the field. 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met three initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (receipt of 
less recognized awards or prizes), (iii) (published materials), and (iv) (participation as a judge of 
others' work). However, in conducting a final merits determination, SCOPS concluded that the record 
did not demonstrate the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim and that he was one of 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. We agree.

as a cricketer in his home country" because his championship and winner titles "ranged from 2002 to

We first note that the Petitioner initially described his job as "playing and coaching the game of 
cricket" on Form 1-140, Immigrant Petitioner for Alien Workers, and claimed that he has an offer of

cricket players in the US." We acknowledge the possibility of an alien's extraordinary claim in more 
than one field, such as a coach and a cricket player, but the Petitioner must demonstrate "by clear 
evidence that the alien is coming to the United States to continue work in the area of expertise." See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). On appeal, the Petitioner focuses only on his intention to be a cricket player 
and does not raise the issue ofhis role as a cricket coach. As such, we consider this issue to be waived 
by the Petitioner. See Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 693 n.1 (BIA 2023) (noting that issues not raised 
on appeal are waived); see also Matter ofO-R-E, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021). 
Here, the Petitioner has received awards and prizes as a competitive cricket player, judged youths' 
abilities in cricket playing, and provided evidence of published materials about his cricket career, his 
membership, his salary as a brand ambassador, and Y ouTube videos of his cricket games. Although 
we acknowledge that the Petitioner has gained acclaim for himself by playing cricket on the
I !national team, the record does not sufficiently establish that the Petitioner's sustained 
national or international acclaim and recognition in the field of expertise, placing him at the very top 
of the field of endeavor, as discussed below. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B); 
see also Hamal v. US. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 
(D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 
2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (holding both sustained acclaim and recognition of 
achievements must be shown).

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 3 The Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements are

2025) ( stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of 
individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of 
his work is indicative ofthe required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with 
a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner 
need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the 
record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
