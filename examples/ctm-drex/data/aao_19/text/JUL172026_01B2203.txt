Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a ceramic fabricator who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did she demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner describes herself as "a distinguished contemporary artist, researcher, and educator 
whose innovative practice blends cultural heritage with modern fabrication technologies, redefining 
the conceptual and technical possibilities of ceramic art." 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two ofthe criteria relating to judging 
and display of the Petitioner's work, but that she had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes 
or awards, membership, published material, original contributions, the authorship ofscholarly articles, 
or performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that she meets each of 
the evidentiary criteria in which SCOPS issued an adverse determination. After reviewing all the 
evidence in the record, we conclude she has not demonstrated she is eligible for this immigrant 
classification.

On appeal, the Petitioner states that SCOPS "does not identify a basis in the regulatory text for 
requiring separate proof that a juried competition award is itself nationally recognized, beyond the 
competition's own documentation of its national scope and selection criteria." First, a national pool 
of candidates is not determinative on this issue as the regulation requires that the award be nationally 
or internationally recognized and selection from a national pool of candidates does not necessarily 
impart national significance to an award. And SCOPS does not need to identify the basis to require 
"proof that a juried competition award is itself nationally recognized" because the justification lies 
within the regulation's plain language for "nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards." 
Also, even if the Petitioner were to establish that the competition itself was nationally recognized, that 
form of acknowledgement does not automatically impute such recognition to every prize or award 
issued during the event. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (finding 
the focus is on the award, not the awarding organization and that evidence about a festival itself does 
not describe the criteria for the award or speak to the award's recognition); see also Visinscaia v. 
Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013). A prize or an award does not categorically gamer 
national or international recognition from the competition in which it is awarded, nor is it automatically 
derived from the individual or group that issued the award. Rather, national and international 
recognition results through the awareness of the accolade in the eyes of the field nationally or 
internationally. USCIS policy speaks to this issue stating: 
Examples of qualifying awards may include, but are not limited to: 
• Certain awards from well-known national institutions or well-known professional 
associations. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. We highlight 
two important qualifiers within that bullet point: (1) certain awards; and (2) well-known. 
In the context in which a petitioner relies on the reputation of the issuing entity or the event, the term 
"certain awards" in the Policy Manual is best construed as referring to the top prizes or honors from a 
nationally or internationally recognized competition or institution. In some cases, receiving such a 
high accolade may be enough to meet the requirements of this criterion. However, a petitioner must 
provide probative evidence showing that the competition or institution is well known in the field, as 
well as material showing the significance oftheir achievement emanating from that event. This should 
be demonstrated through specific means; for example but not limited to, national- or 
international-level media coverage. 
Additionally, unsupported conclusory letters from those in the Petitioner's field are not sufficient 
evidence that a particular prize or award-or a competition or institution-is nationally or 
internationally recognized. Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 184 (upholding a letter describing the award 
criteria was insufficient to show that the award had national or international recognition). Probative 
3 
evidence that a prize, award, competition, or institution is nationally or internationally recognized is 
generally material from sources besides the issuing entity. Id. at 183. Because the record lacks such 
material, we conclude that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language 
requirements of this criterion. 
2. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii).

language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL

4. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134). The regulatory phrase "major 
significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. ?reap, 586 U.S. 392, 415 
(2019) (finding that every word and every provision in a statute is to be given effect and none should 
needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or to have no 
consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided her published work, articles, presentations, and support letters among other 
evidence. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. On 
appeal, the Petitioner addresses support letters, alleges SCOPS did not specifically state what evidence 
she could provide to demonstrate eligibility, and that one of her peer reviewed works was of major 
significance simply for being published in that publication. 
After reviewing the entire record, to include the arguments and evidence submitted on appeal, we 
agree with SCOPS' ultimate determination under this criterion. See Matter ofJ-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 253, 
254 (BIA 2025) ( citing Matter ofBurbano, 20 I&N Dec. 872, 874 (BIA 1994); see also Giday v. INS, 
113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (noting that the practice of adopting and affirming the decision 
below "is not only common practice, but universally accepted"); see also Garland v. Ming Dai, 593 
U.S. 357, 370-71 (2021) (noting an administrative appellate body's act of adopting and affirming the 
below decision is legitimate, incorporating the lower decision's analysis and reasoning on the issues 
into the appellate determination).

6. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
qualifying role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization 
or establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Evidence of a leading role should show that 
the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help top 
establish that a role was leading. A leading role should be apparent by its position in the overall 
hierarchy and the role's matching duties. Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 
1708126, at *7 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025). 
For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. In determining whether the organization or 
establishment, or a department or division thereof, has a distinguished reputation, relevant factors 
include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and relevant media coverage. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). Organizations, establishments, divisions, or departments 
that enjoy a distinguished reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Id. The 
Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language

The Petitioner argues that playing a leading or critical role in a minor university office allows her to

or "bootstrap" the distinguished reputation of the entire university. While USCIS policy offers 
flexibility in what the regulation's language of "organizations or establishments" means, it does not 
allow petitioners to impute a university's broader prestige to the specific subdivision where the 
Petitioner worked. Each entity must be evaluated on its own merits, and assuming the part inherits 
the reputation of the whole commits a fallacy of division. Within the appeal, the Petitioner discusses

USCIS policy provides that the identified entity in which the Petitioner claims she performed in a 
leading or critical role, must be the same entity that has a distinguished reputation. It would be illogical 
for one to perform in a leading or critical role for a small unknown administrative office at Harvard 
University, then turn around and rely on the university's reputation for the distinguished reputation 
element. Doing so would divorce the evidentiary weight of the reputation from the actual significance 
of the alien's role within that specific operation. 
Overall, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 O; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 
28, 2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before and since the Mukherji court made it. 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. Patel 
v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328,332 (2022) (citing INSv. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision)).
