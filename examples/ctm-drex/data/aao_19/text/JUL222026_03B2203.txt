Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria, as required, he did not show his sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrate he is among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
USCIS determined the Petitioner met three of the six claimed evidentiary criteria. However, USCIS 
concluded the Petitioner did not show he garnered sustained national or international acclaim and his 
achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating he is one of that small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contests USCIS' decision and argues his eligibility for three additional 
criteria. Because USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met three criteria, we need not determine 
whether the Petitioner fulfills any farther criteria. See Churywnov v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 
No. 2:18-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 WL 5802041, at *3 (W.D. Wash. Nov. 7, 2019), aff'd, No. 19-36094, 
2022 WL 1657356 (9th Cir. May 25, 2022) (finding that regardless of the plaintiff's claim of meeting 
seven criteria, the plaintiff met the threshold evidentiary burden when he met at least three criteria); 
see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 819 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (stating that the petitioner's 
challenge to USCIS' finding at step one does not move the needle because USCIS determined that he 
prevailed at step one). Accordingly, we will review the totality of the evidence in the context of the 
final merits determination below. 1

Finally, the record contains some recommendation and reference letters. However, the letters are not 
sufficiently persuasive. The letters generally highlight the Petitioner's work and make broad claims. 
For instance, Y-Z- stated that "[h]is research offers a vital scientific foundation for developing 
balanced, evidence-based policies that support both economic development and environmental 
stewardship," "[h ]is work not only advances scientific knowledge but also offers practical strategies 
for achieving sustainable resource use in one of the most water-stressed regions of the United States," 
and "[the Petitioner's] research has already sparked widespread interest and recognition, generating 
thought-provoking discussions at academic conferences and within the scientific community across 
the United States." Here, the letter does not farther elaborate on these assertions and explain how the 
Petitioner's research or work has been viewed by the field as being influential or impactfol, garnering 
him sustained national or international acclaim or placing him among the upper echelon in his field. 
Letters that make general and expansive statements regarding the beneficiary and the beneficiary's 
accomplishments are generally not persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(8)(3). The letters do not claim, nor does the content show, that the Petitioner achieved national 
or international acclaim from the field. Moreover, the letters do not demonstrate that the Petitioner 
has attracted a level of attention placing him the very top of his field. In addition, the letters do not 
show that he has enjoyed a career of acclaimed work. See Jafarov, 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding 
testimonial letters were insufficient to establish his relative status in the field and do not compare him 
to others). 
Here, the letters do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that the Petitioner is 
viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that he has 
garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 
2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the plaintiff's letters point to 
evidence of extensive documentation of his accomplishments to show sustained national or 
international acclaim). Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's work, they do not reflect a career 
of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international acclaim. See 
Bodhankar, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (finding the submitted letters failed to establish the plaintiff's 
contributions resulted in significant acclaim).

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 7 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Price, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of 
accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 
2025) ( stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of 
individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of 
his work is indicative ofthe required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with 
a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner 
need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the 
record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
