Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a powerlifting coach, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner had 
satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, the record did not establish that he is one of 
the small percentage who has risen to the very top of his field and has sustained national or 
international acclaim. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that SCOPS failed to articulate any rationale as to why his submission does 
not satisfy the applicable standard of acclaim and recognition, and this failure to provide a cogent or 
legally supportable justification constitutes arbitrary and capricious decision-making and amounts to 
an abuse of discretion. The Petitioner also argues that SCOPS has applied an unduly restrictive 
standard in his case. He maintains that the totality of the record establishes that he has achieved 
sustained national and international acclaim in the field ofpowerlifting athletics. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) ("EB-1 
petitions, nicknamed Einstein visas, are coveted visas reserved for only the most talented noncitizens 
with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
sustained acclaim and the recognition of the beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one­
time achievement that is a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of 
the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items, such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence 
to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether the record

acclaim, he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor, and his achievements

we will discuss below. While we may not address each piece of evidence individually, we have 
reviewed and considered each one.

The Petitioner argues that SCOPS failed to articulate any rationale as to why his submission does not 
satisfy the applicable standard of acclaim and recognition and applied an unduly restrictive standard 
in his case. However, SCOPS clearly acknowledged and analyzed various documents on record and 
concluded overall that the quality of the evidence lacked probative value in supporting the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the EB-1 visa classification. Under the preponderance standard, we consider not only 
the quantity, but also the quality of the evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and 
credibility. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-
80 (Comm'r 1989). Furthermore, when USCIS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition and 
has made adequate findings, it will not be required to specifically address each claim the petitioner 
makes, nor is it necessary for it to address every piece of evidence the petitioner presents. Guaman­
Loja v. Holder, 707 F.3d 119, 123 (1st Cir. 2013) ( citing Martinez v. INS, 970 F.2d 973, 976 (1st Cir. 
1992); see also Kazemzadeh v. US. Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 (11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. 
US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1993). Here, the record reflects that SCOPS considered all 
evidence in the totality, provided a reasoned consideration to the petition, made adequate findings, and 
articulated the specific reasons as to why SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
by a preponderance that he has extraordinary ability. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons we have discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
