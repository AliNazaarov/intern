Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an editor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
The Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that he met at 
least three of the ten regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now 
before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter oJChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
• the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
• the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue to work in the area ofextraordinary ability; 
and 
• the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376.

B. Published Materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, at F.2(B)(l). 
In denying the petition, SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's submission of six published articles 
but determined that the record did not establish that they were published in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media. For instance, SCOPS noted that the record did not contain 
publication circulation, readership, or viewership information of the published material. In addition, 
SCOPS noted that some of the articles did not contain the authors' name or were not about the 
Petitioner's work. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that "[t]he regulation does NOT require circulation of the 
publication" and that SCOPS "unilaterally imposed substantive evidentiary requirement beyond those 
set forth in the regulations" by requiring such information. However, to determine whether a 
submitted publication qualifies as professional or major trade publications or other major media, 
USCIS considers certain factors. As mentioned above, professional or major trade publications may 
be identified by their intended audience, while other major media and major trade publications should 
have elevated circulation or readership relative to other media in the field. Id. While they are not 
required, such factors are relevant in determining whether their circulation or viewership is high 
compared to others and therefore qualify as "major media." While asserting that the regulation does 
not require information regarding circulation of the publication, the Petitioner does not explain how 
an alien could establish that their publication qualifies as a major media. Further, we acknowledge 
that the Petitioner provided circulation information for the Xinmin Evening News, but the information 
was from over 20 years ago. That is, the record does not contain circulation data at the time the article 
was published in April 2024 to establish that Xinmin Evening News was a major media. Three online 
articles are also about the Petitioner's work, but the printouts from the media sources' websites do not 
include information about their circulation, readership or viewership to establish that they qualify as 
major trade publications or other major media. 4 Without additional information, we are not able to 
determine whether they qualify as major media. Furthermore, two of the online articles the Petitioner 
submitted are not about the Petitioner's work. Therefore, the Petitioner has not demonstrated, by the 
preponderance of the evidence, that the articles were published in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media. Therefore, we agree with SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner 
has not satisfied this criterion.

D. Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." USCIS first 
determines whether the person has authored scholarly articles in the field. In academia, a scholarly 
article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally peer 
reviewed, and may be published in professional or scientific journals or conference presentations. In 
other fields, a scholarly article should be written for others having profound knowledge in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, at F.2(B)(l). 
Second, USCIS determines whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade 
publication or other major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership 
relative to other media in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id. 
The record includes seven untranslated articles. Any document in a foreign language must be 
accompanied by a foll English language translation, and a certification from the translator that the 
English language translation is complete and accurate, and that they are competent to translate from 
the foreign language into English. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The Petitioner also provided excerpts of 
the articles. However, without foll, translated articles, we cannot make a determination as to whether 
these articles contain the characteristics of scholarly articles. Also, we cannot make a determination 
that Petitioner's articles were written for learned persons in the publishing field or for other particular 
learned persons. Learned persons include all persons having profound knowledge of a field. Id.

Moreover, even if the Petitioner had established that the articles were scholarly articles, he does not

following publications: View on Publishing, Modem Publishing, and Publishing Research. SCOPS 
therefore requested the Petitioner to provide independent and objective evidence to verify each 
article/paper was authored by the Petitioner and that each article/paper was published in a professional 
or major trade publication or other major media. As the Petitioner did not provide any such evidence 
in response to SCOPS' request for evidence, SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet this 
criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that neither the regulation nor the USCIS Policy Manual require 
that a scholarly article be found on the China/ Asia On Demand website and he asserts that he 
"submitted abundant documentation of his scholarly articles and information on the professional 
publications" to meet this criterion. While the Petitioner is correct in that it is not a requirement for 
an article to be posted in China/Asia On Demand, it is the Petitioner's burden to establish that he has 
authored scholarly articles. However as discussed, the record does not sufficiently establish that the 
Petitioner has authored the articles. Further, even if the Petitioner had established his authorship, the 
Petitioner does not explain why his articles cannot be found on websites that store Chinese research 
journals. He also does not provide independent and objective evidence establishing his authorship of 
scholarly articles in the field. It is the Petitioner's burden to demonstrate that he meets the plain 
language of the criterion, which he has not done so here. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding on 
this evidentiary criterion. 
E. Role 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof, has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, at F.2(B)(l).

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
