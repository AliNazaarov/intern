Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a business consultant and mentor for immigrant entrepreneurs, seeks classification as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 
U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the ten 
initial evidentiary criteria, he did not establish his sustained national or international acclaim and that 
he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us 
on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by apreponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de nova. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable 
evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in 
business consulting, specifically focused on mentoring and consulting for immigrant entrepreneurs. 
The Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received amajor, internationally recognized 
award. He initially claimed to have met six criteria: memberships in associations; judge of others' 
work; original contributions of major significance; authorship of scholarly articles; leading or critical 
roles; and high salary in relation to others. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner fulfilled three of the 
claimed criteria, but concluded in a final merits determination that he did not demonstrate his sustained 
national or international acclaim in the field or show that he is one of that small percentage at the very 
top of that field and denied the petition accordingly. On appeal, the Petitioner claims that the totality 
of the evidence supports him being one of the few professionals who have reached the top of his field. 
Upon de nova review, the Petitioner has not demonstrated meeting at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria, as discussed below. In addition, the record does not establish sustained national 
or international acclaim and that the Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very top of his 
field. 1

memberships in associations; original contributions of major significance; and authorship of scholarly

The Petitioner has not demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that he participated as ajudge

2. Reserved Issues 
The Director found that the Petitioner also met eligibility for the criteria relating to leading or critical 
role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) and high salary in relation to others at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
Because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we need not determine whether he meets the requirements of these additional two 
criteria, and we will reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) 
(holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we examine and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine 
whether the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is one of the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in 
the field through extensive documentation. While we have concluded that the Petitioner has not 
fulfilled the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria, and thus need not provide the type of final 
merits determination referenced in Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20, we will nonetheless 
consider the issue ofthe Petitioner's qualification under that standard, as it is the focus ofboth SCOPS' 
3 
decision and the appeal. We have reviewed the record in the aggregate and agree with SCOPS that 
the Petitioner has not established eligibility as a person of extraordinary ability. 
Because extraordinary ability is an elite level of accomplishment whose recognition necessarily entails 
a judgement call, it cannot be established through meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
alone. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394-395 (2022). The final merits determination is the 
ultimate statutory inquiry of whether the applicant has extraordinary ability as demonstrated by 
sustained national or international acclaim. Id. In this case, even if the Petitioner had met at least 
three of the initial evidentiary criteria, he has not offered sufficient evidence that he meets that 
standard. 
The Petitioner is an Ecuadorian business manager and consultant with most of his experience being 
unrelated to his indicated area of extraordinary ability, business management with a focus on 
mentoring and consulting immigrant entrepreneurs. He emphasizes his participation in a limited three­
month educational project funded by the Office of Population, Refugees, and Migration of the U.S. 
Department of State, for which he trained refugees and vulnerable Venezuelan migrants. Whereas 
most of his business management and consulting experience is focused in other industries, including 
the distribution of motor vehicles, parts, and supplies; the distribution of construction materials and 
equipment; sustainable construction; warehouse management; and the wholesale of household 
appliances and electronic equipment. The Petitioner does not explain how the evidence of his business 
management and consulting experience in these other industries demonstrates acclaim within his 
indicated field of extraordinary ability or recognition within the field. 
The record includes letters of recommendation from colleagues, friends, and community leaders who 
recognize the Petitioner's professional experience as a business manager and consultant, some briefly 
mentioning his participation in the training project for refugees and vulnerable migrants. The letters, 
however, follow a similar format, merely referencing each of the Petitioner's professional experiences 
and generally attesting to him contributing his business management and strategic consulting work to 
businesses and projects. Moreover, most of the letters use similar language, making general 
representations of the Petitioner's work being creative and innovative which resulted in effective 
business solutions and positive outcomes. In evaluating the evidence, the truth is to be determined not 
by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. at 376. 
The individuals do not explain in detail the Petitioner's business management and consulting work 
and how his work makes him one of the small percentage at the very top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(2).

The Petitioner contends that his contributions to the fields of entrepreneurship and business 
management are of major significance, having been recognized by USCIS. He emphasizes USCIS' 
previous approval his petition for employment-based second preference (EB-2) immigrant 
classification as a member of the professions holding an advanced degree, as well as a national interest 
waiver of the job offer requirement attached to this EB-2 classification. See section 203(b)(2) of the 
Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(2). He points to evidence of published articles and video interviews of him 
describing his petition and its approval. The submitted articles and YouTube video interviews, 
however, were not supported by independent evidence establishing the publications' prestige, 
circulation, or other factors indicative of national or international acclaim. The record lacks evidence 
establishing that USCIS' approval of his prior immigrant petition demonstrates the requisite level of 
acclaim within the Petitioner's indicated field of extraordinary ability. 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended 
for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the 
top. Matter of Price, 20 l&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league 
level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia v. Beers, , 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131 (D.D.C. 2013) (internal 
quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive 
by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 
(D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (determining that EB-1 visas 
are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); See also Hamal v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec. (Hamal I), No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist 
specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even 
highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" 
did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has garnered national 
or international acclaim in the field, and he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
record does not contain sufficient evidence establishing the Petitioner among the upper echelon in his 
field.
