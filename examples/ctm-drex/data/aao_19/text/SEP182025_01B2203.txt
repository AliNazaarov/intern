Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a financial technologist, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in 
business. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner 
met three of the initial ten evidentiary criteria for this classification listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376.

2. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
To establish that the Petitioner satisfies this criterion, he submitted, in relevant part: tax returns for the 
years 2017 - 2020, the latest of which reflects that that he earned a base salary of Dominican Peso 
(DOP) 5,400,000; two SalaryExplorer reports - one providing an average monthly salary for those 
working in the banking industry in the Dominican Republic, and one providing an average monthly 
salary for those working in the information technology industry in the Dominican Republic; and two 
Pay lab salary reports for employees in both of these industries in the Dominican Republic. 
Regarding the SalaryExplorer reports, average and median salaries are insufficient to establish the 
Petitioner commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field. See Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, 
No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *7 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019) (upholding evidence 
of average salary levels is an insufficient basis for comparison to show high salary in relation to others 
in the field); see also Kinuthia, 702 F. Supp. 3d at 11; Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 
853 (C.D. Cal. 2022). Proving a salary is "above average" typically does not suffice to establish a 
"high salary" or "significantly high remuneration." More comprehensive wage data is needed to 
effectively demonstrate high earnings relative to peers. With respect to the Paylab reports, the 
Petitioner does not show or otherwise explain how salary reports providing a gross salary range for 
employees in the banking industry or for employees in the information technology industry reflect an 
"apples to apples" comparison of the salary of a financial technologist in the field of business. These 
additional salary reports are therefore insufficient to demonstrate that he commands a high salary in 
relation to others in the field of financial technology. See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 
WL 5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-
JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding plaintiff must show that salary reports reflect an 
apples to apples, rather than an apples to oranges, comparison). 
For these reasons, the Petitioner has not shown that he meets the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) 
relating to high salary. We will withdraw SCOPS' conclusion to the contrary. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
5 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -
- F.Supp.3d---, 2025 WL 1508028, at* 10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes 
a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Nevertheless, we have reviewed the totality of the evidence in the record and determined that 
he has not established the requisite level of sustained acclaim and standing within his field to show 
eligibility for the requested benefit. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an alien's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the field 
of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. Here, the Petitioner asserts that he has extraordinary ability in the field of 
business as a financial technologist. The record shows that he has submitted evidence relating to his 
membership in associations requiring outstanding achievements oftheir members, published materials 
about him, scholarly articles, his service in a leading or critical role, and his salary. 
As discussed above, the Petitioner submitted evidence pertaining to membership, including letters of 
recommendation from organizations in which he claims membership. However, these 
recommendation letters do not explain how or show that the Petitioner has been recognized nationally 
or internationally for his service in a manner reflective of a career of sustained national or international 
acclaim. Similarly, the invitation does not show that the Petitioner has been so recognized for his 
participation as a member of this international delegation. 
Regarding published materials about the Petitioner, the record reflects that he has garnered some 
attention from the press, for example having been interviewed on two television shows about 
remittances from the United States ofAmerica to Latin America and having one news article published 
about him in which he discusses the same topic. However, the Petitioner has not shown that this 
limited press coverage is indicative of a level of success consistent with being at the top of his field. 
See Hamal v. US. Dep't o_fHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. 
June 8, 2021), ajf'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep 't o_fHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 
1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (upholding plaintiff did not show his press coverage was indicative 
of a level of success consistent with being at the top of his field). An additional article in the record 
below focuses primarily on the Petitioner's employer. The remainder of the articles are authored by 
the Petitioner and published in magazines such as Forbes Dominican Republic. Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown that he has garnered recognition from others in his field for these articles, or how, for 
instance, the publication rate of the articles he authored compares with others at the very top of his 
field. 
In addition, with resrct to the Petitioner's claimed service in a leading or critical role forl 
andl his previous employers, the Petitioner submitted letters ofrecommendation from 
these employers praising his skills and describing the projects that he led for each of them. While 
these letters praise his work, they do not show that the Petitioner has been recognized by the field for 
his work at a level that is tantamount to a career of sustained national or international acclaim. For 
example, the recommendation letters do not offer specific examples of how his work for these 
companies has garnered him recognition in the field ofbusiness or is otherwise reflective ofone among 
that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See generally Mishra v. 
Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *5 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding

does not demonstrate a level of recognition that indicates the required sustained national or international 
acclaim or demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. 
Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record 
does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one ofthe small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. 
C. 0-1 Classification 
On appeal, the Petitioner also notes that he currently holds 0-1 nonimmigrant status, and cites to 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(3) in support of his argument that this prior approval should 
be a relevant consideration in adjudicating his underlying petition for EB-lA immigrant status. We 
acknowledge that the record reflects that the Petitioner was approved for 0-1 nonimmigrant status. 
However, this prior approval for a nonimmigrant classification does not preclude users from denying 
an immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a different standard, statute, regulations, and 
case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. 
June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 
21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). 
Many immigrant petitions are denied after users approves prior nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., 
Sunl[ft Int'l, Inc. v. Mayorkas, No. 20-CV-08869-JCS, 2021 WL 3111627, at *11 (ND. Cal. July 22, 
2021); Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. 
US. Dep 't ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 (D.D.C. 1999), ajf'd sub nom. IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep 't 
ofJust., INS, No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 1999); Fedin Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 
F. Supp. 1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), ajf'd, 905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority 
over SCOPS, the office adjudicating nonimmigrant visa petitions, is comparable to the relationship 
between a court of appeals and a district court. Even if SCOPS has approved a nonimmigrant petition 
on behalf of an individual, we are not bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another 
immigrant petition. See Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. CrV. A. 98-2855, 2000 WL 
282785, at *2 (E.D. La. Mar. 15, 2000).
