Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a sculptor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
(EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not meet the initial evidence 
requirements for the classification by establishing her receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award or by demonstrating that she meets at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria. The 
matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens who have extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability, and whose entry will substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" 
refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the 
field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized 
award). Alternatively, a petitioner can provide evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner earned a Master of Fine Arts in sculpture from a Korean university and has over 20 
years of experience as a sculptor and installation artist. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must demonstrate that she satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed that she could meet five 
criteria: receipt of nationally recognized awards or prizes; membership in associations that require 
outstanding achievements; published materials about her and her work in professional publications 
and major media; original artistic contributions of major significance; and display of her work at 
artistic exhibitions or showcases. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(iii), (v) and (vii). 
SCOPS determined that Petitioner provided evidence of the display of her work at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases and thus satisfied the plain language of the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). The 
record supports the Director's conclusion that the Petitioner, whose artistic work has appeared in many 
group and solo exhibitions, satisfies this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' decision mischaracterizes the record by failing to 
acknowledge or meaningfully analyze most of the evidence she submitted in support of the petition 
and in response to a request for evidence (RFE). She maintains that the evidence was sufficient to 
demonstrate that she has received nationally recognized awards or prizes for excellence, has been 
featured in professional publications and major media, and has made original contributions of major 
significance in her field. 
Upon review, we agree that SCOPS' determinations with respect to the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii) and (v) were conclusory and did not adequately address the Petitioner's specific 
claims and evidence. When denying a petition, SCOPS is required to explain the specific reasons for 
denial. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l )(i). This explanation should be sufficient to allow the Petitioner a fair 
opportunity to contest the decision and to allow us an opportunity for meaningful appellate review. 
Cf, Matter ofM-P-, 20 I&N Dec. 786 (BIA 1994) (finding at a decision must fully explain the reasons 
for denying a motion to allow the respondent a meaningful opportunity to challenge the determination 
on appeal). Here, as further explained below, SCOPS' decision did not satisfy this requirement.

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." We 
first determine whether the alien is the recipient of prizes or awards, and then whether the award is 
nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence in the alien's field of 
endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
SCOPS acknowledged that the Petitioner "provided several awards" and "evidence about the awards 
from the issuing organizations," but ultimately concluded the record contained "no objective evidence 
about the awards or prizes." On appeal, the Petitioner emphasizes that she initially presented evidence 
related to 15 awards, provided a consolidated list often awards in response to the RFE, and specifically 
highlighted in her RFE response the four awards she considered most probative ofher eligibility under 
the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), specifically, those she received at the 35th Korea Art 
Exhibition, the 25th Korea Contemporary Art Grand Exhibition, the World Light Expo Competition 
and the Oita Asia Sculpture Exhibition. We agree with the Petitioner that it is difficult to discern what 
evidence SCOPS ultimately considered in its evaluation of this criterion, because the stated reasons 
for denial contain no references to the specific claims and supporting documentation the Petitioner 
provided, particularly with respect to her RFE response. 
SCOPS' decision reflects a similarly conclusory treatment of the evidence submitted in support of the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), which requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." 
SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's submission of"several articles in publications such as Monthly 
Art Magazine, Yonhap News, and broadcast media." SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not 
meet this criterion because "the evidence does not demonstrate the publications qualify as a 
professional or major trade publication or other major media." On appeal, the Petitioner emphasized 
that she provided evidence of 28 published articles and television appearances in support of this 
criterion, as well as a brief, submitted in response to the RFE, which highlighted the relevance of her 
three Korean television appearances and six of the previously submitted articles, includ{ng thosl

As with the awards criterion, SCOPS' stated reasons for denial contained few references to the 
Petitioner's specific claims and evidence and therefore did not explain why none of the submitted 
materials were sufficient to demonstrate eligibility under this criterion. 
Finally, to meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the 
alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic or business-related contributions of major 
significance in the field." SCOPS acknowledged that the Petitioner provided "letters of support" and 
"publications about the [Petitioner's] work" in support of this criterion but concluded without specific 
reference to this evidence that the record "does not demonstrate the major significance" of her original 
contributions. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS did not acknowledge the specific 
contributions she identified in the record or the evidence she submitted to demonstrate their significance. 
She emphasizes that the evidence she submitted in support ofthis criterion included not only 15 reference 
letters from experts and published materials about her work, but also evidence that her artistic work has 
been funded through grants and artist residencies and is "preserved by national museums, used by

curators to shape exhibitions, referenced by emerging artists and recognized internationally by critics 
and cultural institutions." 
SCOPS did not acknowledge the Petitioner's submission of evidence in support of this criterion beyond 
making general references to "letters of support" and "published materials" and did not specifically 
address the content of the published articles or any of the many letters submitted as expert testimony. 
Instead, the decision reaches a conclusory determination that the letters "do not provide specific 
examples of how the beneficiary's methods or techniques have influenced the work of other artists or 
otherwise equate to original contributions of major significance in the field." Detailed letters from 
experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's contributions may provide 
valuable context for evaluating claims regarding original contributions of major significance. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Here, SCOPS offered an inadequate 
explanation as to why it found the letters to be lacking in probative value. 
While SCOPS is not required to provide a detailed discussion of every piece of evidence the Petitioner 
submitted, the decision here was too general to convey that it was based on a reasoned consideration 
of all relevant evidence in the record and did not adequately explain the specific reasons for denial. 
Accordingly, SCOPS' decision is withdrawn, and the matter will be remanded to SCOPS to re­
evaluate the evidence submitted under the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii) and (v) and to issue 
a new decision. In its review, SCOPS should also consider the Petitioner's appellate brief and 
additional evidence submitted on appeal. 
If: after further review on remand, SCOPS determines that the Petitioner satisfies three criteria, the 
new decision should include a final merits determination evaluating whether she has demonstrated, by 
a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or international claim, that she is one of the 
small percentage at the very top of her field of endeavor, and that her achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
III. CONCLUSION 
While the evidence ofrecord may ultimately be insufficient to demonstrate the Petitioner's eligibility 
for the requested classification, SCOPS' decision did not adequately address the submitted evidence 
or adequately explain the specific reasons for denying the petition. Accordingly, we will withdraw 
SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
