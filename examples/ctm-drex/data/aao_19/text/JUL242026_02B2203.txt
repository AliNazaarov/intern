Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a research scholar and professor who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner warranted an 
approval within a final merits determination. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the 
evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We 
review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has enjoyed a notable career as a professor of African History at a university in his 
home country where he also served as the dean of the faculty of humanities. His research focuses on 
social, cultural, and political history in Africa. He is a former officer of one ofhis country's historical 
associations and he has held visiting and postdoctoral research positions at international universities. 
Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met five of the regulatory criteria. SCOPS decided that the 
Petitioner satisfied three of the criteria relating to judging, the authorship of scholarly articles, and 
performing in a leading or critical role but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes 
or awards, membership, or a high salary or remuneration. 
A. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether he has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim and 
that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor, and that his achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, 
we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20); also see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual 
( stating that in a final merits determination USCIS officers should then evaluate the evidence together 
when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has established by a 
preponderance of the evidence the required high level of expertise of the immigrant classification). In 
this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not documented his eligibility. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 810 (E.D. Pa. 2025); 
Agrawalv. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. 
v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. 
Bankers Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of

the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 
28, 2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before and since the Mukherji court made it. 
First, the Petitioner submits new evidence on appeal. Because he was provided proper notice and a 
reasonable opportunity to respond to a request for evidence (RFE), we decline to consider new 
evidence submitted for the first time on appeal, as the appellate process is not a venue for initial filings. 
Under 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l l), all requested evidence must be submitted together, and administrative 
bodies consistently refuse to review new evidence first introduced on appeal. See Matter ofFurtado, 
28 I&N Dec. 794, 801-02 (BIA 2024); Matter ofIzaguirre, 27 I&N Dec. 67, 71 (BIA 2017); Matter 
ofJin, 29 I&N Dec. 441, 449 (BIA 2026). Additional precedent strongly reinforces this restriction. 
See Matter ofN-P-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 347, 349 (BIA 2025); Matter ofD-J-L-, 29 I&N Dec. 485, 489 
(BIA 2026); Matter ofD-G-E-A- & N-G-G-E-, 29 I&N Dec. 570, 577 n.5 (BIA 2026). 
On appeal the Petitioner presents several arguments regarding the manner in which USCIS implements 
the requirements under the extraordinary ability immigrant visa classification. We will not address 
his arguments against the agency's established methods that are detailed in its published policies. We 
will only state that within a final merits determination, a petitioner must demonstrate more than their 
achievements have been sustained over a duration of time. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2) (stating that with a final merits determination: "To establish eligibility, the petition 
must demonstrate that the person has sustained national or international acclaim and that their 
achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise, indicating that the person is one of that 
small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor.") 
While the Petitioner presents an argument claiming that SCOPS erred in administering the final merits 
determination, our review of their denial decision doesn't bear that out. For instance, SCOPS 
acknowledged the Petitioner's citation record but noted that the record standing alone was inadequate 
to reflect he was among those at the top of his field. He characterizes their analysis as a "reevaluation 
of the sufficiency of the evidence for the already satisfied criteria, imposing unstated requirements 
that go beyond the regulatory language." But that is not the case as their final merits analysis evaluated 
whether his citation record was illustrative of one who was among the leaders in the field and he does 
not explain what relevant claims or evidence he presented before SCOPS that they failed to consider. 
It appears the Petitioner simply disagrees with SCOPS' adverse determinations and attempts to 
characterize his disagreements with allegations that they ignored pivotal evidence, or they applied a 
heightened standard to it. Much of these arguments focus on the first step in the adjudicative process 
under the ten regulatory criteria, but that was not the ultimate basis for SCOPS' denial. That basis fe 11

of why his evidence under any particular regulatory criterion illustrated: (1) that he sustained national

We agree with the Petitioner's claim that SCOPS did not directly discuss all of his evidence, but we 
also recognize that it is not required to. The lack of a direct discussion of every piece of evidence does 
not mean that SCOPS ignored it; it instead may mean that they considered it and concluded it was 
unpersuasive. See United States v. Teixeira, 62 F.4th I0, 25 ( l st Cir. 2023) ( concluding a trier of fact 
"need not articulate its conclusions as to every jot and tittle of evidence in making a determination"); 
United States v. Camillo, 165 F.4th 134, 141 (1st Cir. 2026); Hampton v. Shinn, 143 F.4th 1047, 1087 
(9th Cir. 2025); Pye v. Warden, Georgia Diagnostic Prison, 50 F .4th I 025, l 036 n.3 (11th Cir. 2022). 
When SCOPS provides a reasoned consideration of the petition, and has made adequate findings, it 
will not be required to specifically address each claim the Petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it 
to address every piece of evidence the Petitioner presents. Amin v, 24 F.4th at 394; Lafortune v. 
Garland, 110 F.4th 426,441 (1st Cir. 2024); see also Kazemzadeh v. US Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 
1351 (11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1993). 
Based on our review of SCOPS' denial, we conclude that the Petitioner received a sufficiently thorough 
analysis ofhis claims and supporting material. SCOPS must announce their decisions in terms sufficient 
to enable a reviewing entity to perceive that they have heard and thought and not merely reacted, and 
they have done so here. Molina-Diaz v. Bondi, 128 F.4th 568, 577 (4th Cir. 2025); Funez-Ortiz v. 
McHemy, 127 F.4th 498, 505 (4th Cir. 2025); Ponce-Flores v. Garland, 80 F.4th 480, 486 (4th Cir. 
2023); Ibarra Chevez v. Garland, 31 F.4th 279,292 (4th Cir. 2022); Nolasco v. Garland, 7 F.4th 180, 
190 (4th Cir. 2021). 
SCOPS requested additional evidence and noted that the Petitioner abandoned several of his claims in 
the RFE response, one of which was contributions of major significance as part of the final merits 
determination. As a result, we will not address that category in our analysis below. An RFE is intended 
to clarify eligibility as of the petition's filing date. See 8 C.F.R. § I 03.2(b )(8), ( 12). Under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .2(b )( 13)(i), failure to respond to specific RFE elements constitutes abandonment or waiver ofthose 
claims. A petitioner cannot later revive such claims on appeal after knowingly declining to respond. 
The Board of Immigration Appeals' (the Board) precedents favor this reading. In Matter ofR-S-H-, 
23 I&N Dec. 629, 638 (BIA 2003) the Board explained that when faced with an adverse scenario (e.g., 
SCOPS outlining an evidentiary shortcoming within an RFE) a filing party must offer a response and 
advance their arguments to preserve that topic for consideration on appeal). The Board concluded the 
same in Matter ofC-A-R-R-, 29 I&N Dec. 13, 17 (BIA 2025) in which they decided that when the trier 
of fact requests supplemental statements or documents it deems necessary to complete an adjudication, 
but those materials are not timely submitted, the opportunity to make those statements or documents part 
of the record is waived. Also, in Matter ofLopez-Ticas, 29 I&N Dec. 90, 94 (BIA 2025), the Board 
4 
determined that an issue is waived or forfeited if we issue a notice, and the filing party fails to address a 
particular issue within the notice that they disagree with or that they deem is incorrect. 
So, we will not entertain new arguments on appeal that were not raised in response to SCOPS' RFE. 
MatterofR-S-H-, 23 I&NDec. at 638 (citingMatterofGarcia-Reyes, 19 I&NDec. 830,832 (BIA 1988)). 
The Petitioner had the opportunity to present these arguments and failed to do so. And federal courts 
have acknowledged such USCIS action when the agency determines a filing party's failure to respond to 
a specific eligibility requirement in an RFE means they have abandoned or waived that issue. See 
Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 807 (E.D. Pa. 2025). Issues not properly developed before SCOPS 
are not preserved for appeal. See Santos Garcia v. Garland, 67 F.4th 455, 460 (1st Cir. 2023); Ali v. 
Garland, 33 F.4th 47, 55 (1st Cir. 2022); Indrawati v. US. At(v Gen., 779 F.3d 1284, 1297 (11th Cir. 
2015); Jeune v. US. Atty. Gen., 810 F.3d 792, 800 (11th Cir. 2016). 
SCOPS acknowledged his awards and his fellowships the Petitioner claims are of such a distinguishing 
nature that they situate him "among the very top echelon of scholars" and they noted that while 
impressive, the Petitioner did "not establish that they were awarded to those at the top of the field." On 
appeal, the Petitioner points to letters to support his contention that his awards and fellowships show he 
is at the top of the field. While those letters claim his fellowships are at the apex ofpeer recognition, the 
institution's own documents did not bear out that assertion. Nor did the Petitioner identify what evidence 
he submitted before SCOPS that might support the claims in the letters. USCIS policy provides that 
"[l]etters of support, while not without weight, should not form the cornerstone of a successful claim for 
this classification. Rather, the statements made by the witnesses should be corroborated by documentary 
evidence in the record." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2). While we agree with 
SCOPS that his fellowships are in historied organizations, he did not establish that they are illustrative of 
the types of sustained acclaim that are required by the statute and regulation. 
As it relates to the Petitioner's memberships in associations, in his appeal he presents the same claims 
and evidence in one fellowship that he relied on in the prizes or awards category. That runs contrary to 
the statutory requirement for the Petitioner to demonstrate his "achievements have been recognized in the 
field through extensive documentation." Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) ofthe Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A)(i). 
Congress's intent that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this 
regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required for 
lesser classifications. Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. at 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). 
The Petitioner's appeal notes his judging roles as a Ph.D. and Master's external examiner, assessor for 
professorial promotions, peer reviewer for journal outlets, and selector of winners of scholarly awards, 
and he alleges that "the denial ignores the context and significance of his judging roles." SCOPS noted 
the Petitioner's judging experience is not unusual or particularly notable for someone in his field, and we 
agree. Participation in the peer review process as represented here does not automatically demonstrate 
that an individual has sustained national or international acclaim at the very top of his field. Without 
evidence pre-dating the filing of the petition that considerably sets the Petitioner apart from others in his 
field, we cannot conclude that he is among that small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field 
of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Salient examples might consist of a combination of the 
following: (1) receiving and completing independent requests for review from a substantial number of 
journals or conferences; (2) reviewing abstracts or speaker submissions where the event itself is 
recognized for attracting eminent experts in the field; (3) chairing a technical committee for a reputable 
5 
conference; (4) evaluating proposals for government or prestigious grant agencies; or (4) serving in an 
editorial position for a distinguished journal. The ultimate question is whether the Petitioner's peer review 
activities illustrate his national or international acclaim or placement among those at the top of the field. 
And the Petitioner has not made such a showing here. 
SCOPS concluded the Petitioner did not demonstrate that his publication record is consistent with having 
a career of acclaimed work and sustained acclaim. The Petitioner discusses his citation and publication 
history and notes the denial failed to compare his citation counts to field benchmarks, to the h-index of 
journals in which he has published, and that his citation impact places him among the top 10--20 percent 
of his peers globally, and the top tier of black scholars. The Petitioner did not identify what evidence 
supports these assertions and his statements do not satisfy his burden of proof. Matter ofL-R-M-C- & 
V-A-M-M-, 29 I&N Dec. 757, 758 (BIA 2026) (concluding assertions without corroborating evidence are 
generally inadequate to meet a filing party's evidentiary burden). Additionally, he did not advance some 
these arguments before SCOPS and we will not consider them for the first time on appeal. See, e.g., 
Matter ofFurtado, 28 I&N Dec. at 801-02. 
The appeal brief reflects that SCOPS downplayed the Petitioner's leading roles. He identifies his roles 
as a professor, head of department, and dean of the faculty of humanities at.____________,. 
He alleges that his leadership at that institution contributed to the advancement and reputation of the 
institution, shaping the very foundation of a very new university. While the record reflects he held those 
positions for the university, he did not establish that the institution is one that warrants the level ofacclaim 
necessary to aid him in demonstrating he is among those in the top of his field. Nor did his roles as a 
visiting scholar and lecturer at an American university or delivering a public lecture at a military think 
tank. 
And finally, his claims regarding his salary only serve as a detriment to his eligibility under a final merits 
determination. Not only did he fail to satisfy the regulatory requirements under step one, but he has also 
not established his earnings were indicative of or consistent with sustained national acclaim or a level of 
expertise representing that he is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of his field. 
In summary, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. A favorable 
final merits determination requires a showing that one's extraordinary ability is an elite level of 
accomplishment. Amin, 24 F.4th at 394-95. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at 
the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. 953, 954-55 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). The Petitioner's evidence confirms that he has he is 
an accomplished scholar. However, considering the full measure of the Petitioner's ability and 
achievements, the level ofhis national or international acclaim and the extent to which his achievements 
have been recognized in the field are not indicative of a record of sustained acclaim. Also, he has not 
submitted extensive documentation exhibiting he has attained a level of expertise placing him among that 
small percentage that has risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered
