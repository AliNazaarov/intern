Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Exceptional Ability) 
The Petitioner, a ballroom dance choreographer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had only met 
two of the "at least three" required evidentiary criteria for this classification according to regulation. 
We dismissed a subsequent appeal. The matter is now before us on combined motions to reopen and 
reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
A motion to reopen must state new facts and be supported by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(2). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the 
latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy 
these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. See Matter of Coelho, 
20 l&N Dec. 464, 473 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence have the potential to change the 
outcome). 
On motion to reopen, the Petitioner submits two media articles about interviews with him, one from 
December 2024 and one from January 2025. The Petitioner asserts that these new facts establish 
eligibility, as they demonstrate that he has played a leading or critical role in organizations with 
distinguished reputations according to the requirement at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). But the articles, 
which consist entirely of the Petitioner's responses to interview questions, do not discuss the 
organizations where the Petitioner has worked at length. In addition, to the extent that the Petitioner's 
motion presents documentary evidence of new facts regarding his eligibility for the requested benefit, 
the Petitioner must meet eligibility requirements at the time of filing the petition. See 8 C.F .R. 
§ 103.2(b)(l). 
Turning to the Petitioner's motion to reconsider, such a motion must establish that our prior decision 
was based on an incorrect application oflaw or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the 
evidence in the record ofproceedings at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Because the 
scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these 
proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements 
and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. 
Here, the Petitioner contests the correctness of our prior decision dismissing his appeal. In doing so, 
he relies on Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369 and Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 
2010) to support his claim that he has submitted sufficient evidence of his eligibility for at least three 
required evidentiary criteria. 
First, the Petitioner contests our determination that he did not meet the criterion for "[ d]ocumentation 
of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence 
in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 1 In our decision dismissing his appeal, we 
determined that while the record demonstrated that the Petitioner had received awards, he had not 
shown sufficient evidence of the awards' claimed national or international recognition, including 
evidence that people beyond the granting organizations recognize them for excellence in the field of 
endeavor. Further, we noted that the awards were for dancing, not his stated field of choreography. 
On motion, the Petitioner contends that he provided letters from industry professionals regarding his 
receipt of the awards and that he is not required to show mainstream media coverage. He also argues 
that his accomplishments as a dancer inform his choreographic ability and are therefore consistent 
with the USCIS Policy Manual's discussion of this topic. The Petitioner's motion arguments are 
unavailing. He must show that the awards are nationally or internationally recognized in the field of 
endeavor according to the plain language of the regulation. We conclude that he has not met his 
burden to show eligibility for this criterion on motion. 
The Petitioner also argues that we erred in concluding that he was ineligible for the criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) regarding "memberships in associations in the field for which the 
classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by 
recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or field." We agreed with SCOPS that 
the Petitioner did not provide sufficient independent, corroborating evidence that his claimed 
memberships were based on outstanding achievements as judged by national or international experts 
in his field. On motion, the Petitioner disputes this conclusion and states the criteria for membership 
and identity of the membership for the adjudicators. We acknowledge that, contrary to our 
characterization in the appeal decision, the Petitioner did submit information below regarding the 
evaluation panels. Nevertheless, the Petitioner's focus on these topics is misplaced. While he argues 
on motion that we overlooked the rigor and specificity in these criteria, these alone do not establish 
that outstanding achievements are required. As he has not demonstrated, and does not argue, on 
motion that these associations require outstanding achievements for their members, he has not shown 
that our prior decision was incorrect in this regard.

Next, the Petitioner asserts that our prior decision should have determined that he met the criterion 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) for making an original contribution of major significance in the field. 
SCOPS determined that the training manual the Petitioner authored regarding his dance methodology 
did not sufficiently show major significance in the field, a determination with which we agreed on 
appeal. While we acknowledged the Petitioner had made a contribution and that evidence reflected 
this contribution's originality, the record lacked sufficient independent, objective evidence of the 
manual's "substantial influence beyond one's employers, clients, or customers" as affirmed in 
Strategati, LLCv. Sessions, No. 18-cv-1200, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019). On 
motion, the Petitioner claims that he has demonstrated such major significance according to the 
framework outlined in Volume 6, Part F, Chapter 2 of the USCIS Policy Manual. He argues that his 
dance methodology is used by multiple institutions, the methodology has been adopted nationally and 
internationally, and that his endorsements came from non-affiliated professionals. The Petitioner cites 
several documents in the record in support of this assertion. However, as we stated in our prior 
decision, the majority of the evidence the Petitioner submitted for this criterion comes from former 
coaches, employers, and colleagues; therefore we properly gave these less weight as independent, 
objective evidence. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376 (finding that a petitioner must 
support claims with independent evidence). We recognize the Petitioner's reference on motion to the

The Petitioner additionally disputes our conclusion that he did not sufficiently demonstrate that he had 
performed in a leading or critical role for a distinguished establishment or organization as required by 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). In our appeal decision, we clarified the Petitioner's claim of performing 
in a critical role for the dance sport federation, but determined that the Petitioner had neither supported 
this claim with sufficient evidence nor established that the dance federation had a distinguished 
reputation. The Petitioner disputes the latter finding on motion but has not identified specific 
evidentiary submissions that demonstrate this. We also concluded that the record did not support 
Petitioner's second claim of performing a critical role in a dance school by introducing a new 
successful training methodology. On motion, the Petitioner reasserts his eligibility for this criterion 
and specifically argues that he updated the dance school's methodology after the publication of the 
201 7 article he submitted, which was evident from the letter from the CEO of the dance school. We 
acknowledge this claim, but it is generally a reiteration of the arguments he made below and that we 
have already considered. Therefore, we find no error in our previous conclusion that the Petitioner 
did not meet this criterion. 
Finally, the Petitioner argues on motion we should have determined him to have commanded high 
salary or significantly high remuneration compared to others in his field per the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix). We concluded in our appeal decision that the Petitioner had submitted salary 
information for dance choreographers, but that his own title had been "head coach," and that therefore 
the record lacked an appropriate comparison between the Petitioner and others in his field. On motion, 
the Petitioner asserts that in the Russian dance sport industry, occupational titles often overlap in

function and that there is no formal occupational code or survey category for "head coaches." He cites 
the USCIS Policy Manual in support of his argument that broader occupational categories are 
permitted. While the Petitioner's reference to our policy is accurate, it does not relieve him of the 
burden to demonstrate that the salary information he submitted is in fact comparable to the salary he 
commanded, along with the burden to support his claim that it is significantly high. See Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 3 7 5 ( stating that a petitioner bears burden of proving eligibility for the 
benefit sought). 
In conclusion, although the Petitioner has submitted additional evidence in support of the motion to 
reopen, the Petitioner has not established eligibility. On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not 
established that our previous decision was based on an incorrect application of law or policy at the 
time we issued our decision. Therefore, the motion will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed. 
FURTHER ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
