Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a Brazilian jiu-jitsu instructor, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish either that he received a 
major, internationally recognized award, or that the record satisfied at least three of the 10 initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
MatterofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The te1m "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in ce1iain 
media, and scholarly articles). Ifa criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily apply to 
a petitioner, they may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010).

in the same area of expe1tise. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(2), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
As noted above, only aliens who seek to continue to work in the area of extraordinary ability may be 
eligible for first preference immigrant classification as individuals of extraordinary ability. Section 
203(b)(l)(A)(ii) ofthe Act. Continuing to work in one's area of extraordinary ability, as contemplated 
by section 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii) of the Act, means "working in the same profession in which one has 
extraordinary ability, not necessarily any profession in that field." Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. Ill. 2002). For example, an individual who has "extraordinary ability as a baseball player 
does not imply that [the individual] also has extraordinary ability in all positions or professions in the 
baseball industry such as a manager, umpire or coach." Id. 
Because the Petitioner stated he intends to work as a coach or trainer of other athletes, we will not 
consider prizes or awards he may have received as an athlete. See Mussarova v. Garland, 562 
F. Supp. 3d 837, 844 (C.D. Cal. 2022) (finding that the petitioner's evidence related to awards as a 
water polo player rather than awarded for excellence in the field of water polo coaching). Therefore, 
the record does not support users' conclusion that the record satisfies this criterion, and we withdraw 
USCrS' determination to the contrary.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
8 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INSv. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.
