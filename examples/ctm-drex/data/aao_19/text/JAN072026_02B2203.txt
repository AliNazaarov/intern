Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a historian and Rabbi in the field ofhistory and Jewish studies, seeks classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( 1 )(A), 
8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, 
as required. We dismissed a subsequent appeal but reopened the matter. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence ofsustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not meet any of the four claimed 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (v), (vi), and (viii). On appeal, the Petitioner again 
asserts that he meets the same four criteria. 1 
The Petitioner contends that SCOPS did not fully consider all the evidence submitted and did not 
properly weigh the evidence under the preponderance of the evidence standard. Under the 
preponderance standard, we consider not only the quantity, but also the quality of the evidence, 
including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; 
see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989). We also note that it is the Petitioner's 
burden to submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates his claims. Statements made without 
supporting documentation are oflimited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the Petitioner's 
burden of proof Matter ofSoffici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). 
Upon de novo review of all the evidence in the record, including the Petitioner's response to our 
motion, we agree that he does not meet any ofthe claimed criteria by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
While we may not address each piece of evidence individually, we have reviewed and considered each 
one. In addition, the Petitioner has not established that he has sustained national or international 
acclaim or that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of history and Jewish 
studies.

1. Receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the 
field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
To fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must first demonstrate that he was the recipient of prizes or 
awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
Second, the award must be a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize or award, which the 
petitioner received for excellence in the field of endeavor. Id. Relevant considerations regarding 
whether the basis for granting the prizes or awards was excellence in the field include: (1) the criteria 
used to grant the awards or prizes, (2) the national or international significance of the awards or prizes 
in the field, (3) the number of awardees or prize recipients, and ( 4) limitations on competitors. Id.

3. Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." In academia, a 
scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally 
peer reviewed, and may be published in professional or scientific journals or conference presentations. 
In other fields, outside of the academic arena, a scholarly article should be written for learned persons 
who have "profound knowledge gained by study" in that field. Learned persons include all persons 
having profound knowledge of a field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
To determine whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication or other 
major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership relative to other media 
in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id.

4. Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
In order to meet this criterion, the Petitioner must first show he has performed a leading or critical role 
for an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For 
a leading role, we look at whether the evidence establishes that the petitioner was a leader within the 
organization or establishment or a division or department thereof. Id. For a critical role, we look at 
whether the evidence establishes that the petitioner has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. Id.

leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. Id. The relevant size or longevity of an 
organization or establishment is not a determining factor but is considered together with other 
information to determine whether a distinguished reputation exists. Id. Other relevant factors for 
evaluating the reputation of an organization or establishment include the scale of its customer base or 
relevant media coverage. Id. Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary defines "distinguished" as "marked 
by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­

B. Comparable Evidence 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS failed to consider comparable evidence as per the 
regulations at 204.5(h)( 4) that allows for comparable evidence ifthe listed criteria do not readily apply 
to his occupation. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4) states "[i]fthe above standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the 
petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory 
provision provides petitioners the opportunity to submit comparable evidence to establish the person's 
eligibility, if it is determined that the evidentiary criteria described in the regulations do not readily 
apply to the person's occupation. Id. When evaluating such comparable evidence, we must consider 
whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether 
the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
Outside of a very general statement that his position is "inherently hybrid, encompassing elements of 
education, religious studies, and humanities research," the Petitioner did not demonstrate which

C. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not established that he meets any of the evidentiary criteria under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) and, therefore, has not met the "initial evidence" requirement for the requested 
classification, we need not conduct a final merits determination. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20. Nevertheless, we have reviewed the totality of the evidence in the record and determined 
that he has not established the requisite level of sustained acclaim and standing within his field to show 
eligibility for the requested benefit. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an alien's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the field 
of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. Here, the Petitioner asserts that he has extraordinary ability in the field ofhistory 
and Jewish studies, and has submitted evidence of his education, experience, and achievements whilst 
working in this field. This evidence primarily consists ofhis personal statement, reference letters, and 
portions of two of the books he published. The evidence generally reflects participation and activity 
within religious, teaching, and communal settings, but does not demonstrate broad recognition beyond 
those circles or a level of influence indicative of extraordinary ability. The record as a whole does not 
establish that he has risen to the level of the small percentage at the very top of the field or that he 
enjoys sustained national or international acclaim. 
The reference letters submitted by the Petitioner are universal in their praise for the Petitioner. But 
they are not sufficient to establish that his work in his field has sustained national or international 
acclaim or extends beyond the circle of colleagues and contacts he encountered in the course of his 
career. Letters that make general and expansive statements regarding the Petitioner and his 
accomplishments are generally not persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(3). The letters do not demonstrate that the Petitioner has attracted a level of attention placing 
him the very top of his field. In addition, the letters do not show that he has enjoyed a career of 
acclaimed work. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 
2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding testimonial letters were insufficient to establish his relative status in 
the field and do not compare him to others). 
8 
In addition, the letters do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that he is viewed 
by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that he has garnered 
recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's talents, they do not 
reflect a career ofacclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international 
acclaim. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. 
Apr. 20, 2021) ( finding the plaintiff submitted letters only from people within his circle of professional 
acquaintances, and none of the letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his 
accomplishments to show sustained national or international acclaim). 
The same is true of the Y oreh Y oreh certification he received when he passed the rabbinate exams of 
the chief rabbinate of Israel committee and certifies him to serve as a Rabbi. Likewise, authorship and 
publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. Here, the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate that his publication record is consistent with having a career of acclaimed work, 
sustaining national or international acclaim, and being among the small percentage at the very top of 
his field. The Petitioner, for instance, did not show the significance of his authorship or how his 
publication record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Bhanu v. 
Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the Petitioner did 
not show the significance of authorships reflect that he is among the small percentage at the very top 
of the field 
In general, the Petitioner has submitted insufficient evidence of sustained national or international 
acclaim or recognition of his achievements in his field. See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 
No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. 
United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
(holding that in addition to the requirement of demonstrating "sustained national or international 
acclaim," the plaintiff was also required to provide evidence demonstrating "that his or her 
achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise"). Further, the evidence, when considered 
in the aggregate, does not establish that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage at the very top of 
his field.

reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of
