Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a psychologist, seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements for classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. Specifically, SCOPS determined that she did not meet at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b )(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner states that she is an expert in the field of psychology and the founder and dean of the
I Ipsychology and mental health school in her native Kazakhstan. She completed a 
bachelor's degree in finance in 2008, and several online and in-person courses related to the field of 
psychology between the years of 2020 and 2024. The Petitioner initially stated her intent to continue 
her education in the United States to obtain a "psychology teaching degree" to train other 
psychologists, and to obtain a psychologist license in order to put her "psychotrauma methods, 
personal development, and professional and communication skills training" into practice. When 
responding to SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE), she did not mention furthering her education, but 
stated that she would "conduct training workshops, publish further research, and develop programs" 
in trauma recovery, emotional intelligence, and mental health education. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met one of the evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to her authorship of scholarly articles in professional and 
major trade publications or other major media. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she also meets 
the evidentiary criteria relating to: 
• Participation as a judge of the work of others in her field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in her field; and, 
• Commanding a high salary or significantly high remuneration in relation to others in her field 1

The Petitioner also asserts that she has sustained national acclaim and is one of the small percentage

1. Participation as a judge of the work of others in the field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) calls for "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization for which classification is sought." SCOPS concluded that the evidence of the 
Petitioner's work at academic conferences and in evaluating educational programs was insufficient to 
show that she judged the work of other psychologists, and noted that she had not provided evidence 
of participation in peer review for manuscripts submitted to professional journals in her field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner notes that she never claimed or submitted evidence showing that she 
participated in the peer review process for a professional journal, and that the lack of such evidence 
should not be held against her. We agree that the issue of whether or not the Petitioner has conducted 
this type ofjudging of others' work is not relevant to her meeting this criterion, as the regulation does 
not specify a particular type of evidence that must be submitted. In addition, SCOPS' suggestion that 
this criterion requires "a formal designation in a judging capacity" is not supported in the plain 
language of the regulation. 
In support of this criterion, the Petitioner initially submitted certificates and other evidence regarding 
her participation as an evaluator in two academic conferences held at a private institution. SCOPS 
concluded in its decision that the evidence regarding her work at these conferences did not meet this 
criterion, and on appeal the Petitioner does not continue to base her claim to this criterion on this 
activity, focusing exclusively on the evidence of other activities she submitted in response to SCOPS' 
RFE. We agree with SCOPS that the evidence regarding the Petitioner's participation in these 
conferences is insufficient to show that she judged the work of others in the field of psychology or an 
allied field. 
Turning to the evidence of the Petitioner's evaluation of primary and secondary school psychological 
programs, the evidence shows that she was selected by the Administration of Education of the 
I !(Kazakhstan) region as a member of a temporary commission set up to perform those 
duties. Other evidence from schools in the region showed that she carried out these evaluations on 
four occasions, during which she monitored and reported on the effectiveness of psychological 
programs at these schools. This evidence establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that she 
judged the work of other psychologists in these instances. Accordingly, we withdraw SCOPS' 
determination and conclude that she meets this criterion.

3. Authorship of scholarly articles in the field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for"[e ]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." In academia, a 
scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally 
peer reviewed, and may be published in professional or scientific journals or conference presentations. 
In other fields, a scholarly article should be written for others having profound knowledge in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
To determine whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication or other 
major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership relative to other media 
in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met this criterion, but did not include an analysis stating which 
evidence met all of its elements. We first note that both of the articles written by the author which 
were submitted in response to SCOPS' RFE were published after the date the petition was filed, and 
in fact were published after the RFE was issued. A petitioner must meet eligibility requirements for 
the requested benefit at the time of filing the petition. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(1 ). Therefore we will not 
consider these two articles published in Psychologist and Society: Sociology, Psychology, Pedagogy 
under this criterion.

4. Additional Evidentiary Criteria 
SCOPS also concluded that the Petitioner had not established that she has commanded a relatively 
high salary, or significantly high remuneration, per the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(3)(3)(ix). But we 
need not discuss this additional criterion. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary 
requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the identified basis for denial is dispositive 
ofthe appeal. We therefore reserve the issue ofwhether the Petitioner meets this evidentiary criterion. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
7 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.
