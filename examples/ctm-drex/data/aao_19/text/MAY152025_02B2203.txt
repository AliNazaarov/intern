Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a singer and composer, seeks classification under the employment-based, first 
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate his receipt of a major internationally recognized award or his satisfaction of any of the 
requested category's ten alternate evidentiary requirements. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that 
SCOPS' decision lacked "detailed analysis" and overlooked evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude the he met two alternate evidentiary criteria regarding his receipt of lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized awards and his work's display at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases. But, because he did not satisfy the requisite minimum number of three alternate 
requirements, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. The Alternate Evidentiary Requirements 
SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not meet any of the ten alternate evidentiary requirements - three 
less than needed for a final merits determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, the Petitioner 
contends that he satisfied the following six criteria: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in the field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commercial successes in the performing arts. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iv), (v), (vii), (viii), (x). 
Before SCOPS, the Petitioner argued that he also submitted evidence of published materials about 
himself in the field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). His appeal, however, omits that claim. He 
therefore has effectively waived that issue. See Matter of O-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 
2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 I&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012)) (waiving ineligibility grounds 
not raised on appeal). 
To meet additional criteria, the Petitioner's evidence must objectively satisfy the parameters of the 
applicable regulatory descriptions. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B). "[N]either USCIS nor 
[the] AAO may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (citation omitted).

2. Judge of Others' Work 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge ofthe work ofothers in the same or an allied field ofspecification 
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner submitted a copy of an acknowledgement certificate from a Tajikistani television 
channel. The certificate recognizes him "for his effective work as a member [of] the jury of the 
television contest I I."' 
SCOPS found the undated certificate insufficient to meet this criteria, faulting the Petitioner for 
omitting evidence of "an official invitation" to serve as a judge in his field. SCOPS stated: 'There is 
no objective evidence in the record that the [Petitioner] has been invited to judge the work of others 
in the field [or] ... that [he] actually participated in judging ... the work of others." 
The Petitioner argues that, although the acknowledgement certificate lacks a date, "the document is 
nonetheless credible evidence of [his] participation in judging the work of others in his field." As a 
juror on the TV show, he contends that he assessed "the performances and contributions of participants 
in the music field." He states: "USCIS' emphasis on the absence of supporting materials like 
invitations or correspondence overlooks the practical realities of artistic events in some regions, where 
documentation standards may differ." He contends that the Agency's discounting of the 
acknowledgement certificate "disregards the context in which these events occur and imposes an 
unreasonable burden on the petitioner." 
We agree that, to meet this evidentiary standard, the Petitioner need not necessarily submit a copy of 
an invitation to judge others' work. But his evidence does not sufficiently demonstrate that he judged 
others' work in his or an allied field. The record does not explain the nature ofthe TV show for which 
he judged, its date, whom he evaluated, or on what basis. We appreciate that some supporting 
materials regarding his judging activities may be nonexistent or unavailable. But the Petitioner has 
not explained why he could not provide basic evidence or information regarding the TV show and the 
evaluated performers. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. CV 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *7 (E.D. Pa. 
4 
Sept. 18, 2023) ("Ultimately, if USCTS cannot identify who or what [ a petitioner] judged, then the 
agency's denial was correct and should be affirmed.") 
The Petitioner's evidence does not establish his judging of others' work in his or an allied field. We 
will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding on this evidentiary requirement. 
3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires"[ e ]vidence ofthe alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business­
related contributions ofmajor significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner made original contributions in their field. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. The Agency next considers whether the original 
contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted recommendation letters and copies of media articles, diplomas, certificates, 
and acknowledgments. He contends that these materials "collectively support the claim that [he] has 
made original contributions that have substantially influenced the music field in Tajikistan and 
beyond." He cites evidence of his participation in public cultural events, including performances at 
government-sponsored shows and international festivals that have received commendations from 
Tajikistan's ministry of culture and other government entities. He also states that his compositions 
"merge traditional Tajik melodies with modern musical techniques" and "have received widespread 
acclaim for their innovation and ability to resonate with diverse audiences." He claims that "[t]his 
originality in composition has elevated Tajik music and inspired other artists in the region." 
The record, however, supports SCOPS' finding of insufficient evidence of the purported major 
significance of the Petitioner's contributions. Letters praise his vocal and song-writing abilities and 
state his general popularity and cooperation with state institutions. But his evidence does not 
adequately explain the claimed importance of his work to his field. 
"Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may ... provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of 
major significance," 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. Here, however, the letters on 
behalf of the Petitioner are brief, vague, conclusory, and do not explain how his original work has 
significantly influenced his field. One letter from an official ofTajikistan's culture ministry describes 
the Petitioner's "contributions to the development of Tajik culture and the arts" as "invaluable." But 
the letter does not provide examples of such contributions or explain their purported worth. See Soni 
v. United States, No. 11-2431, 2016 WL 4154137, at *5 (D.N.J. Aug. 2, 2016) (discounting 
"[c]onclusory statements" about the value of a petitioner's contributions as insufficient). 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that his original contributions to his field have major 
significance. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary criterion.

4. Display of Work at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
USCIS first determines whether the displayed work constitutes a petitioner's work product. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 7. If so, the Agency then considers whether 
the venues - including virtual venues - that displayed the work were artistic exhibitions or showcases. 
Id. In this context, the term "exhibition" ordinarily means "a public showing ( as of works of art, 
objects of manufacture, or athletic skill)." Exhibition, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, 
www.merriam-webster.com; see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 7. 
SCOPS found that "the petitioner did not submit any specific evidence" to meet this evidentiary 
requirement. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the record shows that his work "has been 
prominently featured in significant cultural and artistic events, which qualify as artistic exhibitions or 
showcases under the plain language of the criterion." 
To the extent SCOPS found this criterion to limit qualifying evidence to the visual arts, we agree with 
the Petitioner that the regulation's plain language does not support the exclusion of performing arts. 
See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121 (barring USCIS from imposing "novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5"). 
The Petitioner submitted copies of certificates and acknowledgements documenting his musical 
participation in cultural events and his performances at artistic "showcases," such as concerts, music 
award ceremonies, and TV programs. Thus, he satisfied this criterion by submitting evidence of his 
work's display at artistic showcases. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
5. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for 
organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B )( 1 ), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is ( or 
was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division or department. Id. In contrast, a critical 
role means that an alien "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of 
the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization 
or establishment." Id. 
Next, USCIS considers whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. In this context, the word "distinguished" means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, supra. Relevant factors include not only an organization's relative size 
6 
or longevity but also other information such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. 
The Petitioner relied on the recommendation letters on his behalf and evidence supporting them. He 
stated that the letters show that "I play an important critical role in the development of my country's 
culture and art, in the promotion of common cultural values, and the discovery and development of 
young talents." 
SCOPS found that the Petitioner's evidence documented neither his performance in a leading or 
critical role nor an organization's distinguished reputation. SCOPS stated: "The letters did not provide 
the detailed and probative information that specifically addresses how the [Petitioner]'s role for the 
organization or establishment was leading or critical." 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the recommendation letters establish him "as a leading figure 
in shaping and executing key cultural programs" that have "lasting impacts on Tajikistan's cultural 
development and have garnered him international recognition." He states his "involvement in the 
planning and execution of high-profile cultural events" where "his performances were met with 
widespread acclaim" and received acknowledgements from Tajikistan government officials. He 
describes his role as "both hierarchical and functional, as indicated by the letters of recommendation 
and supporting evidence." He states that "[h]is responsibilities encompassed organizing and leading 
events, mentoring young talent, and executing performances that aligned with the strategic goals of 
the organizations." 
The record, however, does not support the Petitioner's contentions. As previously indicated, a letter 
from an official of Tajikistan's culture ministry describes the Petitioner's "contributions to the 
development ofTajik culture and the arts" as "invaluable." The letter also states that he "demonstrates 
a strong sense of responsibility towards his work and community obligations" and "contributed to the 
preparation of concert programs." But the letter lacks details about his contributions and does not 
explain how he led an organization or significantly contributed to the outcome of its activities. See 
Noroozi v. Napolitano, 905 F. Supp. 2d 535, 544 (S.D.N.Y. 2012) ("These letters ... simply do not 
describe, in any detail, how [the petitioner] performed in a 'leading or critical role."') 
A letter from the general director of a Tajikistani government concert tour states the Petitioner's 
cooperation with the organization and his participation as a "solo-vocalist" in state and international 
cultural events. The letter states: "He is distinguished among his colleagues by high professionalism, 
punctuality, [and] responsibility." Another letter from a television station states that the Petitioner has 
"worked closely with the state institutions." But these letters also lack sufficient details to explain 
how the Petitioner led an organization or significantly contributed to the outcome ofits activities. See 
Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) ("USCIS 
reasonably concluded that, as to the ultimate question, it cannot simply take an [organization]' s word 
for it that [ a petitioner]' s role was leading or critical.") 
"This is one criterion where letters from persons with personal knowledge of the significance of [a 
petitioner]' s leading or critical role can be particularly helpful to officers in making this 
determination." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. But the letters must "contain detailed 
and probative information that specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization, 
7 
establishment, division, or department was leading or critical." Id. The letters on the Petitioner's 
behalf lack detailed and probative information explaining his leading or critical role for an 
organization. The letters neither state his responsibilities, establish his place within an organization, 
indicate an organization's goals, nor confirm his claimed "mentoring [ of] young talent." The 
Petitioner therefore has not submitted evidence of his performance in a leading or critical role. 
Also, the Petitioner describes the Tajikistan culture ministry and a media company that provided a 
recommendation letter for him as "distinguished organizations known for their contributions to the 
arts and media sectors in Tajikistan." But, as SCOPS found, the record lacks sufficient evidence that 
an organization for which the Petitioner performed a leading or critical role has a distinguished 
reputation. The record lacks evidence regarding organizations' activities, goals, hierarchies, sizes, 
longevity, or media coverage. The evidence therefore does not establish that any of the mentioned 
organizations have distinguished reputations. See Soni, 2016 WL 4154137 at *5 (stating that "a dearth 
of supporting evidence is an appropriate reason for USCIS to discount [ a petitioner]' s conclusory 
statements"). 
The Petitioner did not provide evidence of his performance in a leading or critical role for 
organizations with distinguished reputations. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this 
evidentiary requirement. 
6. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(x). 
[T]he mere fact that a person has recorded and released musical compilations or 
performed in theatrical, motion picture, or television productions would be insufficient, 
in and ofitself, to meet this criterion. The evidence must show that the volume of sales 
and box office receipts reflect the person's commercial success relative to others 
involved in similar pursuits in the performing arts. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 10. 
The Petitioner contends that the record demonstrates his "significant achievements and recognition in 
his field, as evidenced by media coverage, [ online] platform recognition, intellectual property 
protections, and sustained public acclaim." 
As SCOPS found, however, "the evidence in the record did not meet the requirements for this 
criterion." This criterion specifically requires evidence of commercial successes "as shown by box 
office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(x) ( emphasis 
added). The Petitioner did not submit any evidence of such receipts or sales or compare them to those 
of other performing artists. See Sarin v. United States, No. 2:22-CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 
5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. July 27, 2023) ( "'As shown by' in the regulatory language indicates that 
the subsequent list is not a list of examples, but is rather an exclusive list.") Nor did the Petitioner 
assert "comparable evidence" by showing that this criterion's standards do not "readily apply" to his 
8 
occupation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4); see generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), Comparable 
Evidence (" [I]f a petitioner demonstrates that a particular criterion does not readily apply to the 
person's occupation, the petitioner may submit evidence that is of comparable significance to that 
criterion.") 
Contrary to the applicable regulation's plain language, the Petitioner did not submit evidence of "box 
office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales" or assert "comparable evidence." 
Thus, he did not meet this evidentiary requirement. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding 
regarding this criterion. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner met two alternate evidentiary criteria. But he demonstrated neither his receipt of a 
major internationally recognized award nor satisfaction of the requisite minimum number of alternate 
evidentiary requirements. We therefore need not reach and hereby reserve consideration of his 
arguments regarding a final merits determination. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) 
(per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary 
to their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not meet the requested immigrant visa category's evidentiary criteria. We will 
therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
