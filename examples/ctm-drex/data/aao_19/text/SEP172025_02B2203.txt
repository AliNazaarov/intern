Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an executive in sustainable energy technology, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had won a major, internationally recognized award or met at least three of the 
remaining criteria for initial evidence of extraordinary ability. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The record shows that in 2024, the Petitioner became the Chief Financial Officer (CFO) of._l____. 
a battery manufacturer. The Petitioner was previously employed at I Ian LED lighting

A. Prior 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
The Petitioner asserts SCOPS did not follow U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) 
policy and explain why, notwithstanding her prior 0-1 nonimmigrant visa petition approval, she failed 
to meet her burden of proof to establish her eligibility for classification as an immigrant with 
extraordinary ability. 0-1 nonimmigrant status is granted to aliens of extraordinary ability who are 
coming temporarily to the United States to work in their field. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2( o)(l)(ii)(A)(l). While 
relevant, prior 0-1 classification does not mandate approval of an immigrant petition for an alien of 
extraordinary ability, which is adjudicated based on different statutory provisions, regulations, and 
case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. 
June 17, 2025) ( disagreeing with plaintiff that USCIS should have approved his immigrant petition 
because USCIS previously granted him 0-1 nonimmigrant status); see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (explaining that "[t]hough the prior approval of an 
0-1 petition is a relevant consideration and can be an indicator of eligibility in adjudicating an 
immigrant petition for a person with extraordinary ability, it is not determinative"). While we 
acknowledge the Petitioner's prior 0-1 nonimmigrant status, for the reasons discussed below, she has 
not established her eligibility for immigrant classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.

High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (describing 
relevant evidence). Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's 
occupation and location should be submitted to establish that a petitioner's compensation is high 
relative to others working in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted her 2020 Service Agreement with I Iwhich states her salary is 
£440,000 per year and she is entitled to receive annual bonuses, the maximum of which will be 150 
percent ofher annual salary. The Petitioner also submitted her 2023-2024 tax record showing her total 
pay from I Iwas £546,023 and a currency conversion showing she earned $595,083. The 
Petitioner's Offer Letter froml Istates her base salary will be $400,000 and she will be eligible 
for a bonus of 25 percent of her base salary. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of the mean and high salaries for chief executives. A printout from 
Glassdoor states the highest salary for CEOs in the United Kingdom is £240,604. A printout from 
the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics shows the mean 
annual wage for chief executives is $258,900. A printout from Careeronestop, which is sponsored by 
the U.S. Department of Labor, states the high salary for chief executives in Texas, wherel lis 
based, is $239,200 or higher. As the Petitioner's renumeration has been and is significantly higher 
than the high salaries for chief executives in the United Kingdom and Texas, she meets this criterion. 
SCOPS' contrary determination is withdrawn. 
Remaining Criteria 
As the Petitioner has established on appeal that she meets three of the initial evidentiary criteria, she 
has satisfied the first part of the Kazarian analysis and we need not address and will reserve 
determination of the Petitioner's eligibility under the remaining criteria she claims to meet relating to 
lesser awards, published material, and original contributions. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 
25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on 
issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). We have considered the evidence relevant to 
these remaining criteria in the following final merits determination. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has established that she meets at least three of the evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). As the Petitioner submitted the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate 
whether she has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or

international acclaim, that she is among the small percentage at the very top of her field of endeavor, 
and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a 
final merits determination, we analyze petitioners' accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have extraordinary 
ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); 
see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts she is recognized internationally as a top expert in her field as 
evidenced by the testimony of leading experts in her field, her awards, media publications discussing 
her and her work, her original contributions of major significance to her field, her authorship of 
scholarly articles, her leading and critical roles for distinguished organizations, and her command of 
a high salary. While the evidence demonstrates the Petitioner's significant accomplishments, it does 
not establish that she is an individual of extraordinary ability. 
Although the Petitioner has authored 11 articles published in professional publications, these articles 
do not reflect sustained national or international acclaim as an executive at the very top of her field. 
The articles were published in 2021, 2023, and 2024. Publication in just three years does not indicate 
sustained acclaim over the course of the Petitioner's over 20-year-long career. Moreover, the 
Petitioner did not submit evidence that her articles have been cited by other executives in her field, or 
have otherwise impacted her field in a manner commensurate with sustained national or international 
acclaim as an executive at the very top of her field. 
The Petitioner also submitted numerous articles about her and her work that were published in 
professional and trade publications and podcasts between 2021 and 2024. While these articles show 
the Petitioner garnered limited media attention over those four years, they do not reflect sustained 
national or international acclaim over the course of her over twenty-year-long career.

has achieved sustained national or international acclaim as an executive at the very top of her field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
oftheir respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing towards the top. USCIS has long held 
that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary 
ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r. 1994). Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown that the significance and recognition of her work are indicative of sustained national or 
international acclaim as one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of her field, as 
extraordinary ability is described at section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
Petitioner is consequently ineligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
