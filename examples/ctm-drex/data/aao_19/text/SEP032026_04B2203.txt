Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a fitness specialist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that the beneficiary: 
• Has "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• Seeks to continue work in his or her field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
can demonstrate international recognition ofthe beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
As the Petitioner has not established that she received a major, internationally recognized award, she 
must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). USCIS 
concluded that she met five criteria: 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (receipt of lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized awards), 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) (published material about the alien in 
major media), 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) (authorship ofscholarly articles), 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) 
(display), and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) (leading or critical role). 
Upon review of the record, we will not disturb USCIS' determination that the Petitioner has satisfied 
at least three initial regulatory criteria. However, in reviewing the totality of the evidence in the 
context of the final merits determination, 1 we conclude that the Petitioner has not shown she garnered 
sustained national or international acclaim and her achievements have been recognized in the field of 
endeavor, demonstrating she is one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 2 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that USCIS erred in concluding that she did not meet two additional 
criteria (membership and original contributions), imposed requirements not found in the regulations 
and failed to consider evidence as a whole, and did not properly consider comparable evidence. 
As we conclude that the Petitioner has met the required three initial criteria, we need not address the 
Petitioner's appellate claims that she also meets three other criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), 
and (viii). See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if her successes are sufficient to demonstrate that she has extraordinary 
ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2),(3); see

Moreover, courts have indicated that providing context for one's accomplishments is necessary for 
showing sustained national or international acclaim. See e.g., Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 
No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. 
United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
(upholding the finding that plaintiff did not contextualize his awards throughout his career to show 
sustained national or international acclaim); Trajano v. US. Citizenship and Immig. Services, No. CV 
25-12388-FDS, 2026 WL 2137917, at *6 (D. Mass. July 24, 2026) (finding that although plaintiff 
provided a great deal of evidence concerning his career and certain accolades he had received, he did 
little to situate those awards and accolades in context to show how they demonstrated extraordinary 
ability). 
Based on the regulations and pertinent case law, we agree with USCIS that the Petitioner has not 
sufficiently contextualized or compared her accomplishments and career to other fitness specialists in 
the field, showing that she enjoys a career that meets a very high standard. The Petitioner stated that 
she intends to "continue her career by establishing herself in the United States as a professional 
bodybuilding athlete and high-performance fitness specialist, applying her extraordinary expertise in 
elite athletic conditioning, advanced nutritional science, and specialized fitness programming." She 
also stated that she will continue to compete at the highest levels of professional bodybuilding within 
the International Fitness and Bodybuilding Federation Professional League (IFBB Pro League). 
The evidence reflects that she earned professional status, competed in bodybuilding events, received 
placements and awards, and participated in events with other accom lished athletes. The record 
showed that in 2022 she won the Figure O at and earned an IFBB 
Pro card. It also documented success at the in 2022 and a top five placement at an 
IFBB Pro com etition at in 2023. Further, in 2016, she received first overall place 
in the _________________ These achievements demonstrate that she 
has attained success in athletic competitions. However, the record does not sufficiently establish how 
her competitive achievements place her among the small percentage at the very top of the field. For 
example, the evidence does not provide sufficient context regarding the significance ofher placements 
compared with other professional competitors, the level of competitions in which she placed, or the 
extent to which her results have distinguished her from others competing at the professional level. 
Professional status and participation in competitions are relevant, but they do not by themselves 
establish extraordinary ability. The record also does not sufficiently demonstrate that, after earning

On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the regulations at 204.5(h)(4) allows for comparable evidence 
if the listed criteria do not readily apply to his occupation. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) states "[i]f the above standards do not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the 
beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision provides petitioners with the opportunity to 
submit comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, ifit is determined that the evidentiary 
criteria described in the regulations do not readily apply to the person's occupation. Id. When 
evaluating such comparable evidence, we must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily 
applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable 
to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
Outside of a very general statement that in professional bodybuilding and fitness competition, the 
"markers of elite status differ significantly from those in more conventional professions," the 
Petitioner did not demonstrate which criterion or criteria do not apply to her occupation, nor does she 
articulate how the evidence submitted is comparable in meeting the particular criterion or criteria. A 
general unsupported assertion that the evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's 
occupation is not probative. SeeJavakhadze v. Mayorkas, No. 3:21-CV-00617-E, 2023 WL 8037730, 
at *6 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 20, 2023) (finding plaintiff did not establish eligibility for comparable evidence 
because brief did not explain how or why the criterion did not apply to the plaintiff's occupation). 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that she qualifies for any criterion through the 
submission of comparable evidence.
