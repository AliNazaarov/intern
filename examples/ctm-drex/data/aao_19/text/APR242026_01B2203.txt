Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a lawyer and prosecutor from Brazil, seeks classification under the employment-based, 
first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
In addition, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5) requires that "the petition must be accompanied 
by clear evidence that the alien is coming to the United States to continue work in the area of 
expertise." The regulation further states that "[s]uch evidence may include letter(s) from prospective 
employer(s), evidence of pre-arranged commitments such as contracts, or a statement from the 
beneficiary detailing plans on how he or she intends to continue his or her work in the United States." 
Id. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner filed the instant petition in July 2025 claiming that he has sustained national and 
international acclaim as "a prosecutor, legal academic and public intellectual whose contributions have 
positioned him at the very top of his field." On the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Worker, 
the Petitioner listed his proposed job title as "Law Teachers, Postsecondary" but his occupation as 
"Lawyers and Judges." 1 The record indicates that the Petitioner worked as a federal prosecutor in Brazil 
since 2014, and is currently pursuing a doctoral degree (Ph.D.) in constitutional law and a master oflaws 
(LLM) degree in international human rights law. The Petitioner also included evidence of his teaching 
engagements at various institutions in Brazil, claiming that through these roles, he has "shaped national 
training programs for federal prosecutors and jurists."

Before we evaluate the totality of evidence under Kazarian, we withdraw SCOPS' initial conclusion 
that the Petitioner satisfied the evidentiary criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). To meet this 
criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that his compensation is high relative to that of others 
working in the field. Such evidence may take many forms and examples may include, but are not 
limited to, geographical or position-appropriate compensation surveys and organizational 
justifications to pay above the compensation data. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 9. 
The Petitioner submitted "Proof of Income Paid and Income Tax Withheld at Source" for 2024 and 
2025 from the ~------------~and claimed that his income exceeded that of 
"senior-level lawyers in large Parana-based companies" shown in the salary survey by Salario BR. 
However, the record does not sufficiently establish that the salary shown on Salario BR's website 
printout is the proper basis for comparison and that his position as a federal prosecutor corresponds to 
senior-level lawyers who work in large companies. Both precedent and case law support this 
application of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 
1994) (considering a professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos 
v. US. Dept. o_fHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information 
for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); Crimson v. INS, 934 F. 
Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); 
Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) (comparing salary of NHL defensive player 
to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
As the Petitioner did not provide a comparable range of salaries with similar experience and skills, we 
conclude that he does not satisfy this criterion. Moreover, while the Petitioner indicated that his 
proposed endeavor is working as a law teacher or professor, he did not provide information regarding 
his salary as a law teacher in Brazil. Therefore, the evidence is also insufficient to show that he has 
commanded salary in the teaching area. 
Despite this determination, the record demonstrates that the Petitioner has met the required three 
criteria. Therefore, we will proceed with the final merits determination below.

B. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS erred in its final merits determination by "fragmenting 
the record instead of assessing [the evidence] as a coherent whole" and ignoring relevant evidence. 
We acknowledge that SCOPS' final merits determination did not include all evidence submitted in the 
record. But we nevertheless agree with SCOPS' ultimate conclusion that the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated he sustained national or international acclaim, 2 he is one of the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor, and his achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
The Petitioner has presented two fields of expertise, one as a lawyer/prosecutor and another as law 
teacher/academic. Therefore, we must consider his extraordinary ability in both areas: "[t]o qualify as a 
person with extraordinary ability, the beneficiary must intend to continue to work in the area of his or 
her expertise." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(2) (discussing whether the 
person's intended employment falls sufficiently within the bounds of his or her area of extraordinary 
ability with an example of a person who is transitioning career, e.g., an acclaimed athlete who intends 
to be employed as an athletic coach). Under this policy guidance, the Petitioner's evidence of acclaim 
as a prosecutor cannot suffice by itself to establish extraordinary ability as a law teacher, but we may 
give proportionate weight to such when the record also shows that the acclaim as a practicing lawyer 
has continued into the transitioned career in teaching. This interpretation has been upheld in federal 
court. In Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914 (N.D. Ill. 2002), the court stated: 
It is reasonable to interpret continuing to work in one's "area of extraordinary ability" 
as working in the same profession in which one has extraordinary ability, not 
necessarily in any profession in that field. For example, Lee's extraordinary ability as 
a baseball player does not imply that he also has extraordinary ability in all positions 
or professions in the baseball industry such as a manager, umpire or coach. 
Id. at 918; see also Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F.Supp.3d 837 (C.D. Ca. 2022) (determining that the 
plaintiffls awards as a water polo player were not awarded as a water polo coach); Integrity Gymnastics 
& Pure Power Cheerleading, LLCv. USCIS, 131 F.Supp.3d 721 (S.D. Oh. 2015) (concluding that the 
AAO's reasoning, relevant statutory and regulatory language, and case law was not arbitrary, 
capricious, or otherwise not in accordance with the law in finding that an Olympic gold medal gymnast 
must meet the extraordinary ability classification through her achievements as a coach, her intended 
area of expertise). 
With this background, we evaluate the totality of the evidence and conclude that the record does not 
show that the Petitioner's teaching activities, in addition to his career as a lawyer and prosecutor, have 
been at a level consistent with sustained national or international acclaim in the context of a final 
merits determination.

others in the field or that he was featured in published materials as a lawyer, aside from his name being

Petitioner requested that we accept the news articles featuring the criminal case as comparable 
evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) as prosecutors themselves generally do not receive public media

Petitioner individually, which is what the statue and regulations require, and the Petitioner has not 
sufficiently demonstrated that published materials about this trial set him above other prosecutors who 
worked in the case and places him at the top of the field. 
Additionally, the Petitioner did not establish that he commanded earnings commensurate with 
sustained national or international acclaim. He did not demonstrate, for example, how his salary 
compares to others at the very top of the field or other renowned prosecutors or lawyers who hold

salary separating himself from others in the field or placing him in the upper echelon. See Bodhankar 
v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 
(finding that the plaintiff's high salary was not so high as to indicate that he has risen to the very top 
of the field). Moreover, the Petitioner did not show that he consistently earned high wages throughout 
his career rather than providing salary information for the last two-year period before filing of the 
petition. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 
WL 69056, at *9 (finding the failure to show consistent high wage earnings did not reflect sustained 
national or international acclaim and a single year's, or even a few years', worth ofsalary data unlikely 
to satisfy this requirement).

Accordingly, the record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the 
Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. 7 The Petitioner has not demonstrated that his 
achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. 
H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990).8 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa 
classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those 
progressing toward the top. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) 
( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for 
classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating 
that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 
8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be 
available to only an exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, 
the Petitioner has not shown the significance ofhis work is indicative ofthe required sustained national 
or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more 
accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he 
is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
