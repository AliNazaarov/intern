Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a biomedical and genetics researcher, requests classification under the employment­
based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). Successful 
petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate their receipt of "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their 
fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
U.S Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the petition. The Acting Associate 
Director of Service Center Operations concluded that the Petitioner met two of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria- one less than needed for a final merits determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that she satisfied a third criterion by submitting evidence of original scholarly 
contributions of major significance in her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that she satisfied the third evidentiary requirement. But, because the record does 
not demonstrate acclaim and recognition sufficient to place her among the small percentage at her 
field's top, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for the requested immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, USCIS must then make a 
final merits determination. To merit final approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a 
petitioner's receipt of "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements 
placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 
383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (holding that USCIS' two-step analysis "is consistent with the governing 
statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and Her Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner earned a bachelor ofscience degree in bioinforrnatics and a master 
of science degree in biology from universities in her home country of China. In 2022, a U.S. medical 
school awarded her a doctoral degree in bioinforrnatics and computational biology. Since obtaining 
her Ph.D., the Petitioner has worked as a post-doctoral researcher at a U.S. university. At the time of 
the petition's filing, she had authored or co-authored 16 published peer-reviewed research articles. 
The Petitioner stated that her post-doctoral research focuses on using single-cell multi-omic 
sequencing to study how genes are regulated in individual cells. She stated that, in the future, she 
wants to obtain a U.S. faculty position and establish her own research group, focusing on gene 
regulation during pediatric development. She stated: "By integrating large-scale genomic datasets 
and collaborating with multidisciplinary teams, I aim to uncover specific regulatory events throughout 
childhood development and understand how their malfunctions lead to disease." 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - her receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. She must therefore meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports the Director's findings that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of her: 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in her or an allied field; and 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in her field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi).

B. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, art1st1c, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
When adjudicating this evidentiary criterion, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner made 
original contributions to their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. If 
so, USCIS then determines whether the contributions had major significance to the field. Id. 
Evidence of major significance may include "published research that has provoked widespread 
commentary on its importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been 
highly cited relative to others' work in that field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 5. 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the 
person's contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed 
original contributions of major significance, particularly when the record includes 
documentation corroborating the claimed significance. 
Id. Expert letters should specifically describe a petitioner's contribution and its significance to the 
field and state the basis of the writer's knowledge and expertise. Id. 
As evidence of claimed original contributions of major significance, the Petitioner submitted 
documentation of her published research articles in scientific journals. The record contains excerpts 
from 16 journal articles. The materials indicate that the Petitioner served as first-author on two of the 
16 total articles. The remaining articles list her as a co-author, sometimes one of many co-authors. 
The Petitioner also submitted letters from four independent researchers in her field and two co­
researchers, and information about citations to the articles. 
The Director found that the Petitioner made original contributions but concluded that she did not 
establish their major significance to her field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Director 
erred in requiring her to demonstrate that her "own individual work" - as opposed to the collective 
work of research teams to which she belonged - had major significance to her field. 
In a request for additional evidence (RFE), the Director noted that the research articles that the 
Petitioner co-authored received many more citations than those that she first-authored. At the time of 
the petition's filing, a scholarly profile that she submitted indicated that her first-authored articles 
generated far less of the total citations to her work. The Petitioner's RFE response argued that a co­
author could make original contributions of major significance in a field. But the Director's decision 
found that "the record does not document that the [Petitioner]' s own individual work has already 
impacted the broader field in a major way." 
The Petitioner argues: 
Nothing in the governing statutes or USCIS policy guidance requires a petitioner to 
segregate his or her own work from that ofthe research team to which he or she belongs. 
3 
As explained in the petitioner's response to the RFE, science is inherently 
collaborative. No single scientist can independently execute large-scale, complex 
research projects - particularly in the biomedical field. Collaboration and co­
authorship are standard practice, and co-authors are credited with research results both 
individually and collectively. 
As an example, the Petitioner cites the 2025 Nobel Prize for physiology and medicine. She submitted 
evidence that one of the prize's three winners did not first-author any of the publications that the prize 
committee cited but rather served as a co-author on all of them. 
We agree with the Petitioner that a research co-author could demonstrate original contributions of 
major significance in a field. But we find that that co-author would first have to establish their 
individual contributions to the relevant research project(s) and explain the significance of their 
individual contributions on that project to the field. 
We base our finding on the applicable regulation. This evidentiary criterion requires "evidence of the 
alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major 
significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) (emphasis added). The regulation focuses on the 
individual's contributions, not those oftheir team. Just because an alien contributes to a large research 
project of major significance in their field does not necessarily demonstrate that their individual 
contributions to that project had major significance. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 5 ("Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes 
major, significant contributions to the field.") (emphasis added). 
Case law also indicates that performance of part of an important project in one's field does not 
necessarily equate to a contribution of major significance. See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 
2021 WL 5817324, at *5 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-
01468-JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (affirming USCIS' conclusion that the "record 
does not adequately demonstrate that coaching one member ofa five-person team which won a bronze 
medal would be comparable to a contribution of major significance"). 
Nevertheless, while we disagree that the Petitioner's collective work can constitute original 
contributions of major significance, we find that the letters from her co-researchers and the 
independent researchers demonstrate that she played significant roles in major research projects. A 
researcher at a pharmaceutical company's genomic medicine unit worked with the Petitioner on a 2023 
project involving mammalian evolution of cis-regulatory elements and transcription-factor binding 
sites and outlined the impact her work had on the project. 
Another researcher, an associate professor of molecular biosciences at a Swedish university, stated 
that he closely collaborated with the Petitioner on his post-doctoral research and co-authored several 
articles with her. Regarding a 2021 project involving I 
clusters from mammalian genes, the researcher stated that the Petitioner: 
co-led this project .. and was centrally involved from conception through publication; 
[the Petitioner] played a leading role in shaping the study design .... The conceptual

The Petitioner has demonstrated that she made important contributions to major research projects in 
her field. She has therefore submitted evidence of original contributions of major significance in her 
field. We will therefore withdraw the Director's contrary findings. 
The Petitioner has met three evidentiary criteria. users must therefore make a final merits 
determination on her petition. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3); Amin, 24 F. 4th at 391. The Director did 
not make such a finding. We will therefore make one now. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
To establish eligibility, the Petitioner must demonstrate her receipt of sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognition of her achievements in her field sufficient to show that she is 
one of that small percentage who has risen to her field's very top. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). Congress intended classification as an alien with extraordinary ability to reflect a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field." See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
Under USCrS policy: 
[t]he petitioner must provide sufficient context . . . to establish the person's 
extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances. This means that the petitioner 
must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that 
the person has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). We must consider, in the aggregate, any potentially relevant 
evidence of record, even if it does not fit one of the regulatory criteria or was not presented as 
comparable evidence. Id. 
5 
l. Judge of Others' Work 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, in 2025, she "peer-reviewed" more than ten articles by other 
researchers for publication in international, scientific journals in her field. But she has not 
demonstrated that the amount or quality of her reviews place her among the small percentage at her 
field's top. See Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *23 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 
2025) (upholding USCIS' finding that "the petitioner has not shown how the beneficiary has judged 
to such an extent, or at a high enough level, that her judging surpasses the majority of the field and 
places her in the top echelons of anesthesiology and pain management"). 
Also, the record indicates that all of the Petitioner's judging activities occurred in 2025. Thus, any 
acclaim that she received from the activities would not be "sustained" in nature. See section 
203(b )(I )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring demonstration of "sustained national or international acclaim"). 
2. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
As previously discussed, the Petitioner submitted evidence that she has played significant roles in 
major research projects in her field. She also provided letters from independent researchers praising 
her work. 
The Petitioner, however, has not sufficiently demonstrated that her projects and body of work reflect 
extraordinary ability in her field. See 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("[T]he petitioner must 
explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that the person has 
achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise.") She 
herself acknowledged that she has not established her own research group. The record also does not 
indicate that she obtained funding for any of the studies on which she participated. Id. (stating that 
designation as an investigator, scientist, or researcher on a peer-reviewed and competitively-funded 
U.S. government grant or stipend may indicate extraordinary ability in a STEM field). 
3. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The record shows that, at the time of the petition's filing, the Petitioner had authored or co-authored 
16 published research articles, some of which appeared in highly ranked publications. But she has not 
established that that output level reflects a "career of acclaimed work in the field." See H.R. Rep. No. 
101-723, at 59; see alsoJafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. 
Jan. 5, 2024) ("It was not unreasonable for USCIS to focus ... on the fact that a 'publication record of 
six publications from 2015 and 2022' is inconsistent with 'a career ofacclaimed work and sustain[ed] 
national or international acclaim.'" 
Considering the evidence in the aggregate, the record identifies the Petitioner as a talented researcher 
who has made original contributions to major projects in her field. She met the requested category's 
initial evidentiary requirements and demonstrated her receipt of some acclaim and recognition. But, 
even considering the evidence in its totality, the Petitioner has not established herself among the small 
percentage at her field's top. The record indicates that she has yet to achieve a "career of acclaimed 
work in the field." See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
6 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability, reserving this 
immigrant visa category "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very top of their 
field of endeavor." Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991 ). Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner has not established her claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. See Amin, 24 F.4th 383, 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary 
ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [ a 
petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not 
automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner met the required number of initial evidentiary criteria for the requested immigrant visa 
petition and demonstrated her receipt of some acclaim and recognition in her field. But she has not 
established her claimed extraordinary ability in the field. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
