Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an analyst of the global Middle East region, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the petition. The Deputy Associate 
Director of Adjudications for Service Center Operations found that the Petitioner met the requested 
category's initial evidentiary requirements. But, in a final merits determination, the Director 
concluded that the Petitioner did not demonstrate sufficient acclaim or recognition to establish his 
claimed extraordinary ability in his field. On appeal, the Petitioner claims that the Director overlooked 
and misanalysed evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with the Director that the Petitioner did not establish himself as one of the small 
percentage at his field's top. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for the requested immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, USCIS must then make a 
final merits determination. To merit final approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a 
petitioner's receipt ofsustained national or international acclaim and recognized achievements placing 
them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 
( 5th Cir. 2022) ( finding USCIS' two-step inquiry to be "consistent with the governing statute and 
regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. Final Merits Determination 
To establish final eligibility, the Petitioner must demonstrate his receipt of sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognition of his achievements in his field sufficient to show that he is one 
of that small percentage who has risen to his field's very top. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8)(2). Congress intended classification as an alien with extraordinary ability to reflect a "career 
of acclaimed work in the field." See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
Under USCIS policy: 
[t]he petitioner must provide sufficient context . . . to establish the person's 
extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances. This means that the petitioner 
must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that 
the person has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(2). We must consider, in the aggregate, any potentially relevant 
evidence of record, even if it does not fit one of the regulatory criteria or was not presented as 
comparable evidence. Id.

On appeal, the Petitioner does not deny that the award stems from his work at the university's French

as a Middle East analyst. He states: 
Several countries of the Middle East and North Africa[,] particularly those formerly

The Petitioner adds:

region shaped by linguistic variation and colonial legacies. 
We do not doubt that the Petitioner's knowledge of French language and culture helps him in his work 
as a Middle East analyst. But he has not demonstrated that the award honored him for excellence in 
his claimed field of Middle East analysis. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 184 (D.N.J. 
2021) (upholding our finding that "this award honors the Petitioner for her contributions to the 
promotion of human rights, it does not on its face constitute an award for excellence in the field of 
[her stated expertise].") 
The Act and regulations require the Petitioner to demonstrate acclaim and recognition in his field. See 
section 203(b )(I)( A )(i) of the Act (requiring acclaim and recognition of achievements "in the field"); 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) (requiring acclaim and recognition "in the field of expertise"); see generalZv 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("To establish eligibility, the petition must demonstrate that the 
person has sustained national or international acclaim and that their achievements have been 
recognized in the field ofexpertise.") ( emphasis added). Thus, the record does not demonstrate that 
the Petitioner's award garnered him acclaim and recognition in the relevant field. 
2. Membership in Associations 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his purported membership in a handful of associations. The 
evidence indicates, for example, that he served on: the editorial board of an encyclopedia about Arab 
and Muslim figures in literature and science; a regional committee of an association of French­
speaking universities and research institutions; and the executive board of a Yemeni foundation for 
cultural and political development. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that these memberships relate to his stated field of 
Middle East analysis. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring acclaim and recognition of 
achievements "in the field"); see also Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 846 (C.D. Cal. 
2022) (finding that a water polo coach could not meet this criterion through her membership as an 
Olympic athlete because she never participated in the Olympics as a water polo coach and never 
coached an Olympic team). Also, the record does not indicate that the Petitioner's memberships have 
garnered him much acclaim or recognition. See Hamal v. United States Dep 't of Homeland Sec., 
4 
No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *3 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) ("Hamal's evidence of membership 
... , while demonstrating his professional stature, . . . did not establish the exceptional level of 
distinction required for this extraordinary-ability visa.") 
3. Published Materials About the Petitioner 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, between 2013 and 2022, nine online publications, videos, or 
podcasts mentioned him. Two of these media - a podcast about immigration through the eyes of his 
teen-aged son and a news report on a gathering to remember victims of terrorism in Paris, France -
did not mention his work in his field. The remaining media reported on his participation in conferences 
and roundtable discussions regarding the Middle East. 
The record shows that the publications garnered the Petitioner some acclaim and recognition in his 
field. But he has not demonstrated that this level of media attention places him among the small 
percentage at his field's very top. See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 
2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021 ), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., supra ( affirming our finding that the Plaintiff failed to show that "his press coverage 
is indicative of a level of success consistent with being" at the top of his field); see generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("The petitioner must provide sufficient context ... to establish the person's 
extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances.") Thus, the publications, alone, do not 
demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in the field of Middle East analysis. 
4. Judge of Others' Work 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he served as the chief editor ofan academic journal at a Yemeni 
university and reviewed others' scholarly articles for an international journal. He also stated that he 
served as chief editor of a Yemeni encyclopedia, and that, as chair of the French department at the 
university, he judged scholarly articles for publication and academic promotion. 
The Petitioner, however, has not sufficiently demonstrated that these judging activities garnered him 
acclaim or recognition. See section 203(b )(I )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring demonstrated acclaim and 
recognition "in the field"); Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *23 (D. Neb. 
Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding USCIS' finding that "the petitioner has not shown how the beneficiary has 
judged to such an extent, or at a high enough level, that her judging surpasses the majority of the field 
and places her in the top echelons of anesthesiology and pain management"). 
5. Original Contributions 
The record shows that the Petitioner has made original contributions to his field of Middle East 
analysis. He has published articles, spoken at numerous conferences, and lectured at various 
universities and institutions around the world. The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that 
these contributions generated acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small 
percentage at his field's top. See Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. Ctr., USCIS, No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 
458001, at *4 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (affirming USCIS' finding that the petitioner's evidence of 
original contributions did not demonstrate sustained national or international acclaim); see generally 
5 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("The petitioner must provide sufficient context ... to establish the 
person's extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances.") 
The Petitioner submitted recommendation letters from professors, a former U.S. ambassador to 
Yemen, and Yemen's former foreign minister. The letters praise the Petitioner as a knowledgeable 
expert on Middle Eastern affairs, in general, and Yemen, in particular. The letters also describe his 
expertise as relevant to current affairs in the region. 
The letters, however, do not explain the Petitioner's place or rank within his field. See Bodhankar v. 
USCIS, No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) ("Letters written 
on Plaintiff's behalf describe Plaintiff's contributions to the field as original and unique, yet they fail 
to establish that they resulted in significant acclaim."); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(2) ("The petitioner must provide sufficient context ... to establish the person's extraordinary 
ability in the totality ofthe circumstances.") Also, the letters indicate that all their authors have worked 
with the Petitioner. "It is generally expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained 
national or international acclaim would have received recognition for their accomplishments well 
beyond the circle of their personal and professional acquaintances." Mishra v. Richardson, 
No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021); 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(3). Thus, the Petitioner's contributions to his field, alone, do not establish his claimed 
extraordinary ability. 
6. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The Petitioner claims to have written more than 100 books and scholarly articles. But the record 
indicates that most of these writings do not relate to Middle East analysis but rather to other fields, 
such as: education; translations; literature; and history. 
Regarding the Petitioner's writings on Middle East analysis, he has not placed their number ofcitations 
or the ranking of the journals or publications in which they appeared in context with those of others in 
his field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. CV 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) 
( affirming USCTS' decision that the petitioner "did not establish that his authorships reflect[] that he 
is among the small percentage at the very top of his field"); see generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(2) (stating that a high rate of citations or publications in high-ranked journals may indicate 
extraordinary ability). The Petitioner's authorship of scholarly articles, alone, therefore does not place 
him among the small percentage at his field's top. 
7. Display of Work 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has lectured about the Middle East at numerous international 
conferences. As we previously indicated, these activities garnered him some acclaim and recognition. 
But he has not demonstrated that this acclaim and recognition place him among the small percentage 
at his field's top. See Hamal, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 ("Hamal did not show 'that his plays brought 
praise from critics, drew notable crowds, raised attendance, or were responsible for the success or 
standing of the event;"') see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("The petitioner must 
provide sufficient context ... to establish the person's extraordinary ability in the totality of the 
6 
circumstances.") Thus, display of the Petitioner's work, alone, does not establish his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field.

Considering all the evidence in the aggregate, the record identifies the Petitioner as a talented and 
knowledgeable Middle East analyst, educator, and writer. He met the requested category's initial 
evidentiary requirements and demonstrated his receipt of some acclaim and recognition in his stated 
field of Middle East analysis. But, even considering the evidence in the aggregate, the Petitioner has 
not established himself among the small percentage at the field's top. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability, reserving this 
immigrant visa category "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very top of their 
field of endeavor." Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991). Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner has not established his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. See Amin, 24 F.4th 383, 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary 
ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a 
petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not 
automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner met the initial evidentiary requirements for the requested immigrant visa classification. 
But he did not demonstrate acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage 
at his field's top. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
