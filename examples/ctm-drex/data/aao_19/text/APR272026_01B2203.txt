The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence.

I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
m. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated the Beneficiary's receipt of a major, 
internationally recognized award, the Beneficiary must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Beneficiary claimed to meet eight criteria: 
receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards (i); membership in 
associations (ii); published material about the alien (iii); judge of the work of others (iv); original 
contributions (v); authorship of scholarly articles in the field (vi); performed in a leading or critical 
role (viii); and high salary or remuneration (ix). SCOPS concluded the Beneficiary met two of the 
claimed evidentiary criteria, receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards 
and authorship of scholarly articles in the field. But because SCOPS granted only two of the claimed 
initial criteria, SCOPS did not undertake a final merits determination. On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains the Beneficiary meets all eight criteria. 
Upon de novo review, we conclude the Beneficiary has demonstrated that he meets the criterion at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and therefore satisfied the initial evidence requirements for this classification. 
Specifically, the Beneficiary has established, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he has 
participated as a judge of the work of others in the same or allied field, as required by the plain 
language of the criterion.

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Beneficiary has satisfied at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) and therefore meets the initial evidence requirements for this classification. We therefore need not 
address whether the Beneficiary meets any additional evidentiary criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 
429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
However, granting the third initial criterion does not suffice to establish the Beneficiary's eligibility 
for the classification the Petitioner seeks. USCIS must now make a final merits determination based 
on a review ofthe totality ofthe evidence the Petitioner provided. SCOPS did not make such a finding. 
Rather than make this determination in the first instance, we will remand the matter to SCOPS. 
On remand, SCOPS must analyze the Beneficiary's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence, including the evidence and arguments provided on appeal, to determine ifhe established his 
extraordinary ability in his field of endeavor based on the statutory and regulatory standards. See 
section 203(b )(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119 -
20. SCOPS should consider any potentially relevant evidence of record, even if it does not fit one of 
the evidentiary criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The type and quality of evidence should 
determine the petition's approval or denial. Id. 
III. CONCLUSION 
By a preponderance ofthe evidence, the Beneficiary has satisfied three initial evidentiary criteria. We 
will remand the matter to SCOPS for a final merits determination. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision
