Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a computer engineer who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner had received a major, internationally recognized award, nor did he 
demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility to 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met six of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and the authorship 
of scholarly articles, but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with published material, 
original contributions, performing in a leading or critical role, or a high salary or remuneration. On 
appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he meets the evidentiary criteria SCOPS denied. After reviewing 
all the evidence in the record, we conclude that he has satisfied at least one additional requirement in 
the high salary or other significantly high remuneration criterion. 
l. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
In making the adverse determination for this criterion, SCOPS found that his job did not properly align 
with the software developer information he provided and that he should have offered evidence directly 
related to his job title. SCOPS stated that"[ o ]pen-source and publicly available data shows that Device 
Driver Engineer or Device Engineer is a recognized position in the industry with a separate salary 
information." But on appeal, the Petitioner notes that the U.S. Department ofLabor's ETA Form 9035 
& 9035E, Labor Condition Application for Nonimmigrant Workers classifies him as a software 
developer and he explains the similarities between his job's duties and the Occupational Information 
Network tasks for the Software Developers Standard Occupational Classificational code. We agree 
that this was an appropriate comparison for this criterion. And we conclude that the Petitioner has 
demonstrated that his base pay is adequate to meet this criterion's requirements. 
As the Petitioner has now satisfied at least three of the criteria, it is unnecessary that we evaluate 
whether his evidence meets the other claimed criteria.

B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether he has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim and 
that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor, and that his achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, 
we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20); Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (stating that USCIS officers should then evaluate the evidence 
together when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has established by 
a preponderance of the evidence the required high level of expertise of the immigrant classification). 
In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not documented his eligibility. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 0; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 
2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
According to the Petitioner's correspondence, he earned a Master of Science in Computer Engineering 
from a U.S. institution of higher education, a post-graduate diploma course in cyber security and 
forensics, and an undergraduate degree in electrical and communications engineering from his home 
country. He is approaching two decades of experience within the semiconductor software industry. 
As indicated above, the Petitioner judged others, authored scholarly works, and earned a high salary. 
The record, however, does not demonstrate that his personal and professional achievements rise to a 
level of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 
101-723, at 59 (1990).

and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy

Salary or remuneration. Although we decided that the Petitioner met the high salary criterion, it is 
unclear where he derived his figures expressed in the appeal and we do not agree with his implication that 
he has earned a disproportionately high compensation persisting over multiple years that are corroborated 
by external labor-market benchmarks. While his base salary was adequate to place him among the upper 
percentages of those performing similar work in the same geographic region, it was not so high to place 
him at or near the very top. See Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at 
*5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding that the alien's high salary was not so high as to indicate that he 
has risen to the very top of the field). See also Hamal, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding that earning a 
high salary alone was not sufficient to establish a singularly extraordinary artist). Even ifthe Petitioner's 
base salary was in the top 10 percent-which it was not-that is not adequate to show sustained acclaim 
on a national or international level. Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at 
*9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding the petitioner's salary in the top 10 percent of employees was not 
so impressive as to place him at the very top of the field of endeavor). 
And even though he provided material representing his remuneration beyond his base salary, lacking from 
the record is comparable figures relating to those at or near the top of the field. The Petitioner has not 
provided information that would enable us to provide a proper determination of whether his salary is 
reflective "ofthat small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 
Published material. While the Petitioner provided media coverage that was about him and his work in 
the field, he did not illustrate how this coverage rose to the level illustrating he was among those in the 
top of his field. General recognition for his work efforts does not categorically place him among the top 
echelon of his field. See Hamal v. DHS, No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 
8, 2021), ajf'd sub nom. Hamal v. DHS, 2023 WL 1156801 (upholding the determination that the filing 
party did not show his press coverage was indicative ofa level of success consistent with being at the top 
of his field). The Petitioner has not explained how seven articles over the most recent three-year period 
prior to the petition filing date demonstrates his sustained national or international acclaim necessary for 
this highly restrictive classification when his career spans nearly over two decades. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act. Therefore, the Petitioner did not establish that the media reporting on him and 
his work activities reflects a career of acclaimed work in the field or that it was indicative of the very 
high standard to present more extensive documentation. See H.R. Rep. No. l 0 1-723, at 59 ( 1990); 56 
Fed. Reg. at 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991).

Contributions. The Petitioner characterizes his contributions to the field around four claims: ( l) an over 
the air (OTA) software solution at an augmented reality company, (2) a device lifecycle and service 
management platform at an electronic payment solutions company, (3) input/output (IO) self-testing 
software, and (4) his work on a USB forum. We observe a common thread that nms through three ofthe 
four claims in the form of the Petitioner adopting an existing information technology concept someone 
else created, then applying that to a new situation. The Petitioner was not applying concepts that were 
foreign to the information technology industry and instead was simply modifying a common process. 
Without distinct changes to what already existed in combination with an alteration in how it was applied, 
that process arguably lacks originality on the Petitioner's part. 
For the OTA updates to a device's lifecycle and security, that effort might have been critical to the 
device's infrastructure, but he has not demonstrated that OTA updates was a concept he devised rather 
than one that existed in the industry long before he simply adopted it while working at an augmented 
reality company. Similarly, his work at an electronic payment solutions company involved him 
developing solutions to architecture issues. The claims for his work relating to this company are better 
placed under the leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments than they are as 
original contributions to the field. Regarding his work on USB4 initiatives, he applied software 
principles from other areas in the form of input/output (IO) testing methods used to determine how 
much a system can deviate from its ideal operating conditions before failing. 
And finally, his work on the IO self-testing software, the claim that this was originally his creation is in 
question due to conflicting evidence in the record. Within the letter from the Senior Director of Software 
Engineering at the Petitioner's employer that he submitted with the initial filing, they stated that although 
the Petitioner played an essential role in, and was the original developer working on, the self-testing tool, 
it was a group of over 25 personnel who actually performed the bulk of the work and completed the tool 
under his leadership. He was identified as the individual who maintains the tool, but from the Senior 
Director's letter, it is not apparent that this was an original contribution of major significance that can be 
fully attributed to the Petitioner. This conflicts with information in other support letters that asserted he 
"envisioned, designed, developed, and deployed" the claimed software tool. The Petitioner has submitted 
at least two forms of evidence that put forth conflicting facts. Consequently, he has created a visible 
friction within the record, which he must resolve through some form of evidence that preponderantly 
establishes where the truth lies. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988). 
The record may show that an alien's work had practical impact-such as successful deployment in a 
product, performance gains, or commercial benefits-but the evidence may not demonstrate that this 
impact stemmed from a substantially original intellectual contribution. The underlying techniques or 
processes may have been well established in the field before the alien's involvement, and their role may 
have consisted primarily of implementing, adapting, or incrementally optimizing those existing methods 
for a new device or context. That type of adaptation can reflect notable scientific work, but it does not 
necessarily reflect a new algorithm, framework, or conceptual approach that meaningfully departs from 
prior work. Taken together, the record can reasonably be viewed as demonstrating the Petitioner's 
impactful, professional work that applies and refines known techniques, but not as establishing that his 
individual efforts amount to original contributions of major significance to the field. 
Because his work might have been original to the situation, but was not conceptually originally his work, 
it is much less persuasive. Without a showing of originality, the Petitioner has not illustrated how his

Courts emphasize that evidence must demonstrate how a role resulted in a reputation commensurate 
with being at the very top of the field, reflecting that the impact of a petitioner's work was viewed by 
the field as unusually influential or significantly important. Agrawal, 2025 WL 2879217, at *24 
(upholding that alien's evidence failed to describe how they had garnered sustained national or 
international reputation through their roles); Bodhankar, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (finding that the filing 
party's work for organizations did not result in national or international acclaim). Without specific 
documentation linking organizational success to personal, sustained recognition, high-ranking positions 
do not inherently satisfy this standard. Joseph, 2024 WL 1299065, at *7, report and recommendation 
adopted, 2024 WL 1289452, aff'd, 2025 WL 458001 (upholding the determination that the alien's 
leading role in a successful company does not necessarily demonstrate a sustained career or the impact 
of his work has risen to the level where it places him at the very top of the field).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's 
burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that 
burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
