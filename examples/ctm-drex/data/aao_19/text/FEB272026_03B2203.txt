Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a researcher and academic in the aerospace engineering field, seeks classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner 
satisfied at least three ofthe initial evidentiary criteria, as required, she did not show her sustained national 
or international acclaim and demonstrate she is among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field of 
endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Under section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act, an individual is eligible for the extraordinary ability 
classification if: (i) they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and their 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) they seek to 
enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) their entry into 
the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner may demonstrate 
international recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award). Absent such an achievement, the petitioner must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation demonstrating that they meet at least three of the ten criteria listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 , 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS ' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-
32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

As to the letters ofreference provided, as noted above, we acknowledge that the Petitioner's colleagues 
in various countries have relied on her research in their own work. However, the letters do not provide 
sufficient context to understand where the field was prior to the Petitioner's contributions such that 
her work could be considered of major significance within the meaning of the regulation. Moreover, 
none of the letters provide a point of comparison for us to evaluate how the Petitioner's contributions 
relate to other researchers in the field. For example, because many of the Petitioner's research 
publications are co-authored, the writers do not distinguish if the Petitioner's co-authors would also 
be considered to have made original contributions of major significance to the field, or if it was the 
Petitioner's work alone that me1its their praise. Similarly, the testimonial letters describe the 
Petitioner's impact on the writers' individual research and her strong leadership abilities and technical 
knowledge (which we acknowledge is well-above an entry level researcher), however none of the 
authors compare her to other researchers in the field to provide a standard for finding that her 
contributions should be deemed of major significance. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & 
Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding testimonial letters were 
insufficient to establish his relative status in the field and did not compare him to others). Lastly, the 
Petitioner's collaboration with international scholars and researchers in her field demonstrates that she 
has consistently worked and garnered a level ofvisibility in her field, however that alone is insufficient 
to establish the "major significance" of her contributions, as contemplated by the regulation. 
The Petitioner asserts that her contributions are of major significance because they have advanced the 
field of aerospace engineering. In addition, she notes that her contributions to academic journals as 
an editor and contributor, and her international collaborations and reputation in the field, collectively 
amount to original contributions of major significance, within the meaning of the regulation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that our policy emphasizes that a relatively high citation rate and a 
high h-index is "considered a highly reliable metric for ranking researcher impact." We acknowledge 
that the h-index of a researcher is the number of papers authored by the researcher with at least h 
number of citations for each publication, which the Petitioner has co-authored at least 13 publications 
8 
that garnered at least 13 citations. However, the Petitioner does not provide evidence to establish if 
this is high "relative to others in the field" such that this measure demonstrates the "major significance" 
of the impact of her research on the field. Furthermore, while we have considered the Petitioner's 
assertion that her h-index of 13 places her in the top 3% ofauthors publishing in aerospace engineering, 
this is a quantitative measure of impact whereas our inquiry requires us to undergo a qualitative 
analysis of the impact of her work on the field. 3 
Taken as a whole, the Petitioner's research, leadership, and academic contributions to the field of 
aerospace engineering are well documented and demonstrate that she has progressed to positions of 
higher visibility and that she has garnered a positive international reputation among her peers. 
However, as presently constituted, the record falls short of establishing that her original contributions 
have been of "major significance" to the field. 
For all these reasons, the Petitioner has not met the requirements for the criterion and we withdraw 
SCOPS' contrary finding. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
While we have concluded that the Petitioner has not fulfilled the initial evidentiary requirement of 
three criteria, and thus need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20, we will nonetheless consider the issue ofthe Petitioner's qualification 
under that standard, as it is the focus of both SCOPS' decision and the appeal. We have reviewed the 
record in the aggregate and agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner has not established eligibility as a 
person of extraordinary ability. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if her successes are sufficient to demonstrate that she has extraordinary 
ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); 
see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.4 Because extraordinary ability is an elite level of 
accomplishment whose recognition necessarily entails a judgement call, it cannot be established 
through meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria alone. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 
394-395. The final merits determination is the ultimate statutory inquiry ofwhether the applicant has 
extraordinary ability, as demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or 
international acclaim, 5 she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and 
her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation.

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended 
for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the 
top. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that 
the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design,"); Hamal v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec. (Hamal 11), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
and see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal 1), No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 
(D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian v. USClS, 596 F.3d at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a 
published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that 
"[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. 
Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. Ill. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous 
baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). While the Petitioner 
need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the 
record is insufficient to demonstrate that she has sustained national or international acclaim and is 
among the small percentage at the top of her field. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2).
