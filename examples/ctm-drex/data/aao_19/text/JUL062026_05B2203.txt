Form I-I40, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a jurist, legal scholar and university professor in his home country of Brazil, seeks 
classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference (EB-I) classification makes 
immigrant visas available to aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability in the sciences, 
arts, education, business or athletics through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had met at 
least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification and that he seeks to enter the 
United States to continue working in an area of extraordinary ability that falls within "the sciences, 
arts, education, business or athletics." USCIS based its decision, in significant part, on a determination 
that the Petitioner claims extraordinary ability in the field of "law and judicial systems" and that "the 
field oflaw, the practice of law [and] work in the field oflaw is not one of the fields within the EB-I 
category" under section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If the petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022).

On the petition, the Petitioner indicated that his occupation is in the "Legal" field and that his proposed 
employment would fall under the Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) Code for "Judges, 
Magistrate Judges and Magistrates." In a supporting letter, the Petitioner indicated his intent to 
"further his legal scholarship and expand his academic and advisory engagements in the United

guest lectures on comparative judicial systems, research collaborations in areas such as human rights 
and electoral law, and advisory contributions to legal reforms. 
In a request for evidence (RFE), USCIS stated: 
The statute requires that you demonstrate extraordinary ability "in the sciences, arts, 
education, business or athletics" and that the beneficiary "seeks to enter the United 
States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability." See sections 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) and (ii) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A)(i) and (ii). 
In the record of proceeding, you provided evidence indicating that your field and 
expertise primarily lies in Law, that you have degrees in law, and that you have, are 
and will be coming to the U.S. to continue focusing on law, practicing law and working 
in the field oflaw. 
2 
The RFE farther stated that "the practice of law is a profession for purposes of eligibility for the EB-
2 immigrant visa" under section 203(b)(2)(A) of the Act, and that "Congress specifically included 
members of the professions in sections 203(b )(2)(A) and 203(b )(3)(A)(ii) and excluded them from 
section 203(b)(1 )(A). USCIS reasoned that "if Congress had intended all aliens of extraordinary 
ability, regardless of their field, to qualify under section 203(b)(l)(A), there would have been no 
purpose in including the phrase "in the sciences, arts, education, business or athletics." 
In response to the RFE, the Petitioner emphasized that he has distinguished himself not through the 
practice of law, but in the field of education, noting that his eligibility "is rooted in [his] educational 
and scholarly leadership" and has resulted in "invitations to speak and engage academically in the 
United States." He asserted that his achievements in law "overwhelmingly concern his work as a 
professor, director-general of a judicial school, president of an association of judicial education 
institutions, author of instructional texts, and leadership of national training and academic programs," 
which he stated are "textbook examples of accomplishments in education ... even though the subject 
matter of the education involves law and judicial systems." 
USCIS denied the petition for the reasons stated in the RFE, concluding that Petitioner did not claim 
extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business or athletics, or intend to work in one of 
these areas in the United States, as required under section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(ii) ofthe Act. 1 The decision 
briefly acknowledges the Petitioner's explanation that his area of extraordinary ability falls within the 
field of education. However, USCIS disregarded that explanation, emphasizing that he "filed Form I-
140 for the specific position of Justice (Judge, Magistrate Judges, and Magistrate) and indicated in the 
initial evidence that [his] expertise is in the field of law and judicial systems." 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that USCIS incorrectly determined that persons qualified as 
professionals in the field of law are categorically excluded from EB-1 classification eligibility and 
mischaracterized both his area of expertise and the nature of his intended work in the United States, 
which he reiterates should be considered within the field of education. The Petitioner also emphasizes 
that despite the occupation listed on the petition, he has documented his background as a legal scholar, 
professor, and leader in the field of judicial and legal education and indicated his intent to work in 
these areas in the United States, rather than practicing law or seeking to pursue a role as a judge. 
Upon review, we conclude that the Petitioner has established by a preponderance ofthe evidence that, 
consistent with section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) and (ii), his area of extraordinary ability and intended work in 
the United States can be characterized as falling within the field of education, rather than practicing 
law as a profession. As noted, the Petitioner indicates that he intends to participate in legal symposia,

The Petitioner claimed to meet up to seven of the ten evidentiary criteria under 8 e.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), but users did not fully address the evidence presented under those criteria. 
Rather, users observed under the analysis of each criterion that the achievements documented in the 
record are in the field of "law" and cannot meet the evidentiary requirements for establishing 
extraordinary ability. Therefore, while the evidence of record may ultimately be insufficient to 
demonstrate the Petitioner's eligibility for EB-1 classification, we will withdraw the decision and 
remand this matter to users. 
On remand, users should review the entire record, including the Petitioner's submission and 
arguments on appeal, and issue a new decision. If users finds that the Petitioner satisfies the 
minimum number of regulatory criteria, it should consider whether the totality of the evidence 
establishes that he has demonstrated sustained national or international acclaim that places him among 
the small percentage at the very top of his field. See section 203(b )(1) of the Act; 
8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.
