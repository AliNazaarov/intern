Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a snowboard and kitesurfing instructor who seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding that although he met at least three of the regulatory criteria 
the record did not establish that he warranted an approval in the final merits determination. The matter 
is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to 
demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de novo. 
Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we will 
dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)( 4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable material if he or she is able to demonstrate that 
the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has been involved in either the snowboarding or kitesurfing sports for approximately 
25 years with the last 15 years as an instrnctor or a coach. 
A Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met nine of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied six of the criteria relating to prizes or awards, published 
material, judging, display of the Petitioner's work, performing in a leading or critical role, and a high 
salary or remuneration, but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with membership, original 
contributions, or the authorship of scholarly articles. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he meets 
the evidentiary criteria SCOPS denied as well as a favorable determination in a final merits 
determination. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude that he has not 
demonstrated eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant classification. 
We note this case presents an issue the USCIS Policy Manual addresses about continuing to work in 
the area of expertise for athletes in which an alien has achieved acclaim as an athlete but intends to 
work in the United States as an instrnctor or coach and states: 
The officer may encounter instances where it is difficult to determine whether the 
person's intended employment falls sufficiently within the bounds of his or her area of 
extraordinary ability. Some of the most problematic cases are those in which the 
beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim is based on his or her abilities 
as an athlete, but the beneficiary's intent is to come to the United States and be 
employed as an athletic coach or manager. Competitive athletics and coaching rely on 
different sets of skills and in general are not in the same area of expertise. However, 
many extraordinary athletes have gone on to be extraordinary coaches. 
2 
Therefore, in general, if a beneficiary has clearly achieved recent national or 
international acclaim as an athlete and has sustained that acclaim in the field of 
coaching or managing at a national level, officers can consider the totality of the 
evidence as establishing an overall pattern of sustained acclaim and extraordinary 
ability such that USCIS can conclude that coaching is within the beneficiary's area of 
expertise. 
Where the beneficiary has had an extended period of time to establish his or her 
reputation as a coach beyond the years in which he or she had sustained national or 
international acclaim as an athlete, depending on the specific facts, officers may place 
heavier, or exclusive, weight on the evidence of the beneficiary's achievements as a 
coach or a manager. 
See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (emphasis 
added). The Petitioner doesn't present an argument explaining why his achievements as a competitor 
should weigh heavily in his favor within step one under the regulatory criteria. His most recent 
achievements as a competitor appear to be in 2011, so he has not "clearly achieved recent national or 
international acclaim as an athlete" which might enable him to satisfy the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F .R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) with athletic achievements. The evidence also points to approximately 2010 when 
he became an instructor, so he "has had an extended period of time to establish his or her reputation 
as a coach beyond the years in which he or she had sustained national or international acclaim as an 
athlete." 
We will therefore only evaluate his eligibility based on his achievements and abilities as an instructor 
with "exclusive, weight on the evidence of the beneficiary's achievements as a coach or a manager." 
Because SCOPS did not evaluate his claims in this manner, this may result in a different determination 
under both steps in the adjudicative process. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner provided several awards as a competitor and one as an instructor. SCOPS determined 
that the Petitioner met the requirements of this criterion. But as we noted above, because he has had 
adequate time to establish himself as a coach or instructor, his claims will be limited to coaching and 
instructing achievements. 
It appears the Petitioner's only claimed prize or award as an instructor was in 2019 when he was named 
an honorary member of the Association of Professional Ski Instructors of Ukraine (APSTU). The 
Petitioner initially only submitted the award itself and in the response to SCOPS' request for evidence 
(RFE) he claimed this constituted a nationally recognized award for excellence because it was granted 
3 
by the national-level professional association for merits in the development of snowboarding in 
Ukraine. 
But we do not agree with the Petitioner's position on what constitutes national or international 
recognition in this context. National and international recognition results, not from the entity that 
issued the prize or award, but through the awareness of the accolade in the eyes of the field nationally 
or internationally. Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (finding "the focus is 
on the award, not the awarding organization"); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 
(D.D.C. 2013). Agency policy addresses the requisite level of a prize's or award's recognition noting 
we consider the "national or international significance of the awards or prizes in the field" and states 
"national or international recognition is most often associated with awards given to individuals at the 
highest level in a given field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). See Guida v. 
Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) (upholding the 
determination that awards must be nationally or internationally recognized). 
A national or international level competition may issue lesser awards that merely receive local or 
regional recognition, which do not meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. The 
requisite recognition should be evident through specific means; for example but not limited to, national 
or international-level media coverage. Courts have recognized other factors when considering whether 
an award was nationally or internationally recognized such as the number of entrants who competed 
in the same category or their level of experience. Nakos v. USCIS, No. 3:25-CV-0985-D, 2026 WL 
2042845, at *4 (N.D. Tex. July 15, 2026). 
Additionally, unsupported conclusory letters from those in the Petitioner's field are not sufficient 
evidence that a particular prize or award is nationally or internationally recognized. Probative 
evidence that an award is nationally or internationally recognized is generally material from sources 
besides the issuing entity. Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 183. 
Because the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of this 
criterion, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination. 
2. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, the 
Petitioner must establish the circulation statistics are high relative to other similar forms of media. 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
4 
The Petitioner provided multiple articles and SCOPS determined that he met the requirements of this 
criterion. 
While some of the published material is about the Petitioner and his work as an instructor, what he 
relies on to demonstrate that the articles appeared in one of the required publication types does not 
meet the regulatory requirements. He highlighted that one article appeared in USA TODAY, but a 
review of that article reveals the following: "Members of the editorial and news staff of the USA 
TODAY Network were not involved in the creation of this content." This disclaimer signifies that the 
piece is likely paid commercial content, a press release, or sponsored material rather than independent 
journalism. It appears to separate USA TODAY Press Release's professional reporters and editors 
from the creation, wording, and claims of the text. Similar messages are conveyed on other USA 
TODAY websites reflecting the placement ofthe material is part ofa paid service. See Business News, 
USA TODAY (Aug. 17, 2026), https://www.usatoday.com/press-release/. While we consider this 
material and we do not exclude it, it carries significantly diminished evidentiary value. 
In another article that appeared on MSN.com, it too contains hallmarks that it could be paid content 
as it states "The post ... appeared first on Under30CEO," where the author posts blog stories. A 
review of the material on Under30CEO reveals the two biogs are identical and simply appear in 
different publications. This is also known as syndication or content syndication which "is the process 
of pushing your blogpost, article, video or any piece of web-based content out to other third-parties 
who will then republish it on their own sites [which is] useful if you're a smaller publisher or an 
up-and-coming writer who wants a larger audience from a more authoritative site." What is content 
syndication and how do I get started?, Search Engine Watch (Aug. 17, 2026), 
https://searchenginewatch.com/2016/08/03/what-is-content-syndication-and-how-do-i-get-started/. 
USCIS evaluates media coverage based on editorial independence and objective third-party 
recognition and this type of replication across publications warrants diminished evidentiary value. 
This syndication creates the appearance it was distributed through automated networks or paid 
distribution services rather than independent editorial selection, or that it is self-promotional materials 
which USCIS policy does not allow. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l) (noting 
that marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the person's products or promoting the 
person's services are not generally considered to be published material about the person which 
includes seemingly objective content about the person in major print publications that the person or 
the person's employer paid for). Consequently, repeating the same text across aggregator sites or 
partner networks fails to provide the robust, objective proof of national or international recognition 
that his criterion requires. 
Other material under this criterion also suffers from evidentiary shortfalls such as the article from 
KP.RU. First, that article was not about the Petitioner and his work in the field; he was merely 
mentioned within it. Second, it mentioned him in passing as a contestant along with his finishing place 
in the competition and it was not about his work as an instructor. It therefore carries no evidentiary 
weight in these proceedings for the reasons we discussed above under USCIS policy about continuing 
to work in the area of expertise for former athletes.

3. "Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work 
of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
This criterion requires not only that the Petitioner was selected to serve as a judge, but also that he is 
able to produce evidence that he actually participated as a judge. Additionally, these duties must have 
been directly judging the work of others in the same or an allied field in which the Petitioner seeks an 
immigrant classification within the present petition. The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying 
all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided evidence that he judged athletic competitors and SCOPS determined that he 
met the requirements of this criterion. But that is not the area of expertise in which we are considering 
this petitioner's achievements. A petitioner applying for this classification as a coach or instructor-as 
is the Petitioner's situation here-must show that they were acting as an instructor while judging other 
coaches or instructors. See Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 850 (C.D. Cal. 2022) (finding 
that, because the alien sought classification as a water polo coach, evidence that she judged water polo 
players rather than other coaches did not satisfy the judging criterion). Here, the Petitioner provided 
evidence that he was judging competitors and not coaches or instructors, so his claims and evidence 
do not satisfy this criterion's requirements. As a result, we withdraw SCOPS' decision to the contrary. 
4. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
qualifying role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization 
or establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). Evidence of a leading role should show that the alien was a leader for the 
claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help to establish that a role was leading. 
A leading role should be apparent by its position in the overall hierarchy and the role's matching 
duties. Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *7 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 
2025). 
For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l). In determining whether the organization, establishment, department, or division has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. Id. Organizations, establishments, divisions, or departments that enjoy a 
distinguished reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Id. The Petitioner 
must submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024).

5. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner to submit 
evidence of a "high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others 
in the field." That means a petitioner must provide comparative evidence for those performing similar 
work, during the same approximate time and geographic location, within the field. See generally 
6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l ). And the presentation of only average salaries in the field 
is generally inadequate to demonstrate a petitioner's earnings are high, or their other types of 
remuneration are significantly high, in relation to the rest of the field. 
7 
The Petitioner provided a services agreement, a document for services rendered for one month 
demonstrating his pay for that one-month timeframe, a bank statement covering 2021, and 
documentation establishing the average pay for coaches and instructors. While we agree the evidence 
demonstrates that during one month he earned above the average monthly salary, we do not agree that 
meets the regulation's requirements of "a high salary" as the Petitioner claims. We do not consider 
evidence consisting of a one-time payment to equate to a salary, which generally is a predetermined 
regular payment (see 29 C.F.R. § 54 l .602(a)). However, this criterion allows for more than a salary 
and permits a filing party to show "other significantly high remuneration for services." So, if his 
contracts do not specify a salary and he's compensated in some other manner, then he would have to 
show "other significantly high" remuneration (a tougher standard) relative to other instructors. 
First, the bank statements may reflect money entering and exiting his bank account, but they do not 
demonstrate the source of those funds such that he has demonstrated what deposits were for his high 
salary7 claims. That leaves him with a one-month period in January of2021 in which he was instructing 
in theDregion as evidenced by the document titled "Acceptance for Services Rendered." But what 
the Petitioner has not established is how the amount he earned for instructional services in January of 
2021 compares to what others in his field were receiving for similar services and whether that is 
sufficient to demonstrate he has commanded significantly high remuneration in relation to others in 
the field. In other words, the Petitioner presented this as his monthly salary but failed to corroborate 
those assertions with evidence and he has not established what might constitute a level of significantly 
high remuneration when compared to others in the field. 
Consequently, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements 
of this criterion. 
6. "Evidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to demonstrating that the displayed work 
was the alien's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the work was displayed must be 
artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). While 
Merriam-Webster's online dictionary definition of "exhibition" includes public showings other than 
those that are artistic in nature, the plain language of the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and 
explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in nature. Id. 
The Petitioner claimed his athletic performances on YouTube as comparable evidence under this 
criterion, but that fails for several reasons. First, consistent with this decision, the Petitioner may only 
rely on his achievements as an instructor or a coach and not as an athlete. That alone precludes his 
comparable evidence claims. But his evidence is also inadequate as it is simply a printout oftwo pages 
from Y ouTube and he has not identified any evidence to corroborate his claims about "Fashion 
marine" as indicated in his initial correspondence with USCIS or what that constitutes. 
And, the Petitioner has not established that displaying videos or shorts on Y ouTube amounts to a 
display, and if so that it was at exhibitions or showcases. Also of importance, the Petitioner merely 
asserted that "[t]he plain language of this criterion, which references 'artistic exhibitions,' is not a 
8 
direct fit for the petitioner's occupation." But that is an insufficient method to properly claim 
eligibility under the comparable evidence provision at 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)( 4). Instead, the Petitioner's 
explanation has to be present and he cannot simply offer a "general unsupported assertion that the 
listed evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's occupation [as that] is not 
probative" and such claims should be discounted. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l). 
Multiple reasons establish that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence meeting this criterion's 
requirements. 
7. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that he is a member of an association in his field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 
2780815, at *3--4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner claims his honorary membership in APSIU. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did 
not meet the requirements of this criterion. 
For item one listed above on the use ofrecognized national or international experts who serve as judges 
of prospective members' achievements, the Petitioner relied on the membership certificate, a letter 
from the APSIU vice president, and documents from an unassociated entity describing its certification 
process. While it can be argued that the vice president ofAP SIU can be a nationally or internationally 
recognized expert who is one judge of candidates' achievements, he was not the only one making that 
determination. And the extract of the AP SIU Regulation on Membership that the Petitioner provided 
does not specify that the remaining personnel who make the decision of whether prospective members 
have outstanding achievements must have attained any stature, much less that they are nationally or 
internationally recognized experts. It only provides that the decision on honorary members "is granted 
by decision of the Board or the General Meeting." So, his arguments and evidence fail on that point. 
And for item two's outstanding achievement requirement, the extract of the APSIU Regulation on 
Membership states that honorary members are those who have made a significant contribution to the 
development of instructional activities, alpine skiing, or the activities of APSIU. But the document 
does not specify what constitutes a significant contribution and the Petitioner has not provided 
adequate evidence to show this organization meets the regulatory standard. Tapia, 2025 WL 2 780815, 
at *3 (finding that it is within the plain language of the membership criterion for USCIS to require 
objective evidence).

B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner argues and submits evidence for two additional criteria on 
appeal relating to his contributions in the field and the authorship of scholarly articles at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v) and (vi) respectively, it is unnecessary that we make a decision on these additional 
grounds because he cannot numerically meet the required number of criteria. As the Petitioner cannot 
fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the 
remaining issues. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 
24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that 
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); see also Matter ofDelcarmen-Lara, 29 I&N Dec. 830, 83 7 
n.2 (BIA 2026) ( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise 
ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, No.
