Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a lecturer and instructor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b0(1)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as 
an alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internally recognized award, or that 
the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) 
- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
111. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed to meet six criteria: membership in associations (ii); 
published material about the alien (iii); judge of the work of others (iv); original scientific, scholarly, 
artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field (v); authorship of 
scholarly articles in the field (vi); and performed in a leading or critical role (viii). USCIS evaluated 
the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the request for evidence and 
concluded the Petitioner satisfied four criteria, published material, judge the work ofothers, authorship 
of scholarly articles in the field, and performed in a leading or critical role. USCIS also considered 
the totality ofthe evidence in a final merits determination and concluded the Petitioner did not establish 
that he is among the small percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims to meet all six criteria and contends that USCIS "improperly 
discounted evidence," misapplied the Kazarian framework, and imposed a heightened standard. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has established that he meets the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether 
he has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he has sustained national or 
international acclaim and is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and 
that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final 
2 
merits determination, we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if his successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in 
the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2)-(3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
As previously noted, the Petitioner contends on appeal that USCIS failed to conduct a proper final 
merits analysis. Nonetheless, we exercise de novo review of all issues of fact, law, policy, and 
discretion. See Matter o_f Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537. This means that we look at the record 
anew and are not required to defer to findings made in the initial decision. Furthermore, our decision 
may address new issues that were not raised or resolved in the prior decision. 
While we may not discuss every document in the record, we have reviewed and considered each one. 
Based on our de novo review, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this 
highly restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will discuss below. 
The Petitioner maintains that his record demonstrates "sustained scholarly activity, recognized 
authorship, professional leadership, peer review authority, and documented professional recognition." 
However, the record, in the aggregate, does not support the conclusion that he has shown the required 
acclaim and recognition, or otherwise shown that he is one of the small percentage of individuals who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility for the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) based on his 
membership to the American Society oflnternational Law (ASIL), the Ukrainian Bar Association, the 
International Bar Association, and the International Center for the Development of Science and 
Technology. In support of this claim, he submitted membership certificates, welcome emails, 
membership identification numbers, and invitation letters to events hosted by these organizations. 
Regardless of whether the submitted evidence satisfies the regulatory criterion, the record does not 
demonstrate that the Petitioner's memberships resulted in sustained national or international acclaim or 
a career of acclaimed work. See Donskoy v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 21-CV-
04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 11, 2021) (upholding the petitioner's 
membership did not demonstrate the requisite level of achievement or sustained acclaim to establish 
extraordinary ability). For example, the Petitioner has not established how his membership in ASIL 
reflects that he has risen to the very top of his field. The "About ASIL" page submitted in the record 
indicates that membership is available to individuals interested in international law and does not 
demonstrate that admission is limited to those who have attained distinguished achievements in the 
field. Furthermore, the Petitioner's membership in three of the four organizations identified in the 
record began in May and June 2025, shortly before the filing ofthe petition. Such recent memberships 
do not support a finding of sustained acclaim. Additionally, the record does not contain evidence 
demonstrating that the Petitioner received national or international recognition as a result of his 
membership in any of these organizations.

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 1 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Price, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is 
such an elite level of accomplishment); Onaghise v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 
2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding that extraordinary ability visas are known 
as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are "reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). In 
this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the 
field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one 
more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate 
that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Although the Petitioner himself and his support letters emphasized his extraordinary ability in his field, 
the record is insufficient to demonstrate sustained acclaim in order to qualify for an immigrant visa 
under this classification. The totality of the evidence does not demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
garnered a level of recognition for expertise in his field that would be expected from an individual at 
the top ofhis field. Accordingly, the record, as a whole, does not demonstrate eligibility for the benefit 
sought. The commentary for the proposed regulations implementing section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary 
ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive 
documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 FR 30703-01, 30704. Here, the 
Petitioner has not established that he enjoys a career that meets this very high standard. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has submitted the required initial evidence that meets at least three of the ten categories 
of evidence listed under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we have provided the type of final 
merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1119-20. Based on our review of the record 
as a whole, we conclude that the record, in the aggregate, does not support a conclusion that the

Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required to be classified as an alien of 
extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals at the top of their 
respective fields. Here, he has not shown that his accomplishments rise to the required level of 
sustained national or international acclaim, or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also Section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility to be classified as an 
alien of extraordinary ability in his field. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
