Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a martial arts athlete, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, 
internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten regulatory 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has experience competing, coaching, and training others in several martial arts fields, 
and he intends to continue his work in the United States. Because the Petitioner has not indicated or 
established that he received a major, internationally recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of 
the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner 
satisfied the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), which concerns his participation as a judge of the 
work of others in his field, but that he did not meet any of the other claimed criteria. On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), and (v). 1

B. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for"[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we determine whether the 
association requires those at the alien's level ofmembership to have attained outstanding achievements 
in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual at F.2(B)(l). 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that the documentation of record establishes that he meets this 
criterion. He first points to his receipt of several j Ititles of the Kyrgyz Republic, 
stating that these titles "confer membership in the country's elite circle of nationally and 
internationally distinguished athletes and coaches-a distinction that can only be obtained by 
demonstrating performance at the highest national level." The Petitioner, however, does not offer 
evidence to demonstrate that the "elite circle" is a formalized organization, nor has he provided 
documentation relating to anyone who judged his eligibility for the 1 ltitle, including 
any evidence showing that the judge or judges were recognized experts in the fields relating to the 
title. The regulatory document, for example, depicts that certain placements and rankings are required 
to achieve the mastery titles, such as winning a certain number of tournaments against opponents of 
certain ranks. However, it does not indicate that judges of an athlete's qualifications for a mastery 
title have a level of expertise in any athletic field. 
The Petitioner also references his affiliations with several associations on appeal, including NAGA, 
for which he has provided extensive documentation detailing rules and requirements for tournament 
competitors, referees, and spectators. This documentation, however, does not include discussion of 
any requirements for membership. The Petitioner's references on appeal to his memberships with 
United World Wrestling, the World Martial Arts Kazakhstan Association, and the World Federation 
of Mixed Martial Arts, are not supported by any documentation concerning membership requirements. 
He asserts that his "inclusion and awards issued by these organizations demonstrates recognition and 
membership by recognized authorities within the global martial arts and combat sports community." 
However, his implication that awards he has received demonstrates membership, without supporting 
evidence of membership requirements, is insufficient to demonstrate eligibility under this criterion. 
Finally, the Petitioner claims on appeal that his "membership within the Taekwondo Federation and 
achievement of a Black Belt in Taekwondo demonstrates his advanced mastery, discipline, and long­
term commitment to excellence in the martial arts." The record includes a certification from the World 
Korea Professional Taekwondo Federation for his black belt, information about the federation, and 
information about the belt system. This documentation, however, makes no reference to membership 
with the federation or specific membership requirements, but focuses on requirements for achieving 
ascending belt levels. It is not evident whether or how the federation offers membership requiring

C. Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that their contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, have 
remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance. 
See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an original 
contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual at F.2(B)(l). 
On appeal, the Petitioner refers to letters he previously submitted from organizations and individuals in 
the fields of martial arts who have direct knowledge of his work as an athlete, trainer, and coach. He 
states that SCOPS "failed to assess [his] achievements in the proper context of the field, disregarding 
evidence showing that [his] contributions-though specialized-were adopted and implemented by 
major institutions and cited by other professionals." Based on the evidence of record, however, it is 
unclear what original contributions ofmajor significance the Petitioner has made to his field. 
More specifically, the letters from organizations in the field express appreciation for his work without 
further elaboration. Letters from the World Martial Arts Kazakhstan Association and a village council 
in the Kyrgyz Republic express appreciation for his contribution to the development of sports and the 
"patriotic education of youth" in the republic, but they do not describe his specific contributions 
furthering sports development or youth education. A letter from the Federation of Non-Traditional 
types of wrestling and pankration thanks the Petitioner for "holding the championship of Kyrgyz 
Republic in non-traditional types of wrestling and pankration." Letters from the Mixed Martial Arts 
Federation for Brazilian Vale Tudo commend him for his service as chiefreferee and for his promotion 
of the sport and assistance in organizing tournaments. Using brief and generalized terms, these letters 
thank the Petitioner for his involvement in the athletic community, but they do not reference any 
original or specific contributions he has made to the field-of major significance or otherwise. 
Further, although the letters from individuals with knowledge of the Petitioner's work laude his skills 
and experience, they do so in a broad manner that does not illustrate what particular contributions he 
has made in the context ofhis field. For example, a letter from an individual who trained the Petitioner 
describes him as an exceptional and responsible person, as well as one of his best students. Another 
letter from an individual who trained with the Petitioner describes him as a "very talented, multi­
faceted athlete and coach." Letters from the Petitioner's former students express confidence in his 
ability to "continue to prepare excellent athletes" and that "his students will achieve great results." 
They speak highly ofhis character and credit him for his role in helping these athletes advance through 
5 
various stages of their careers as professional fighters. These letters do not identify any original or 
"specialized" contributions that "were adopted and implemented by major institutions and cited by 
other professionals." 
While these letters from organizations and individuals in the field generally demonstrate the 
Petitioner's positive influence within his sports community and positive impacts he has made on some 
of his students, they do not demonstrate that he has made an original contribution ofmajor importance 
to the field. We note that some letters mention that the Petitioner has coached fighters who have 
placed highly in tournaments, and the record includes some information about these individuals' 
competition histories, inferring a claim ofcontributions ofmajor significance to the field through their 
performance. The record does not, however, include any information by which to compare their 
achievements to athletes under the tutelage of another coach to demonstrate that their performance is 
principally attributable to the Petitioner's original contributions as a coach. 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions ofmajor 
significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed 
significance.4 Submitted letters should specifically describe the person's contribution and its significance 
to the field and should also set forth the basis of the writer's knowledge and expertise. Id. In this case, 
the letters lack specific, detailed information explaining how the Petitioner has made original 
contributions ofmajor significance in the field. USCIS need not accept primarily conclusory statements. 
1756, Inc. v. The US. Att'y Gen., 745 F. Supp. 9, 15 (D.C. Dist. 1990). The record does not establish that 
the Petitioner has made original athletic contributions ofmajor significance in the field. Accordingly, the 
Petitioner has not established that he meets the requirements of this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -
- F.Supp.3d---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes 
a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required 
to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
