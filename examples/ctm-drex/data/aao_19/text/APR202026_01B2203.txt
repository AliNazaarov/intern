Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an equestrian trainer, coach and rider, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements for this classification by meeting at least three 
ofthe evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) or through evidence ofa one-time achievement 
(a major, internationally recognized award.) The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 
C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence ofsustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

B. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met (through comparable evidence 
per 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4)) two of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to 
her receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards and her authorship of scholarly 
articles in her field. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she also meets the evidentiary criteria3 
relating to: 
• Her membership m associations in her field requiring outstanding achievements of their 
members; 
• Published material about her and her work in the field in a qualifying publication, and; 
• Her leading or critical role for organizations having a distinguished reputation. 
After reviewing all of the evidence in the record, we disagree with SCOPS' conclusions regarding 
both criteria that they determined the Petitioner met, and withdraw those conclusions. In addition, per 
the foregoing discussion we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that she meets at least 
three of the evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), and therefore does not meet the initial 
evidence requirements for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
1. Evidence of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." The 
Petitioner claimed in her RFE response that the awards she received from her participation in 
competitions as a showjumping athlete should be considered, and that the awards received by horses 
and athletes she trained should be considered as comparable evidence under this criterion. But SCOPS 
did not consider the Petitioner's awards received as an athlete, and per the discussion above we will 
also not consider that evidence under this criterion. 
Turning to the evidence of awards received by horses and athletes trained and coached by the 
Petitioner, she asserted that these should be considered to be comparable evidence under this criterion. 
When making such a claim, a petitioner must first establish that a particular evidentiary criterion is 
not readily applicable to their occupation. If a petitioner so establishes, then they must demonstrate 
that the evidence submitted is truly comparable to the evidence described in that criterion. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
Here, SCOPS accepted that the evidence submitted by the Petitioner showed that this criterion is not 
readily applicable to her occupation of coach and trainer, and concluded that the evidence of awards 
received by athletes and horses coached and trained by the Petitioner were comparable to the evidence 
called for in the criterion's plain language. Leaving aside the issue of whether this criterion does not

4. Additional Evidentiary Criteria 
Although the Petitioner claims eligibility for two additional criteria on appeal, relating to published 
materials about her at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and her leading or critical role for distinguished 
organizations at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(3)(3)(viii), we need not reach these additional grounds. As the 
Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3), the identified basis for denial is dispositive of the appeal. We therefore reserve the 
Petitioner's appellate arguments regarding these additional evidentiary criteria. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that s/he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
