Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an information security analyst in the field of information technology (IT) and 
cybersecurity, 1 seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 2 Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b0(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as 
required. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if they have extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act.

The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the filed of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). In the initial petition, the Petitioner claimed to satisfy five criteria. In response 
to SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE), the Petitioner wholly abandoned one criteria and provided 
further detail in connection with the remaining four criteria. SCOPS evaluated the claims the 
Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the RFE and concluded the Petitioner did not 
satisfy any of the criteria she claimed over the course of the proceedings. On appeal, the Petitioner 
claims the same four criteria she discussed in her response to SCOPS' RFE. After reviewing all the 
evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this highly 
restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will discuss below. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification is 
sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the association for which she claims 
present or past membership requires that members have outstanding achievements in the field, as 
judged by recognized experts in that field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Examples of associations in the field requiring outstanding 
achievements of their members may include a membership in certain professional associations and a 
fellow with certain organizations or institutions. Id. 
Associations may have multiple levels of membership, and the level of membership afforded to the 
person must show that in order to obtain that level ofmembership, recognized national or international 
experts judged the person as having attained outstanding achievements in the field for which

membership in the association was not based on outstanding achievements n the field include instances 
where the person's membership was based solely on (1) a level of education or years of experience in 
a particular field, (2) the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an association's publications, and (3) a 
requirement for employment in certain occupations, such as union membership or guild affiliation for 
actors. Id.

3. We Reserve the Remaining Claimed Criteria 
We note the Petitioner claims she met an additional two criteria related to performing in a leading or 
critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation and receipt of 
lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
However, as noted above, the Petitioner's unsuccessful demonstration of her eligibility under the 
criteria we discuss means she cannot numerically meet the required number of at least three criteria 
out ofthe 10 available and the four she claimed. Accordingly, discussion ofthe Petitioner's remaining 
claims would serve no legal purpose and we reserve them. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 
(1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on 
issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification that is intended for individuals already at 
the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing towards the top. See Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability; 
Visinscaia, 4 F.Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary 
ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"). The record does not demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has achieved a degree or recognition indicative of the required sustained national or 
international acclaim or that the Petitioner's work is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Re. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
garnered sustained national or international acclaim in the field and that the Petitioner is one of the 
small percentage to have risen to the very top of the field. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for immigrant 
classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above­
mentioned reasons. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
