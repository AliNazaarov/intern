Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an automotive engineer, seeks first preference immigrant classification as an individual 
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, 
internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten regulatory 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we conclude that USCIS did not properly consider the evidence. We will therefore withdraw the 
decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. The alien must seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area 
of extraordinary ability and the evidence must show that the alien's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of a beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Alternatively, the petitioner can submit 
evidence that the alien beneficiary meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles. Ifthose standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once the initial evidence requirements have been met, the next step is a final merits determination, in 
which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of their field of endeavor. 
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022); 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is an engineer and entrepreneur who intends to implement his innovation projects in 
the United States. As the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, he must demonstrate that he meets the initial evidence requirements 
by satisfying at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed 
to have satisfied the following criteria: lesser awards, memberships, original contributions, authorship 
of scholarly articles, artistic exhibitions, leading or critical role, and high salary. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(ii), (v)-(ix). 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner met the awards criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) but did not 
meet the remaining criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that USCIS did not adequately evaluate 
the evidence in the record. He claims that USCIS evaluated evidence under a criterion he did not 
claim, did not address a criterion he claimed, and did not address comparable evidence submitted for 
some of the criteria. 1 The Petitioner also claims that USCIS assessed some of the criteria under 
evidence submitted in a previously denied petition and applied consideration of sustained national or 
international acclaim when evaluating the initial criteria. 
Upon review, we agree that USCIS did not adequately address the Petitioner's specific claims and 
evidence. While USCIS is not required to provide a detailed discussion of every piece of evidence 
the Petitioner submitted, the decision here does not convey that it adequately considered all relevant 
evidence in the record. 
For example, USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy the published material criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). However, the Petitioner did not claim to meet that criterion. In addition, 
the decision refers to evidence not contained in the record, such as contracts with companies he hired 
to provide services related to his business venture. The decision also refers to all of his patents as 
"provisional" when the record includes several registered patents. Further, as the Petitioner states on 
appeal, USCIS did not address certain evidence, such as evidence of several months of his income and 
comparative salary data submitted for the high-salary criterion. Moreover, USCIS did not provide a

Because USeIS's decision does not adequately analyze the facts ofthe matter and apply the regulatory 
standards, we will remand the matter for entry of a new decision. While the evidence of record may 
ultimately be insufficient to demonstrate the Petitioner's eligibility for the requested classification, 
users' decision did not adequately address the submitted evidence in denying the petition. See 8 
C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l)(i); see also Matter ofM-P-, 20 I&N Dec. 786 (BIA 1994) (finding that a decision 
must fully explain the reasons for denying a motion to allow the respondent a meaningful opportunity 
to challenge the determination on appeal). 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
