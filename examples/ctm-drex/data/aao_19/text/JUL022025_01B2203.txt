The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter

I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
Consequently, we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. However, because SCOPS denied the petition based upon a final merits 
determination, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the 
evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim, that she is among that small percentage at 
the very top ofher field, and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze petitioners' accomplishments and weigh 
the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
The record shows the Petitioner has served as a peer-reviewer of numerous articles for several 
prestigious scientific journals. SCOPS concluded her peer-reviews did not demonstrate extraordinary 
ability, in part, because such service was "routine or expected." On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that 
peer review "is a great responsibility that is only given to the most capable, experienced, and 
accomplished scholars" and is not a required service. To the extent SCOPS diminished the weight of 
the relevant evidence by indicating peer reviews were obligatory, we withdraw that conclusion. 
Nonetheless, the evidence is insufficient to show that the Petitioner's peer review of journal articles 
reflects assessment ofthe work ofother scientists commensurate with those at the very top ofher field. 
For example, the record does not indicate that the Petitioner has served on the editorial board ofhighly 
ranked scientific journals or assessed the work of other scientists in a manner otherwise indicative of 
being at the very top of her field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 
(E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding USCIS' conclusion in a final merits determination that a 
researcher did not provide evidence that "sets him apart from others in his field, such as evidence that 
he ... served in editorial positions for distinguished journals or publications, or chaired technical 
committees for reputable conferences"). 
The record also establishes that the Petitioner has published her research in co-authored journal articles 
and two book chapters, and that the research of one of her articles was supported by a grant from the 
National Institutes of Health (NIH). Although some of the Petitioner's articles have been published 
in highly ranked journals, the record does not demonstrate that most ofher articles have been published 
in highly ranked journals or are otherwise comparable to the publication record of scientists at the very 
top ofher field. Additionally, the Petitioner is the first author ofjust two articles and one book chapter. 
While the Petitioner received fonding from the NIH for one research project, the record does not 
demonstrate that most ofher work has been fonded by prestigious organizations in a manner consistent 
with being at the very top of her field. The record is insufficient to establish that the Petitioner's 
publication and fonding records are reflective of being at the very top of her field. 
While we do not discount the numerous contributions her research has made to her field, the record 
does not establish that the Petitioner's research has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim commensurate with being at the very top of the biomedical sciences field. Of the articles she 
submitted that cite her work, the majority cite her work after one sentence in the introduction or a 
summary of related research. Only two articles cite her work more than one time, the most being just 
5 
three times. While the recommendation letters describe how other scientists have cited the Petitioner's 
work, they do not establish that the impact of her work has extended beyond those individual research 
teams or that her research has been applied in cancer treatments. The record shows the Petitioner is 
an accomplished scientist whose research has been widely cited, but the evidence does not establish 
that her research has impacted her field to the same extent as scientists at the very top of her field. 
The Petitioner's publications, peer reviews, fonding, citations, and recommendation letters show she 
is a well-respected scientist whose research has made significant contributions to her field. The 
evidence is insufficient, however, to show that the Petitioner is among that small percentage at the 
very top of her field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
oftheir respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing towards the top. USCIS has long held 
that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary 
ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r. 1994). Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown that the significance and recognition of her work are indicative of sustained national or 
international acclaim as one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of her field, as 
extraordinary ability is described at section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
Petitioner is consequently ineligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
