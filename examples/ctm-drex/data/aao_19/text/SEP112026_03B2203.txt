Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a research scientist who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did she demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant 
classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 20 I 0) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
After receiving her Ph.D. in Engineering Sciences (Bioengineering), then serving as a postdoctoral 
research fellow, the Petitioner was scheduled to work as an adjunct assistant professor at a U.S. 
university with a start date in the fall of 2027. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met three of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and the authorship 
of scholarly articles but that she had not satisfied the criterion associated with original contributions. 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that she meets the contributions criterion. After reviewing all the 
evidence in the record, we conclude the petition should remain denied. 
1. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect 
and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or 
to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner claimed her individual authorship, being named as the inventor on a commercially 
licensed patent application and the creator and lead developer of open-source software tool 
repositories, and the fact that her datasets were widely adopted in her field of computational biology 
2 
and bioengineering were qualifying under this criterion. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner's 
research, publications, and citations represented standard scientific work but did not prove her efforts 
had been widely relied upon or had made a major, influential impact on the field as a whole. While 
they found the evidence showed successful project, organizational, and patent contributions, those 
achievements only demonstrated smaller scale success for specific clients or employers. Also, routine 
professional activities like conference presentations and peer review services were not found to 
inherently rise to the level of major contributions. And finally, they concluded the support letters 
lacked specific examples of broader impact and could not substitute for independent, preexisting, and 
objective documentary evidence of major significance. 
Within the appeal, the Petitioner advances several errors and claims. She alleges that SCOPS failed 
to consider some of her evidence because the denial does not reference several exhibits-by exhibit 
number-submitted in response to the request for evidence (RFE), that similarities between the RFE 
and the denial demonstrate SCOPS' failure to use reasoned decision making, the denial diminished 
her contributions because she was part of a collaborative group, and it included a final merits standard 
even though SCOPS declined to perform that type of analysis. She also views SCOPS to have erred 
when they did not allow post-filing achievements to factor into their decision, and she explains how 
the existing record satisfies this criterion's standards. 
We begin addressing the Petitioner's statement that the denial did not discuss some of the evidence 
she provided, to include her comment that SCOPS did not list the exhibit number in the denial decision. 
The Petitioner does not offer an explanation ofwhy the decision should have included exhibit numbers 
associated with the material she provided in the proceedings before SCOPS, so we will set that aside. 
But to address her broader point that SCOPS didn't specifically address all the documentation she 
provided, she does not identify a basis that would require them to. She provided over 700 pages of 
evidence consisting of 155 exhibits and SCOPS is not expected to address each and every one. 
When USCIS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition, and has made adequate findings, it will 
not be required to specifically address each claim the Petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it to address 
every piece of evidence the Petitioner presents. Amin, 24 F.4th at 394; Lafortune v. Garland, 110 F.4th 
426,441 (1st Cir. 2024); see also Kazemzadeh v. US. Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 (11th Cir. 2009); 
Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1993). 
We will consider the Petitioner's appellate claims and decide whether any of the materials that she 
identifies as being ignored might have prejudiced her. A filing party bears the burden of not only 
establishing that an error might have occurred, but also "how [the] specific pieces of evidence would 
materially affect the outcome of [the] case." Matter ofS-K-E-, 30 I&N Dec. 27, 30 (BIA 2026). It is 
through those means that the filing party might show they were prejudiced by any alleged error. Id. 
(citing Matter ofR-C-R-, 28 I&N Dec. 74, 77 (BIA 2020), Matter ofD-, 20 I&N Dec. 827,831 (BIA 
1994)). The party that "seeks to have a judgment set aside because of an erroneous ruling carries the 
burden of showing that prejudice resulted." Shinseki v. Sanders, 556 U.S. 396, 409 (2009) (quoting 
Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, 116 (1943)); Molina-Martinez v. United States, 578 U.S. 189, 203 
(2016). We also view similarities between the language within the RFE and the denial in the same 
manner, considering whether she was prejudiced by any errors she demonstrates in the denial.

The Petitioner also claims SCOPS applied a final merits standard despite declining to conduct a final 
merits determination. She makes this conclusion because the decision mentioned "eminence" and "a 
career of acclaimed work." SCOPS included that language after evaluating her claimed criteria in a 
closing section of the decision. We find no instances in which they applied such a standard to the 
Petitioner's individual criterion claims, so we do not view this as an error. 
And she is mistaken when she alleges SCOPS should have considered any of her accolades that 
occurred after the petition filing date. Within the RFE response, the Petitioner provided additional 
evidence of her achievements that occurred after she filed the petition, which users generally does 
not permit. Within the appeal, the Petitioner states her updated citation count as represented on Google 
Scholar should factor into her eligibility because each "of the ten publications generating those 
citations was published and in the record before the ... priority date." 
A request for an immigration benefit "must establish that he or she is eligible for the requested benefit 
at the time of filing the benefit request and must continue to be through adjudication." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.2(b)(l); Delaware Valley Reg'l Ctr., LLC v. DHS, 106 F.4th 1195, 1204-06 (D.C. Cir. 2024); 
Ahmed v. Mukasey, 519 F.3d 579, 582 (6th Cir. 2008). Any additional evidence submitted in 
connection with a benefit request at a later date, including evidence responding to a request from 
users, must also establish "eligibility at the time the benefit request was filed." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.2(b )(12); Robinson v. Napolitano, 554 F.3d 358, 364 (3d Cir. 2009). "Under this rnle, USCrS 
will deny [ an immigration benefit] if the [ filing party] becomes eligible only after the [benefit] was 
filed." Tingzi Wang v. USCIS, 375 F. Supp. 3d 22, 27 (D.D.C. 2019) (citing 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l2)); 
Doe v. USCIS, 410 F. Supp. 3d 86, 100 (D.D.C. 2019). 
users may not approve a visa petition if a petitioner was not qualified at the priority date but expects 
to become eligible at a subsequent time. See Matter of Izummi, 22 T&N Dec. 169, 175-76 (Assoc. 
Comm'r 1998); Matter ofKatigbak, 14 r&N Dec. 45, 49 (Reg'l Comm'r 1971). Ultimately, in order 
to be meritorious in fact, a petition must meet the statutory and regulatory requirements for approval 
as of the date it was filed. Ogundipe v. Mukasey, 541 F.3d 257,261 (4th Cir. 2008). In summary, we 
are not persuaded by the Petitioner's appellate arguments on this issue.

Now to her specific appellate claims of eligibility under this criterion. The Petitioner notes the USCIS 
Policy Manual "identifies several categories of evidence probative of major significance, including 
'published research that has provoked widespread commentary,' 'evidence that the person's work has 
been widely cited,' 'patents or licenses for the person's innovations,' and 'evidence that others have 
built upon or used the person's work."' See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. She alleges the evidence in the record satisfies each of those 
categories. 
To begin, the Petitioner discusses how her work has been independently implemented and relied upon 
in other research. She notes that 13 independent peer reviewed studies by unaffiliated research groups 
across three continents have directly cited to her work. And within those studies, eight papers took an 
element from her own work and used it to run their experiments and seven used data from her work to 
run theirs. That others have relied on and her work in this manner demonstrates it holds value, but not 
necessarily that it has been of major significance in the field. What's absent from her claims is how 
this level of influence has been significantly impactful within the broader field. Even when others 
have adopted a claimed contribution to the field-but those efforts have not borne a result that has 
been significantly influential-that cannot be the type and level of contribution that might satisfy this 
criterion's demanding requirements. Amin, 24 F.4th at 393-94. The degree ofinfluence the Petitioner 
demonstrates here fails to satisfy this rigorous criterion, which represents the most demanding 
requirement of this highly restrictive immigrant classification. 
While we agree that it is notable when one's work has influence on other researchers' work, a 
petitioner should be able to demonstrate how that lesser influence extends to impact the field as a 
whole, as simply demonstrating an original contribution in the field falls far short of this criterion's 
requirements. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 ( citing Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35 (upholding a 
finding that a ballroom dancer had not met this criterion because she did not corroborate her impact in 
the field as a whole). But beyond the factual matter that others have relied on her work, the Petitioner 
has not demonstrated how that extends to her broader professional community and in significant ways. 
Next, the Petitioner discusses commercial licensing and adoption of the work in the industry. She 
states that her "patented technology was licensed to a for-profit entity on November 22, 2024-almost 
a full year before filing-for the manufacture and sale ofresearch products." First we clarify that the 
Petitioner did not offer evidence that the referenced "patented technology" was granted by the U.S. 
Patent and Trademark Office; only that her name was included as an inventor on a patent application. 
Agency policy provides additional information applicable to this case stating: "If a patent remains 
pending, USCIS generally requires additional supporting evidence to document the originality of the 
person's contribution, such as detailed reference letters." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l). The Petitioner has not identified any such evidence regarding the pending patent 
application naming her as one inventor. But setting that factor aside, she provided evidence from a 
research center that the intellectual property covering or associated with that patent application was 
licensed to a for-profit entity. The research center withheld the name of the for-profit entity and it is 
unclear what impact the licensed intellectual property has had in the Petitioner's field of endeavor. 
Additionally, the license to the for-profit entity was nonexclusive. An exclusive license to one 
company may be more persuasive to show that it sought to monopolize the technology, whereas a 
nonexclusive license that is only purchased by a single entity might be more suggestive that interest 
5 
in the intellectual property is relatively low across the broader industry. In other words, the licensing 
of the intellectual property is a positive aspect in a general sense, but falls far short of meeting this 
criterion's mandates. See Sun v. Jaddou, No. 3:24-CV-01466-E, 2025 WL 1556944, at *5 (N.D. Tex. 
June 2, 2025) (upholding the conclusion that the petitioner did not show how her patents had a major 
impact on the field as a whole); Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou, No. 1:23CV256, 2024 WL 4008312, at *10 
(M.D.N.C. Aug. 30, 2024), report and recommendation adopted sub nom. Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou 
Dir. of United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. l :23-CV-256, 2024 WL 4267307 (M.D.N.C. 
Sept. 23, 2024) ( finding that even if a patent indicates an innovation is both novel and useful, "useful" 
and "of major significance" are not the same). 
Within the appeal brief: the Petitioner also asserts that the content of her patent application has been 
cited as prior art in multiple patent applications. This also speaks to the originality of the work, but 
she still does not address how it demonstrates a level of impact in the field as a whole. The level of 
the impact in the field as a whole through the wide use ofthe innovation is a more appropriate measure 
to determine if an individual has sufficiently influenced their field. See Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, 
No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019). See also Matter of 
NYSDOT, 22 I&N Dec. 215,221 n.7, (Comm'r 1998) (although vacated as it relates to adopting a new 
framework for adjudicating national interest waiver petitions, the comparative analysis relating to 
patents remains valid and pertinent to this case). 
We end our discussion of patents with the USCIS Policy Manual's content providing "[ e ]vidence that 
the person's work was ... patented, ... while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will 
not necessarily establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance in the field" and instead 
"evidence that the person developed a patented technology that has attracted significant attention or 
commercialization may establish the significance of the person's original contribution to the field." 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). As we noted above, even though the 
Petitioner has shown the intellectual property in the patent application has attracted some attention, 
she did not demonstrate it was significant attention or commercialization. 
Turning to her citation record, the Petitioner in her appeal refers to the evidence from Dimensions.ai 
that she provided in the RFE response. That material contains citations to her work that occurred after 
she filed the petition. As such, we incorporate our above discussion explaining why post-filing 
achievements will not factor into this petition. Any of that material must be submitted with a new 
petition filing. 
We are also not persuaded by the Petitioner's two final arguments. First, she notes her work was 
selected to feature on the cover of multiple prominent publications as evidence of impact. However, 
she has not established that journal cover features automatically constitute a contribution of major 
significance. While cover selections reflect the editors' or publishers' high opinion of the research's 
potential, this recognition alone does not demonstrate actual, field-wide influence. Specifically, the 
Petitioner failed to demonstrate that her published research "has provoked widespread commentary on 
its importance from others working in the field, [ and she did not provide] documentation that it has 
been highly cited relative to others' work in that field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l) (noting that such objective markers are a better representation to demonstrate the 
significance of an individual's contributions to their field of endeavor).

We are also not persuaded that being mentioned in two science news articles about scholarly research 
she was associated with shows that her work has been ofmajor significance in the broader field. 1 First, 
this is evidence that cannot meet the published material criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), as it is 
not about her and her work in the field. Published news articles are not sufficient evidence under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they are indicative of contributions of major 
significance and the mere appearance of work the Petitioner was associated with does not meet that 
standard. 
These articles do not describe how any of the Petitioner's original contributions have resulted in a 
significant impact in her field as a whole as they convey the findings and list potential future effects 
in the field. While the evidence shows promise in the Petitioner's work, it does not establish how her 
work already qualifies as a contribution of major significance in the field, rather than prospective, 
potential impacts. The significant nature of her work has yet to be determined or measured. This 
criterion requires petitioners to show they have already made a significant impact in their field. A 
petitioner must establish the elements for the approval of the petition at the time of filing. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .2(b)(1 ), (12). A petition may not be approved if the Petitioner was not qualified at the priority 
date, but expects to become eligible at a subsequent time. Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N Dec. at 49. 
This evidence does not establish that, as of the priority date, the Petitioner contributed to her field in 
a significant manner as the regulation requires. 
For the material SCOPS discussed in its denial decision, the Petitioner has not identified an error in 
which she was prejudiced. Nor has she demonstrated that the material she includes in the appeal that 
SCOPS did not specifically discuss would have changed the outcome of the decision under this 
criterion. As a result, she has not demonstrated she has made the type of contributions to the field that 
are required under this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring a petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. "In creating the 'extraordinary ability' visa, Congress hoped to 'draw the world's best and brightest 
to American shores' by giving 'preferential treatment to highly talented immigrants."' Branski v. USCIS, 
No. 25-CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at* 11 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) ( quoting Amin, 24 F.4th at 386). 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and it is unnecessary 
that we provide further analysis of the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; 
see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL

intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in 
this matter. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) ( citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-

unnecessary to the ultimate decision)). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
