Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a research scientist specializing in remote sensing, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that, although the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidentiary 
requirements, he did not establish, as required, that he has sustained national or international acclaim 
and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied five of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media; 
• (iv), Participation as a judge of the work of others; 
• (v), Original contributions of major significance; 
• (vi), Authorship of scholarly articles; and 
• (viii), Leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments. 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met three of the criteria, pertaining to published material, 
judging, and scholarly articles, and proceeded to a final merits determination. On appeal, the Petitioner 
asserts that he meets all five claimed criteria. 
We need not address these initial criteria at length, because the outcome of the appeal hinges on the 
final merits determination. But we will briefly state that the Petitioner's evidence of a leading or 
critical role is stronger than the evidence relating to published material. 
To satisfy the requirements of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), published material about the alien, the alien 
and the alien's work need not be the only subject of the material, but that material must include a 
substantial discussion of the alien's work in the field. See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. Final Merits Determination 
Because SCOPS determined that the Petitioner submitted the required initial evidence, SCOPS 
considered the question of whether he has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his 
sustained national or international acclaim and that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh 
the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 2 In this matter, we agree with SCOPS that 
the Petitioner has not established eligibility. 
In the final merits determination, SCOPS concluded that, while the Petitioner's evidence meets the basic 
requirements of at least three initial evidentiary criteria, that evidence does not show a level ofrecognition 
consistent with sustained national or international acclaim. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS erred by discussing individual criteria in isolation rather 
than considering the record as a whole. But in addition to discussing the granted criteria, SCOPS stated: 
"In considering the totality ofthe evidence, the petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements are 
of the level of eminence reflective of a 'career of acclaimed work in the field' as contemplated by 
Congress." SCOPS concluded that, although the Petitioner "has been recognized for his work to a certain 
degree," "the record does not differentiate the petitioner amongst other similarly employed Research 
Scientists to show that he has garnered a career of acclaimed work of such preeminence and distinction 
as to elevate him to that small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor with sustained national 
or international acclaim." 
The Petitioner contends that SCOPS, having concluded that the Petitioner's evidence satisfies three 
initial criteria, impermissibly sought to "nullify" that same evidence "by imposing a higher arbitrary 
standard" during the final merits determination. But there is no fundamental conflict here. As the

In this instance, the Petitioner's peer review work and other activities with dissertation committees 
and conference panels qualifies as judging the work of others, satisfying the requirements of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv). But we agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner has not established that the 
circumstances under which he performed that work amount to sustained national or international 
acclaim. The Petitioner did not establish the requirements to perform peer review and other activities 
for the organizations named in the record. The reputation or prestige of a given organization does not 
establish those requirements. The Petitioner therefore has not supported his claim, on appeal, to have 
"judged at the highest levels of the field." 
Particularly with regard to peer review work, the record indicates that thousands of peer reviewed 
articles appear every year, each one typically reviewed by more than one reviewer. The volume of 
publications also indicates that publication of scholarly articles is routine, even expected, in the field. 
A researcher can certainly achieve acclaim through his or her published work, if that work is 
sufficiently consequential and influential.

We acknowledge evidence that some ofthe Petitioner's published work has been highly cited, and this 
is a positive factor in the Petitioner's favor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2). 
But a positive factor is not by itself a determinative or dispositive factor. We must consider the record 
as a whole, rather than focus on the most positive ( or most negative) elements of that record. 
The USCIS Policy Manual gives weight to "evidence demonstrating that the total rate of citations to 
the person's body of published work is high relative to others in the field, such as the person has a 
high h-index for the field." Id. Raw citation numbers do not establish that the citations are "high 
relative to others in the field." The Petitioner did not provide sufficient evidence to establish how the 
citation of his work stands in relation to others' work in his field. 
The Petitioner noted that researchers in several different countries have cited his work and requested 
source codes from him. The geographic scope of these requests does not necessarily place the Petitioner 
at the top of his field. The record shows that any given article may cite dozens or even hundreds of 
sources from different countries. In the absence ofother objective evidence, these citations alone would 
not establish international acclaim.

The regulatory standard for extraordinary ability at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) for this category is "the 
very top of the field of endeavor." Nothing inherently prevents a researcher from reaching the top of 
a given field at an early stage of his or her career, but a petitioner cannot show this level of distinction 
by limiting discussion to exclude the most experienced members of that field. 
In response to a request for evidence, the Petitioner submitted copies of seven email messages from 
researchers in various countries, requesting the Google Earth source codes used in a mapping project. 
These messages show that the Petitioner's work is useful and ofinterest, but the Petitioner did not provide 
context to show that seven such requests is an unusually high number, indicating sustained acclaim. 
Likewise, a printout from a government website shows that a dataset created in conjunction with a 2019 
study had been downloaded 391 times since May 2020. The Petitioner did not establish that this is a 
significantly high number of downloads, and did not corroborate his claim that the government database 
"only curates datasets of high value and impact after extensive review." 
The Petitioner has shown that his position atl lis funded by a grant from NASA. The Petitioner 
has not established that this grant funding demonstrates, or requires, national or international acclaim. 
Without further corroborative and explanatory evidence, the prestige of the funding entity does not 
establish or imply such acclaim. The record tends, instead, to indicate that such funding is a relatively 
common means of support for non-tenured research positions. The Petitioner's job offer letters from 
I !indicate that his employment is non-tenured and "contingent upon funding." The Petitioner 
has not shown that this arrangement demonstrates or is consistent with national or international 
acclaim at the top of his field.

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to

Act. Here, SCOPS has concluded that the Petitioner has satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" 
requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), but this "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary 
requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa." Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp.

In this instance, the record as a whole indicates that the Petitioner is a talented and productive 
researcher who has undertaken some influential work. But the evidence provided does not reach the 
much higher threshold of sustained national or international acclaim. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
