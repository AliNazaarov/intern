Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a business and financial management solutions company, seeks to classify the 
Beneficiary as an individual of extraordinary ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification 
makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record satisfies at least three of the 10 initial 
evidentiary criteria but it does not show sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate 
that the Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
According to the Petitioner, it intends to continue employing the Beneficiary as an expert "in the field 
of Artificial Intelligence (AI) Machine Leaming, specifically in Computer Vision, Natural Language 
Processing, and Generative AI." 
As noted above, SCOPS concluded the record satisfies at least three of the 10 initial evidentiary 
criteria, specifically 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(v)-(vi), (viii)-(ix). However, SCOPS determined that the 
record does not show sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that the Beneficiary 
is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2)-(3)). On appeal, the 
Petitioner reasserts that the record satisfies at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria, that it shows 
the Beneficiary has sustained acclaim, and that it demonstrates he is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. For the reasons discussed below, upon de novo review, we 
conclude the record does not satisfy at least three of the 10 initial evidentiary criteria. 
A. Withdrawal of Initial Evidentiary Criteria 
Before we address SCOPS' basis for denying the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers, 
we first withdraw two of SCOPS' conclusions regarding initial evidentiary criteria. 
1. Leading or Critical Role 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." For 
a leading role, we consider whether evidence establishes the individual is or was a leader within the 
organization or establishment, or a division or department thereof See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. For a critical role, we consider whether 
evidence establishes the individual has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the 
outcome of the organization or establishment's activities, or those of a division or department of the 
organization or establishment. See generally id. 
2 
The extent of SCOPS's analysis of this criterion is the conclusory statement, "The record includes 
evidence of the [B]eneficiary having performed in a leading or critical role with [the Petitioner]. As 
such, the submitted evidence meets this criterion." SCOPS did not clarify whether the Beneficiary 
performed in a leading role or a critical role, nor did it specify what evidence may establish that. 
The record contains limited information regarding the Petitioner's organizational and leadership 
structure among "over 17,000 employees in offices in the United States, India, Canada, the United 
Kingdom, Israel, Australia, and other locations." The Petitioner does not purport that the Beneficiary 
led the organization or establishment in its entirety and, in tum, it does not clarify what its divisions 
or departments are and which one(s) it believes the Beneficiary led-or even the particular division(s) 
or department( s) in which the Beneficiary worked-as opposed to any other leaders of such division( s) 
or department(s). 1 Rather, the record contains statements from the Petitioner and the Beneficiary's 
coworkers, characterizing the Beneficiary as performing a leading role for initiatives such as the 
Petitioner's generative artificial intelligence projects and its "AI-based document extraction and 
classification system." The Petitioner bears the burden of clearly articulating whether-and 
demonstrating how-the Beneficiary may have led it or a division or department of it, as opposed to 
leading certain projects within some unspecified division or department; however, it does not meet 
that burden here. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76; see also 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii); and see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
Next, we acknowledge that the Petitioner asserted the Beneficiary "has played a critical role working 
on various backend ... applications" for its software products, and it describes the importance of 
information security for maintaining its reputation and customer usage. However, although the 
Petitioner relates the Beneficiary's software development work to the Petitioner's priority of 
information security, the record does not establish how the Beneficiary's contributions affected the 
outcome of the Petitioner's activities, or those of a division or department thereof 
As noted above, the record does not clearly establish what the Petitioner's divisions or departments 
are, and the division(s) or department(s) in which the Beneficiary worked. The record also does not 
clearly establish the activities of those unspecified divisions or departments, the outcomes of those 
activities, the causal nexus between the Beneficiary's contributions and the outcomes of those 
divisions' or departments' activities, and the significance of his contributions to those outcomes. 
Rather, the Petitioner generally claims that customers' information would be less secure, its reputation 
would be worsened, and it would lose customers without the Beneficiary's contributions to certain 
software development projects. However, the Petitioner also informs that it was founded in 1983 and 
it provided products and services for many decades before it hired the Beneficiary, and before he began 
making contributions. 
The record does not reconcile how the Beneficiary's contributions affected the Petitioner's activities' 
outcomes, or those of divisions or departments thereof, compared to the outcomes of its activities 
throughout the many decades preceding his contributions, based on the contributions of the 
Petitioner's 17,000 other employees throughout the world. Again, the Petitioner does not meet its 
burden ofclearly articulating whether-and demonstrating how-the Beneficiary contributed in a way 
1 For example, if the Beneficiary merely led teams or projects assigned to him by a division or department manager, the 
Beneficiary's leadership role would be minimized. 
3 
that is of significant importance to the outcome of the Petitioner's activities, or those of a division or 
department thereof. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76; see also 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii); and see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner does not assert that the Beneficiary performed in a leading or critical role for any other 
organization or establishment. For the foregoing reasons, the record does not establish how the 
Beneficiary performed in a leading or critical role for the Petitioner, or a division or department

§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
2. High Salary or Othe Significantly High Remuneration 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 
The extent of SCOPS' analysis of this criterion is the conclusory statement, 'The record included 
evidence of the [B]eneficiary earning a high salary relative to others in the field. As such, the 
submitted evidence meets this criterion." SCOPS did not elaborate on what evidence in the record 
may support such a conclusion. 
The Petitioner submitted IRS Forms W-2 for the Beneficiary for the years of2022-24, to establish the 
annual wages it paid him; wage information provided by the U.S. Department of Labor, Office of 
Foreign Labor Certification (OFLC) and Careeronestop.com; documents that purport to be U.S. 
Department of Labor, Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) wage estimates; and two job postings. The 
Petitioner does not assert that the Beneficiary's salary or other remuneration outside the period of 
2022-24 was high in relation to others in the field. We further note that the Beneficiary's income 
varied each year; therefore, the record does not conclusively establish what his wage would have been 
in any given year outside of the years for which the Petitioner submitted IRS Forms W-2. Moreover, 
the Beneficiary's total remuneration is substantially different from his salary alone; therefore, a 
meaningful analysis of this criterion requires probative information about how his total remuneration 
relates to the total remuneration of others in the field, not merely relative to others' base salaries. 
Although the record establishes the salary or other remuneration the Petitioner paid the Beneficiary, it 
does not establish that the Beneficiary's salary or other remuneration is high, in relation to others in 
the field. The wage information the Petitioner submitted pertains to "software developers," but the 
record does not establish that that is the most appropriate occupational classification as a frame of 
reference. The Petitioner stated on the Form I-140 that the Beneficiary's job title is "senior staff 
software engineer" and that his position corresponds to the U.S. Department of Labor's standard 
occupational classification for software developers. A copy of the Beneficiary's resume in the record 
describes his duties as including the following: "leading GenAI initiatives," "led a team to creation 
of [the Petitioner's] first AI based document extraction and classification criteria," and "lead a team 
for content creation and automation." Given the Beneficiary's "senior" position, entailing what he 
describes as a persistent leadership role for multiple teams or projects, his duties appear to exceed 
4 
those of typical software developers, potentially entailing managerial duties. 2 The record does not 
resolve whether a general software developer is the most appropriate occupational classification for 
the Beneficiary's position. 
Even if "software developer" is the most appropriate occupational classification for the Beneficiary's 
position, the Petitioner nevertheless narrowed the scope of "others in the field," as contemplated by 
this criterion, by characterizing the Beneficiary as an expert "in the field ofArtificial Intelligence (AI) 
Machine Leaming, specifically in Computer Vision, Natural Language Processing, and Generative 
AI." Meaningfully comparing the Beneficiary's total remuneration to that of "others in the field" 
focuses on the total remuneration of other experts "in the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) Machine 
Leaming, specifically in Computer Vision, Natural Language Processing, and Generative AI," and not 
merely all other software developers in all specializations with all sets and levels of knowledge, skills, 
and abilities, as total remuneration among such disparate workers may vary. See, e.g., Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. 953, 955 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( considering a professional golfer's earnings compared 
to other Professional Golfers' Association Tour golfers); Skokos v. DHS, 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 
(9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to 
others in the field); Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering a National 
Hockey League (NHL) enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 
444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) ( comparing salary of an NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL 
defensemen). 
Even to the extent that wage information in the record for "software developers" may be the most 
appropriate occupational classification as a frame of reference, it bears minimal probative value. The 
OFLC wage information states that it pertains to the period of"7 /2025 - 6/2026." However, as noted 
above, the Petitioner submitted IRS Forms W-2 for the Beneficiary for the years of 2022-24. Wage 
information for portions of 2025 and 2026 do not inform what wages of software developers were 
during 2022-24, the period in which the Petitioner purports the Beneficiary's salary or other 
remuneration was high relative to others. In tum, the Careeronestop.com wage information does not 
state the period during which it reports data, although we note that it bears a 2025 copyright notice, 
indicating that its information falls outside the period of 2022-24. Finally, the documents that purport 
to be BLS wage estimates are not properly authenticated. Rather, they bear a handwritten statement 
"BLS wage estimates (U.S.)," without any other indicia that the data depicted in them actually 
originated from the BLS. Even to the extent that the purported BLS data may be deemed authentic, 
similar to the Careeronestop.com information, the documents do not indicate the date range to which 
it purportedly corresponds. For the foregoing reasons, none of the wage information probatively 
establishes what salaries or other remuneration may have been during the relevant period, even if that 
occupational classification were most appropriate in this circumstance. 
The two job postings the Petitioner submits are for "senior software engineer" positions at Capitol 
One and Liberty Mutual. We first note that, similar to the evidence discussed above, neither of the 
job postings appear to correspond to the relevant period. The Capitol One job announcement states 
that it was posted "08/21/2025" and, although the Liberty Mutual job announcement does not identify

Even ifthe job postings were dated during the relevant period and addressed others in the Beneficiary's

Without disclosing what the positions' compensation plans and total remuneration would be, neither 
job posting informs what other "senior software engineer" workers receive, in order to compare those 
salaries or other remuneration to that of the Beneficiary. And, given that employers providing 
unspecified additional remuneration to software engineers seems to be a common practice, the 
omission of cash bonuses and L TI from the wage information from OFLC and Careeronestop.com 
addressed above further undermines their limited informative value regarding what the occupational 
classification's actual total remuneration estimates may be. We also note that, even ifthe Capitol One 
and Liberty Mutual job postings specified the total salary or other remuneration those positions 
actually would receive, job postings for only two other workers provide limited information regarding 
what the salary or other remuneration range generally is among all relevant workers. 
For all of the foregoing reasons, the record does not establish what the salary or other remuneration of 
other "senior staff software engineers" were during the period in which the Petitioner asserts the 
Beneficiary's salary or other remuneration qualifies. Therefore, we withdraw SCOPS' statement that 
the record satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d 
at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Nevertheless, we will address the final merits below because SCOPS' 
basis for denying the Form 1-140 was that the record does not support the conclusion the Beneficiary 
is in that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and that he sustained 
6 
national or international-rather than localized or internalized-acclaim. 3 See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) 
of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). For the reasons discussed below, we agree. 
As noted above, letters from the Petitioner and the Beneficiary's coworkers bear minimal limited 
informative value. For example, the letters generally reference the Beneficiary's "career of over 
9 years" and, similarly, the Beneficiary's resume in the record claims that the Petitioner employed him 
since 2015, apparently with the position title of "Sr. Staff Engineer" for the entire time. However, the 
record does not establish when the Petitioner actually hired the Beneficiary and, more to the point, 
when the Beneficiary may have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and attained any degree 
of national or international acclaim by virtue ofhis employment among 17,000 other individuals who 
work for the Petitioner, and how he may have sustained such acclaim after that unspecified date. The 
Petitioner does not clearly indicate the date(s) on which the Beneficiary's performance may have been 
leading or critical to it or any particular division or department thereof, rather than merely leading 
certain projects or teams within unspecified divisions or departments, and how such a performance 
may have been recognized outside the Petitioner's own organization to be indicative of having risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor and accruing national or international acclaim, let alone how 
such acclaim may have been sustained. Likewise, the record does not establish what acclaim­
whether national, international, or otherwise-the Beneficiary may have received as a result of his 
salary or other remuneration in 2022-24, which limits its relevance for determining whether he may 
have sustained the requisite level of acclaim. 
We acknowledge that some of the patents addressed in the record may have garnered a degree of 
interest from others; however, that interest has thus far been brief: given the close proximity between 
the patent grants and the Form 1-140 filing date. We also acknowledge the Beneficiary is credited as 
a contributor to research projects leading to those patents and one scholarly article. In particular, we 
note that, unlike the Beneficiary, two of the four co-authors for the research paper crediting him were 
professors of computer and information engineering. But even when considered together, they do not 
sufficiently establish that he has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, or the nexus between 
his specific contributions and any acclaim the research paper may have received. 
For the foregoing reasons, the record does not support the conclusion that the Beneficiary has sustained 
national or international acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field 
of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The evidence does not meet at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Additionally, the totality ofthe record does not support a conclusion that the Petitioner has established 
the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-
20. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically 
meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 
1994). The Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
3 Black's Law Dictionary defines "sustain" as "to support or maintain, especially over a long period of time" or "to persist 
in making (an effort) over a long period of time." Sustain, Black's Law Dictionary (11th ed. 2019).

to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act; see also 8 C.F .R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2).
