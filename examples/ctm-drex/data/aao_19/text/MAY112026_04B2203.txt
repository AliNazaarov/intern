Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an audio engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b0(1)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner qualifies as an alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internally 
recognized award, or that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
111. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed to meet eight criteria: receipt of lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards (i); published material about the alien (iii); judge of the 
work of others (iv); original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of 
major significance in the field (v); authorship of scholarly articles (vi); display of work in the field at 
artistic exhibitions or showcases (vii); performed in a leading or critical role (viii); and high salary or 
remuneration (ix). SCOPS evaluated the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in 
response to the request for evidence and concluded the Petitioner satisfied three criteria, published 
material, judge of the work of others, and leading or critical role. SCOPS also considered the totality 
of the evidence in a final merits determination and concluded the Petitioner did not establish that he is 
among the small percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims to meet all eight criteria. He contends that SCOPS misapplied the 
Kazarian framework, issued a conflicting decision, and focused on individual analysis of each 
criterion rather than reviewing the evidence as a whole in the final merits determination. The 
Petitioner also contends that SCOPS cited to Matter ofHo in its decision without providing sufficient 
notice. See Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582 (BIA 1988).

B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has established that he meets the requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether 
he has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he has sustained national or 
international acclaim and is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and 
that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final 
merits determination, we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the 
evidence to determine if his successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in 
the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2)-(3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
As previously noted, the Petitioner contends on appeal that SCOPS failed to conduct a proper final 
merits analysis. Nonetheless, we exercise de novo review of all issues of fact, law, policy, and 
discretion. See Matter o_f Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537. This means that we look at the record 
anew and are not required to defer to findings made in the initial decision. Furthermore, our decision 
may address new issues that were not raised or resolved in the prior decision. 
While we may not discuss every document in the record, we have reviewed and considered each one. 
Based on our de novo review, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this 
highly restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will discuss below. 
The Petitioner maintains that he has "demonstrated sustained national and international acclaim and 
is among the small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of sound engineering." He 
asserts that the record demonstrates he "operates at the pinnacle of a highly specialized field, that his 
work is recognized by peers, institutions, and the national industry, and that his influence extends 
beyond individual projects into the development of the field itself." However, the record, in the 
aggregate, does not support the conclusion that he has shown the required acclaim and recognition, or 
otherwise shown that he is one of the small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor. See Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner asserts that he has held a critical role as a senior audio engineer for a company with a 
distinguished reputation. He explains that his responsibilities extend beyond mixing sounds to ensure 
that "sound is not just heard but felt." He also emphasizes his collaboration with "dialogue editors, 
artists, and technicians and assistants" to provide clear direction and ensure his work aligns with the 
"creative vision." He further asserts that his contributions have resulted in a "20-30%" cost savings 
and reduced the risk of delays, failed quality checks, or rejections by platforms due to non-compliance 
with audio standards. Although SCOPS determined that the Petitioner has met the regulatory criterion, 
it concluded that he has not demonstrated gaining national or international attention or placing him 
among the upper echelon in his field because of his critical roles.

The Petitioner submitted expert opinion and support letters to corroborate his claim. For instance, he 
submitted a letter that describes him as "an indispensable and highly critical professional." Another 
author states that the Petitioner's "technical and creative decisions" have "a direct impact on the 
overall quality of the production and the audience's experience." A third letter highlights the 
Petitioner's "solution-oriented mindset, technical mastery, and leadership skills," asserting that these 
qualities have played "a decisive role" in the company's success and that many projects would not

leader in his field and an experienced audio engineer, noting that he is consistently assigned to the 
"highest-budget and most-watched productions," which the author claims demonstrated the 
Petitioner's "critical role within the company." 
While the record demonstrates that the Petitioner performs a central and influential role within his 
company, it does not include documentation that ties the Petitioner's leading or critical roles for his 
previous and current employers to recognition in his field for accomplishments garnering sustained 
national or international acclaim. For instance, the evidence is insufficient to demonstrate that the 
Petitioner's work drew attention from the field beyond the accolades and resulted in national or 
international acclaim. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The Petitioner also has not offered sufficient evidence 
showing that the field or the public had taken notice ofhis work, or that the field has accepted, adopted, 
or otherwise widely implemented processes he had created. 
The Petitioner states that his work has been "publicly exhibited through his creative leadership in

the record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's displayed work distinguishes him as one of that 
small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field. Moreover, the Petitioner did not show how 
the number of his displayed work compared with those of others at the very top of the field.

Regarding the Petitioner's original contributions to the field, and in the context of a final merits

Here, regardless of whether the evidence establishes the regulatory criterion of original contributions of 
major significance, the evidence is insufficient to demonstrate that the Petitioner's contributions are 
indicative of sustained national or international acclaim. The evidence also does not demonstrate that his 
work has been widely recognized beyond his immediate professional circles or that it has positioned him 
among the small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor. 
Although the Petitioner provided support letters, the record does not contain sufficient information to 
establish that the Petitioner is viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, to be among the

being among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has submitted the required initial evidence that meets at least three of the ten categories 
of evidence listed under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we have provided the type of final 
merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1119-20. Based on our review of the record 
as a whole, we conclude that the record, in the aggregate, does not support a conclusion that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required to be classified as an alien of 
extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals at the top of their 
respective fields. Here, he has not shown that his accomplishments rise to the required level of 
sustained national or international acclaim, or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility to be classified as an 
alien of extraordinary ability in his field. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
