Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a director of aviation finance, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that, although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
the initial evidentiary requirements, it did not establish that the Petitioner has sustained national or 
international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. The matter is 
now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate recognition 
of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If the petitioner does not submit this evidence, then they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). If the evidentiary standards do not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, 
they may submit "comparable evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner, currently employed by Iis a director of aviation finance. His experience includes 
financial structuring, aircraft sales, and export credit financing. He intends to continue his work in the 
United States to promote liquidity in aircraft financing and support the growth of U.S. aviation exports 
and the national economy. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied seven of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards; 
• (ii), Membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media; 
• (iv), Participation as a judge of the work of others in the field; 
• (vii), Display of work at artistic exhibitions and showcases; 
• (viii), Leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• (ix), High salary or remuneration for services. 
USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met the requirements for four of the claimed criteria: published 
material about him, participation as a judge of the work of others, performance in a leading or critical

role, and high salary or remuneration for services. 1 However, in a final merits determination, users 
concluded that the Petitioner did not show that he garnered sustained national or international acclaim 
and that his achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor at a level to demonstrate that

Because users concluded that the Petitioner has established three of the initial criteria, on appeal we

B. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the denial rests on a subjective reassessment ofthe same evidence 
already deemed sufficient under the regulatory criteria. He states that users did not articulate any 
objective or measurable benchmark against which the evidence was evaluated and that this approach 
uses an additional filter beyond the standard of preponderance of the evidence that departs from the 
two-step framework in Kazarian. He further asserts that USeIS discounted each category of evidence 
in isolation. 
As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, that he is one of that small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(l). The 
preponderance standard governs whether disputed facts are more likely than not established; it does 
not lower the substantive showing that the extraordinary-ability classification demands. Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. Although the Petitioner claims that the decision "reflects an unusually 
restrictive interpretation of the 'top of the field' requirement," the regulation itself defines the 
classification narrowly, reserving it for "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the 
field of endeavor." 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). In determining whether a petitioner has established 
extraordinary ability, we use a two-step analysis to evaluate the evidence. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20. See also generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8). As described in the USCIS 
Policy Manual, the evidence evaluated in the first step analysis is also reviewed in totality in the second 
step, where the officer must determine whether the person is one of that small percentage of the who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and that the person has sustained national or 
international acclaim. Id. Objectively meeting the regulatory criteria in the first step does not establish 
that the person in fact meets the requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability. 
Id. A final merits determination that requires persuasive, objective evidence of that standing is 
applying, not departing from, the preponderance standard. In a final merits determination, "the 
petitioner must provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence and considerations to 
demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary 
ability in the totality of the circumstances." Id.

Viewing the record as a whole-the qualifying published material, the single judging engagement, the 
Petitioner's leading role and compensation, and the conference participation and industry recognition 
he urges us to weigh in his favor-the record depicts an accomplished, well-regarded, and rising 
professional in a specialized segment of a global industry. It does not, however, establish by a 
preponderance of the evidence that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim 
placing him among the small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of aviation finance. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Evidence that an individual is successful, 
respected, or even praised by senior figures in the field is not the same as evidence of preeminence. 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121; Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (even 
performance at the major-league level does not automatically establish extraordinary ability). Further, 
satisfying the plain language of four regulatory criteria does not compel a different result, since the 
final merits determination exists precisely to test whether evidence meeting that numeric threshold 
nonetheless falls short of the statute's substantive standard. Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-22. 
It is the Petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the benefit sought. See section 291 of the Act; 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 T&N Dec. at 375-76. For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not 
established, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he is one of the small percentage who has risen 
to the very top of the field of aviation finance, as required for classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability under section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The appeal will be dismissed. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
7 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5, aff'd, 2023 WL 
115680 l (stating that the extraordinary ability visa category is reserved for a very small percentage of 
prospective immigrants and courts have upheld denials of this restrictive category for very impressive 
individuals); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) 
(stating that EB-1 visas are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount ofindividuals 
with extraordinary ability); see also Hamal v. Dep 't of Homeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 
2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a 
published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that 
"[c ]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. 
Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball 
players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
