Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability "as An International 
Consultant and Policy Analyst for Central Asian Affairs." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant 
visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three, as required, of the 
initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). USCIS determined the Petitioner met only one (role under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)) of the 
five claimed categories of evidence. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he fulfills four additional 
criteria. 1 
A. Awards 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion, and we agree with that 
determination. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's 
receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of 
endeavor." USCIS determines whether the award is a lesser nationally or internationally recognized 
prize or award which the person received for excellence in the field of endeavor. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1 ), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Relevant considerations 
regarding whether the basis for granting the prizes or awards was excellence in the field include, but 
are not limited to: the criteria used to grant the awards or prizes, the national or international 
significance of the awards or prizes in the field, the number of awardees or prize recipients, and 
limitations on competitors. Id.

an award available only to persons within a single locality, employer, or school may have little national 
or international recognition); see also Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) 
(USCIS must focus on the recognition of the award, not its issuer). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate he meets this criterion. 
B. Published Materials 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion, and we agree with that 
determination. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p ]ublished material about the 
alien in professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in 
the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of 
the material, and any necessary translation." USCIS determines whether the published material was

related to the person and the person's specific work in the field for which classification is sought. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The Petitioner claims eligibility for this 
criterion based an article published by Radio Azattyk. 
At the outset, the Petitioner did not include the required author of the article. See, e.g., Javakhadze v. 
Mayorkas, No. 3:21-CV-00617-E, 2023 WL 8037730, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 20, 2023) (upholding 
articles did not contain the required authors); Kumar v. Dir. of US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 
CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding plaintiff's evidence did 
not include the required title, date, or author); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087, at *4 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (upholding petitioner did not provide sufficient evidence of the 
required title, date, and author of the material); Limon v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV H-25-0063, 
2026 WL 2000101, at *4 (S.D. Tex. June 22, 2026) (upholding articles were either missing the name 
of the publication, the name of the author, or the date of the publication); Nakos, 2026 WL 2042845, 
at *5 (finding plaintiff's contention that identifying the publisher of a submitted material satisfies the 
requirement that the evidence include the author of the material is inconsistent with the regulation's 
plain language). 
Notwithstanding the above, the article does not reflect published material about the Petitioner. Rather, 
the article relates to convicted defendant! land his request for early release from prison. 
See, e.g., Lapteva, 2025 WL 815087, at *4 (finding the regulation and USCIS policy clearly intend for 
evidence under this criterion to say something substantive about the petitioner and her work); Nakos, 
2026 WL 2042845, at *5 (upholding that the article only mentioned the plaintiff in passing). In fact, 
the Petitioner is mentioned one time as informing Radio Azattyk that the I lhad not 
received any appeals froml I See, e.g., Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 
2023 WL 6931334, at *4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) (upholding articles only citing, quoting, or 
referencing the beneficiary do not qualify as about him); Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. Ctr., United 
States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001, at *3 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) 
(upholding an article that included quotes from the petitioner, did "not make the article about" him); 
Nelson v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. CV 21-10334-LTS, 2022 WL 20583561, at *5 (D. 
Mass. June 15, 2022) (finding a reasonable decisionmaker could conclude articles are not about the 
expert quoted nor are they comparable to such articles). 
In addition, the Petitioner did not establish that Radio Azattyk represents a professional or major trade 
publication or other major medium. USCIS determines whether the publication qualifies as a 
professional publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. See generally 6 USCIS

publication, major trade publication, or major media, relevant factors include the intended audience 
(for professional and major trade publications) and the relative circulation, readership, or viewership 
(for major trade publications and other major media). Id. The Petitioner provided an "About Us"

qazpolit.org about Radio Azattyk, RFE, and RL. However, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how the 
content points to Radio Azattyk's professional, or major trade publication, or other major medium 
standing.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the high salary criterion, (8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix)), we need not reach this ground, as well as a review of USCIS' favorable 
determination for the role criterion (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)), because the Petitioner cannot fulfill 
the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See Kinuthia, 702 F. 
Supp. 3dat 11, ajf'd, 2025 WL3491324, cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 2643 (declining to consider a criterion 
when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we decline to reach and hereby reserve 
these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are 
7 
not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate 
decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
