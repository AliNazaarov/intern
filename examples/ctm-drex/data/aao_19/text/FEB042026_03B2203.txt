Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a structural engineer who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did she 
demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility to 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance ofthe evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de nova. Matter of Christo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
We adopt and affirm SCOPS' ultimate determination. See Matter ofBurbano, 20 I&N Dec. 872, 874 
(BIA 1994); see also Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (noting that the practice of 
adopting and affirming the decision below "is not only common practice, but universally accepted"). 
SCOPS provided a reasoned analysis of the record and although the Petitioner claims SCOPS 
committed various errors in the denial, she has not shown that any error-or combination of 
errors- would result in a different outcome for her petition. It is not enough to demonstrate errors in 
an agency's decision; a petitioner must also establish they were prejudiced by the mistakes. Shinseki 
v. Sanders, 556 U.S. 396, 409 (2009); Molina-Martinez v. United States, 578 U.S. 189, 203 (2016). 
We do, however, withdraw SCOPS' comments when they considered whether her entry will 
substantially and prospectively benefit the United States as they included language and concepts that 
do not apply to this immigrant classification. However, as other unmet requirements are dispositive 
of this appeal, we do not need to address this issue any further. 
We also note that the Petitioner initially addressed two criteria that she abandoned in her response to 
SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE) as she did not offer a response to that notice. Those criteria relate 
to memberships under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and the display of her work under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vii). She does not address the membership criterion on appeal, but she does raise her 
eligibility under the display criterion. But when she failed to address either of these requirements in 
the RFE response, she relinquished her ability to further claim them later in the proceedings, such as 
on appeal. An RFE is intended to clarify eligibility as of the petition's filing date. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.2(b)(8), (12). Under 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(13)(i), failure to respond to specific RFE elements 
constitutes abandonment or waiver ofthose claims. A petitioner cannot later revive such claims on appeal 
after knowingly declining to respond. 
The Board of Immigration Appeals' (the Board) precedents favor this reading. In Matter of R-S-H-, 
23 I&N Dec. 629,638 (BIA 2003) the Board explained that when faced with an adverse scena1io (e.g., 
SCOPS outlining an evidentiary shortcoming within an RFE) a filing party must offer a response and 
advance their arguments to preserve that topic for consideration on appeal). The Board concluded the 
same in Matter ofC-A-R-R-, 29 I&N Dec. 13, 17 (BIA 2025) in which they decided that when the trier 
of fact requests supplemental statements or documents it deems necessary to complete an adjudication, 
but those mate1ials are not timely submitted, the opportunity to make those statements or documents pati 
of the record is waived. Also, in Matter ofLopez-Ticas, 29 I&N Dec. 90, 94 (BIA 2025), the Board 
dete1mined that an issue is waived or forfeited if we issue a notice, and the filing party fails to address a 
particular issue within the notice that they disagree with or that they deem is incorrect. 
So, we will not entertain new arguments on appeal-such as her new comparable evidence 
arguments-that were not raised in response to SCOPS' RFE. Matter ofR-S-H-, 23 I&N Dec. at 638 
(citing Matter ofGarcia-Reyes, 19 I&N Dec. 830, 832 (BIA 1988)). The Petitioner had the opportunity 
to present these arguments and failed to do so. And federal courts have acknowledged such USCIS action 
when the agency dete1mines a filing patty's failure to respond to a specific eligibility requirement in an 
RFE means they have abandoned or waived that issue. See Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 807 
(E.D. Pa. 2025). Issues not properly developed before SCOPS are not preserved for appeal. See Santos 
Garcia v. Garland, 67 F.4th 455,460 (1st Cir. 2023); Ali v. Garland, 33 F.4th 47, 55 (1st Cir. 2022); 
Indrawati v. US. Atty Gen., 779 F.3d 1284, 1297 (11th Cir. 2015); Jeune v. US. Atty. Gen., 810 F.3d 
792, 800 (11th Cir. 2016). 
Also, despite SCOPS informing the Petitioner of the types of comparative evidence USCIS policy 
requires to demonstrate a media source qualifies as other major media, the record still does not contain 
that requisite type of material. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (emphasis added) (noting when USCIS evaluates whether a 
submitted publication is other major media, relevant factors include the relative circulation, 
readership, or viewership that should be compared to other similar statistics). See also Branski v. 
Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *5 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) (indicating that the failure 
to provide context to circulation figures makes it difficult to assess their significance); Krasniqi v. 
Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 185 (D.N.J. 2021) (upholding this office's determination the plaintiff 
provided no basis to gauge the circulation ofthe publication in which she appeared); Jafarov v. USCIS, 
No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *7 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding the petitioner's 
evidence reflecting only of the frequency of their circulation, with conclusory claims about their 
national importance, insufficient to establish the major status of the publications). 
And to briefly touch on her contributions of major significance claims, while she appears to have 
played an important role in a technology's development, she has not established-as claimed-that

she was the sole person to which it can be attributed. Vague references in support letters that she 
played a pivotal role are insufficient to support her claims that she solely developed the technology. 
And although the technology does appear outside of her home country, she has not demonstrated the 
product she claims association with, in and of itself, resulted in contributions ofmajor significance in 
the field, rather than existing as one similar product among a class of open web steel joists structural 
technologies. 
Ultimately, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the record supports granting an additional criterion, 
which would satisfy the first oftwo steps in the adjudication process, thereby warranting a final merits 
analysis in the second procedural step. The Petitioner has not explained how the evidence she 
submitted adequately supports her claims that she qualifies for this highly restrictive immigrant 
classification. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
