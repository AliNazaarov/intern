Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a corporate attorney, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for 
U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
two of ten initial evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On 
appeal, the Petitioner contends that he satisfied two additional criteria by submitting evidence of his 
receipt of nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field and his 
commandment of significantly high remuneration for his services. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he has not met the two additional evidentiary criteria that he claims to have 
satisfied. But, because we fault the remuneration evidence for a different reason than SCOPS did, we 
will withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternate evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (establishing a two-step process for 
adjudicating extraordinary ability petitions); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The Petitioner earned a bachelor of law degree in Taiwan and master and doctorate degrees in law in 
the United States. Since 2010, he has worked as chief counsel and director of the corporate and 
compliance legal division of a large semiconductor company there. He specializes in cross-border 
investment legal strategy and regulatory compliance. He seeks to continue working for his employer 
by leading the legal affairs of its operations in the United States. 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of his: 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field; and 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with outstanding reputations. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), (viii).

evidentiary criteria: receipt ofnationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence 
in his field; and commandment of a high salary or significantly high reimbursement for services in his 
field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ix). 2 To satisfy the additional claimed requirements, his evidence

B. The Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards for Excellence in the Field 
This criterion requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines if a petitioner - as opposed to their 
employer, for example - received prizes or awards. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)(l ), 
Criterion One. If so, USCIS then considers whether an award is nationally or internationally 
recognized and issued for excellence in the field of endeavor. Id. Relevant considerations include: 
an award's criteria; its national or international significance in the field; the number of award 
recipients; and any limitations on competitors. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, as part of an internal trainer program, he received an annual 
"Excellent Instructor Award" from his current employer. He said that he was selected for the award 
from among 4,000 internal trainers at the company. 
As SCOPS found, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that this award garnered national or 
international recognition. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)( 1 ), Criterion One. ("[ A ]n 
award available only to persons within a single locality, employer, or school may have little national 
or international recognition.") ( emphasis added). Although the Petitioner works for a large company, 
he has not established the award's recognition beyond his employer. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence of how a larger audience viewed Plaintiff's 
awards, there was no way for the agency to evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely 
enough to satisfy this criterion."). 
The Petitioner also submitted evidence that he received a one-year, $15,000 fellowship from a 
scholarly exchange foundation in 2009 based on his doctoral dissertation. On appeal, he notes that 
qualifying awards for this criterion may include "[ c ]ertain doctoral dissertation awards." 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion One. 
Here, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the fellowship's issuance for excellence in the 
legal field. His dissertation concerned legal studies. But the record shows that the foundation issues 
these fellowships more broadly to researchers in the humanities and social sciences. Of the 13 
fellowship recipients in 2009, the record identifies the Petitioner as the only one researching law. He 
has not demonstrated that he competed for the fellowship against others in his field. See Sun v. Jaddou, 
No. 3:24-CV-01466-E, 2025 WL 1556944, at *4 (N.D. Tex. June 2, 2025) (ruling that a petitioner did 
not establish recognition in her specific field of an award involving various fields); see also 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion One (listing "[!]imitations on competitors" as a relevant factor 
when considering whether an award was issued for excellence in a field). 
3 
Also, the Petitioner has not established that the fellowship's issuance received national or international 
recognition. He provided evidence that the issuing foundation honors a former president of Taiwan, 
and the Petitioner claims that the foundation is very prestigious. But he has not demonstrated that the 
fellowship award itself garnered national or international recognition. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. 
Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (stating that USCIS must focus on the recognition of the award, not 
its issuer); see also Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 136. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated his receipt of nationally or internationally recognized awards for 
excellence in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding. 
2. Commandment of a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration for Services 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted copies ofcompensation statements from his employer. The statements show 
his receipt of total salaries of 3,711,912 Taiwan New Dollars (TWD), or $116,771.92, and 3,895,412 
TWD, or $122,544.59, in 2023 and 2024, respectively. 3 The statements also show that he received 
performance bonuses and profit sharing totaling 26,614,000 TWD in 2023 and 25,280,000 TWD in 
2024. Thus, his total annual compensation amounts were 30,325,912 TWD, or $953,622.02, in 2023 
and 29,175,412 TWD, or $917,443,64 in 2024. 
The Petitioner further submitted copies of salary guides for Taiwan. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion Nine ("Officers evaluate persons working outside of the United States based on 
the wage statistics or comparable evidence relevant to the applicable work location.") For general 
counsels/directors, the 2023 salary guide shows a minimum compensation amount of 3,000,000 TWD 
and a maximum of 8,000,000 TWD. For the same occupation, the 2025 salary guide reflects a 25th 
percentile amount of4,500,000 TWD and a 75th percentile amount of8,500,000 TWD. The Petitioner 
therefore argues that the total annual compensation for his services significantly exceeded that of 
others in his field in Taiwan. 
The record does not support SCOPS's finding that the Petitioner "has not demonstrated that the 
remuneration he received from his performance bonus and profit sharing were earned for his services 
in the field." His 2023 and 2024 compensation statements state his "total annual compensation 
including salary and benefits" for the respective years and detail his salary, performance bonus, and 
profit sharing. The statements also "thank [him] for [his] dedication to [his employer]'s success" in 
the respective years. Thus, a preponderance of the evidence indicates that he received his salary, 
performance bonuses, and shared profits for the services he provided to his employer in his field. 
Nevertheless, the Petitioner has not demonstrated significantly high remuneration for his services in 
relation to others in his field. The titles of the "salary guides" suggest that they include only salaries 
for general counsels/directors in Taiwan. The record does not establish that the amounts in the salary

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the two additional evidentiary requirements that he claims to have met. 
He did not receive an opportunity, however, to respond to the alleged deficiency regarding the 
evidence of his claimed commandment of significantly higher remuneration than others in his field. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
