Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria, as required, he did not show his sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrate he is among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

B. Memberships 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
3 
The record reflects that the Petitioner did not claim eligibility for this criterion at initial filing. 1 In his 
RFE response, the Petitioner argued that his "three CCIE [Cisco Certified Internetwork Expert] 
certifications, when evaluated in totality with his extensive professional contributions and leadership 
roles, constitute comparable evidence of sustained national and international acclaim under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4), demonstrating recognition that clearly extends far beyond any single organization and 
reflects broad industry acceptance of exceptional achievement in the field." USCIS acknowledged the 
Petitioner's receipt of CCIE and determined that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion, including 
through the submission of comparable evidence. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that "[t]he record establishes that CCIE certification satisfies this 
requirement through a standardized, expert-administered evaluation process that objectively measures 
technical mastery." In addition, he references a previously submitted letter from S-I-, deputy chief 
technical officer at.__________. who stated that "[t]he CCIE certification is widely regarded 
as the highest-level technical credential in network engineering" and "CCIE status is awarded only 
after candidates successfully complete rigorous hands-on laboratory examinations that test real-world 
engineering skills under complex and time-constrained conditions." 
However, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how CCIE certification qualifies as a membership in an 
association pursuant to the requirements ofthis regulatory criterion. Again, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for 
which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." See also generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) (indicating that USCIS determines if the association for 
which the person claims present or past membership requires that members have outstanding 
achievements in the field as judged by recognized experts in that field). We consider the term 
"membership" using its ordinary, common meaning. See, e.g., Perrin v. United States, 444 U.S. 37, 
42 (1979) ("A fundamental canon of statutory construction is that, unless otherwise defined, words 
will be interpreted as taking their ordinary, contemporary, common meaning ...."). Here, the 
Petitioner possesses a "certification" or "credential" rather than a membership. Likewise, the 
Petitioner did not establish how CCIE qualifies as an "association" rather than as a type ofcertification. 
Although S-I- opined that the Petitioner's "achievement is exceptional because he holds three CCIE 
certifications across distinct technical domains," the Petitioner did not show that CCIE certification is 
tantamount to being a member in an association. See, e.g., Victorov v. Barr, No. CV 19-6948-GW­
JPRX, 2020 WL 3213788, at *7 (C.D. Cal. Apr. 9, 2020) (upholding that while an association gives 
out a title, the purported honor does not make the recipient a member of an organization or any other 
association). Thus, we need not further consider whether CCIE certification requires outstanding 
achievements, as judged by recognized national or international experts.

Moreover, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) states that "[i]fthe above standards do not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the 
beneficiary's eligibility." Moreover, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1) provides: 
This regulatory provision provides petitioners the opportunity to submit comparable 
evidence to establish the person's eligibility, if it is determined that the evidentiary 
criteria described in the regulations do not readily apply to the person's occupation. 
When evaluating such comparable evidence, officers must consider whether the 
regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether 
the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
A general unsupported assertion that the listed evidentiary criterion does not readily 
apply to the petitioner's occupation is not probative. Similarly, general claims that 
USCIS should accept witness letters as comparable evidence are not persuasive. 
However, a statement from the petitioner can be sufficient to establish whether a 
criterion is readily applicable if that statement is detailed, specific, and credible. 
Although officers do not consider comparable evidence where a particular criterion is 
readily applicable to the person's occupation, a criterion need not be entirely 
inapplicable to the person's occupation. Rather, the officer considers comparable 
evidence if the petitioner shows that a criterion is not easily applicable to the person's 
job or profession. 
In order to establish eligibility through comparable evidence, the Petitioner must first show that the 
particular criterion does not readily apply to the occupation and demonstrate how the evidence is truly 
comparable to that criterion. In other words, the Petitioner must show that the membership criterion 
does not readily apply to an information security analyst, software developer, or computer network 
architect. As he did not make any such argument regarding the inapplicability of the criterion to his 
occupation, the Petitioner did not demonstrate his eligibility to submit comparable evidence for this 
criterion. See, e.g., Zizi v. Cuccinelli, No. 20-CV-07856-SVK, 2021 WL 2826713, at *8 (N.D. Cal. 
July 7, 2021) (holding that "it is Plaintiff's burden to demonstrate that the standards in [(h)(3)] did not 
readily apply to his occupation before USCIS is required to consider comparable evidence"); Skokos 
v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 209CV00193RLHPAL, 2010 WL 11538054, at *5 (D. 
Nev. Jan. 13, 2010), aff'd sub nom. Skokos v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712 (9th Cir. 
2011) (holding that "it is up to the petitioner to argue before the agency that he is entitled to the desired 
classification under § 204.5(h)(4) if he believes the standards set forth in 
§ 204.5(h)(3) do not readily apply to him"); Liu v. Mayorkas, No. CV 20-704 (FYP), 2021 WL 
5578672, at *8 (D.D.C. Nov. 30, 2021) (upholding plaintiff's obligation to demonstrate that 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4) was applicable to her case before her comparable evidence could be 
considered). Accordingly, we not farther consider whether his CCIE certification qualifies as 
comparable evidence under the membership criterion. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner did not show he fulfills this criterion, including through 
the submission of comparable evidence.

C. Published Materials 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner fulfilled this criterion. However, for the reasons discussed 
below, we will withdraw that determination. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires 
"[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major media,

Initially, the Petitioner provided a YouTube screenshot claiming to be an interview of the Petitioner 
withl IHowever, the screenshot is in a foreign language without the required English 
language translation. In addition, the Petitioner did not include the original language transcription of 
the interview along with a certified English language translation of the transcription. Any document

§ 103.2(b)(3). The translator must certify that the English language translation is complete and 
accurate, and that they are competent to translate from the foreign language into English. Id. Because

Moreover, the evidence does not include the required date and author of the material. See, e.g.,

E. Comparable Evidence 
Notwithstanding our comparable evidence discussion under the membership criterion, the Petitioner 
makes a separate comparable evidence argument and asserts: 
USCIS also rejected comparable evidence on the ground that the regulatory apply to 
the Petitioner's occupation. That reasoning misstates the relevant inquiry. The issue is 
not whether the criteria exist in theory, but whether they adequately capture how 
excellence is recognized in the field. Where they do not, USCIS is required to consider 
comparable evidence. 
However, the Petitioner's assertions contradict the comparable evidence prov1s10ns under the 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), which states that "[i]fthe above standards do not readily apply to 
the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the 
beneficiary's eligibility." The Petitioner did not show that the intent of the comparable evidence 
regulation is to "adequately capture how excellence is recognized in the field," nor did the Petitioner 
demonstrate that the purpose of meeting individual criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) is to 
capture how excellence is recognized in the field. In fact, such "recognition" is decided in a final 
merits determination if the individual meets at least three regulatory criteria. In a final merits 
determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to 
determine sustained national or international acclaim,2 he is one of the small percentage at the very 
2 See generally 6 USC1S Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(l) (stating the definition of sustain is ·'to support or maintain, 
especially over a long period of time ... To persist in making (an effort) over a long period of time"). 
9 
top of the field of endeavor, and his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20.3 
Again, the comparable evidence provision provides petitioners the opportunity to submit comparable 
evidence to establish the person's eligibility under the evidentiary criteria, if it is determined that they 
do not readily apply to the person's occupation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). When evaluating such comparable evidence, USCIS must consider whether the regulatory 
criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is 
truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. Id. 
Here, the Petitioner did not demonstrate or identify which criteria, if any, do not readily apply to his 
occupation in order for us to even consider his eligibility to submit comparable evidence. See, e.g., 
Goncharov v. Allen, No. 3:21-CV-1372-B, 2022 WL 17327304, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 29, 2022) 
(finding because petitioner did not make a claim that his field does not have associations, he failed to 
demonstrate that his occupation qualifies for the submission of comparable evidence); Javakhadze, 
2023 WL 8037730, at *6 (finding plaintiff did not establish eligibility for comparable evidence 
because brief did not explain how or why the criterion did not apply to the plaintiff's occupation); Zizi, 
2021 WL 2826713, at *8 (holding that "it is Plaintiff's burden to demonstrate that the standards in 
[(h)(3)] did not readily apply to his occupation before USCIS is required to consider comparable 
evidence"); Skokos, 2010 WL 11538054, at *5, aff'd, 420 F. App'x 712 (holding that "it is up to the

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although USCIS determined the Petitioner met the roles criterion under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) 
and the Petitioner contests the unfavorable decision regarding the original contributions criterion under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), we need not review these determinations because the Petitioner cannot 
fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See Kinuthia, 
702 F. Supp. 3d at 11, aff'd, 2025 WL 3491324, cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 2643 (declining to consider a 
criterion when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we decline to reach and hereby 
reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
