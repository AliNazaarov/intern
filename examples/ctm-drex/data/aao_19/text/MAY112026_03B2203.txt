Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability in business. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, ofthe initial evidentiary criteria. In addition, SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate his intent to continue his work in his area of expertise in the United States. The matter is 
now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien' s entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 
2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

C. Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." The 
Petitioner did not initially claim eligibility for this criterion. However, in his RFE response, the 
Petitioner claimed that he "reported gross professional income of COP $329,175,000, derived from 
senior executive leadership and strategic advisory activities in the business and industrial sectors," and 
"[t]hese earnings reflect compensation for high-level executive and consulting services consistent with 
his professional field and responsibilities." Although he provided his 2024 Colombian Income Tax 
Return, the Petitioner did not offer supporting evidence to corroborate his reported earnings. 2 
Moreover, his ambiguous references to "high-level executive," "strategic advisory activities," and 
"consulting services" and lack of supporting evidence does not demonstrate whether his reported 
income was derived from a single employer or combined with other employment or income. 
Therefore, the Petitioner did not show his actual employment income. 
As comparable earnings data, the Petitioner offered evidence from salaryexpert.com regarding the 
average salary of a chief executive officer (CEO) in Colombia. Again, the Petitioner did not show 
whether his $329,175,000 reported income was earned in whole or in part as a CEO. Even if we 
considered the entirety of his income as a CEO, the salaryexpert.com information reflects that he does 
not command a high salary in relation to other CEOs in Colombia. USCIS determines whether the 
person's salary or remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). While he earned more than the average 
salary ($290,122,593), the highest CEOs earn COP $490,042,196, substantially more than the 
Petitioner's $329,175,000. See, e.g., Kapoor v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 763 F. 
Supp. 3d 247,258 (E.D.N.Y. 2025) (stating USCIS reasonably concluded that the petitioner's evidence 
comparing average and median salaries of single-role positions to his multi-role position was 
insufficient to establish he commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field); see also Kinuthia 
v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 9 (D. Mass. 2023), aff'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Trump, No. 23-2029, 2025 
WL 3491324 (1st Cir. Aug. 12, 2025) (finding the petitioner compared his salary to the median or 
average salary of others rather than the salaries of other top earners, as the criterion requires); 
Strategati, LLCv. Sessions, No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL2330181, at *7 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 
2019) (upholding evidence of average salary levels is an insufficient basis for comparison to show 
high salary in relation to others in the field); Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, 
at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 
5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding evidence must demonstrate that plaintiff's salary is high rather 
than simply above average). Thus, the Petitioner did not establish that he commands a high salary in 
relation to other CEOs in Colombia.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the contributions (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v)) and 
roles (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)) criteria, we need not reach these grounds because the Petitioner 
cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See 
Kinuthia, 702 F. Supp. 3d at 11, aff'd, 2025 WL 3491324, cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 2643 (declining to 
consider a criterion when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we decline to reach 
and hereby reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter.3 
See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
