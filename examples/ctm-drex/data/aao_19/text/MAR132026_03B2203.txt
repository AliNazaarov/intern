Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a professional squash player, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability in athletics. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had satisfied at 
least three of the ten evidentiary criteria for this classification, as required. The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If the petitioner does not submit this 
evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to 
submit comparable material if the they can demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence 
requirements, we then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th 
Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step analysis of extraordinary ability "consistent with the governing 
statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a professional squash player who has played for Pakistan's junior and senior national 
teams in international competition and as an internationally ranked competitor on the Professional 
Squash Association (PSA) Tour. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied seven of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (i), Lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards; 
• (ii), Membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media; 
• (iv), Participation as a judge of the work of others; 
• (v), Original contributions of major significance; and 
• (viii), Leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments. 
The Petitioner also claimed eligibility under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), by providing "comparable 
evidence" ofthe display ofhis work, his authorship ofa published article, and his commercial success, 
asserting that the corresponding criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), (vii) and (x) do not readily apply 
to his occupation. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner satisfied the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) based on his 
submission ofcomparable evidence. Therefore, he must establish that he meets at least two additional 
criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he meets all claimed criteria and contends that SCOPS 
"misapplied legal standards related to evidentiary sufficiency, comparable evidence, and the two-step 
analysis set forth in Kazarian." 
Upon de novo review, we conclude that the Petitioner has met at least two additional criteria under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and has therefore satisfied the initial evidence requirements. 
1. Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not demonstrate his receipt oflesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in his field of endeavor under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). In

reaching this conclusion, SCOPS observed that, although the Petitioner submitted evidence of his 
receipt of prizes or awards in squash competitions, there is "nothing in the record" showing that the 
awards are nationally or internationally recognized and no "objective documentary evidence 
demonstrating the significance of the awards." 
We disagree and find sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the Petitioner's receipt of a gold medal 
at the._______________________, held in Poland in 2016, satisfies the 
plain language requirements of this criterion. While the competition was limited to players under the 
age of 19, the record shows that the competitors were elite junior players chosen through a selective 
process to represent their countries at the highest level of international competition, and that the event 
received international media coverage that extended beyond the competition's organizer and 
participating national squash federations. See generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, F.2(B)(l) 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual, (noting that, while limitations on competitors can be a relevant 
factor, there is no specific requirement that an award be open to all members of the field, including 
the most experienced, to meet the requirements of this criterion). Accordingly, the Petitioner has 
satisfied the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
2. Published material in major media 
The plain language of the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires published material about a 
person in professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the person's work 
in the field for which classification is sought. SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's submission of 
multiple articles about him which relate to his work in the field but concluded that the supporting 
evidence relating to the online publications' circulation was insufficient to demonstrate the articles 
were published in "major media" or other qualifying media. SCOPS appeared to question the 
reliability of online articles, observing that "while internet websites can be accessed worldwide ... 
anyone with a computer can create a website." 
The record indicates that the Petitioner provided copies of more than 30 published articles in support 
of this criterion. While many of the articles do not satisfy all elements of this criterion, the Petitioner 
provided sufficient objective evidence in response to SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE) to establish 
that the newspapers! landl Iincluding their digital versions, qualify as major media in 
the United Arab Emirates and Pakistan, respectively, based upon the credible online circulation 
metrics and rankings submitted. Further the articles published by these newspapers in 2018 are solely 
focused on the Petitioner and his career as a squash athlete. Therefore, the Petitioner has demonstrated, 
by a preponderance of the evidence, that he satisfies this criterion.
