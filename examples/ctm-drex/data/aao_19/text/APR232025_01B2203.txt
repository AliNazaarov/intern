Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, who seeks employment as a basketball coach, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had satisfied 
at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. In making this decision, SCOPS observed 
that most of the evidence in the record concerns the Petitioner's past career as a basketball player, not 
as a coach. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner had not met any of the criteria. In making that conclusion, 
SCOPS stated that evidence relating to the Petitioner's past career as a basketball player does not 
relate to the Petitioner's intended field of endeavor as a basketball coach. 
SCOPS did not err by taking the Petitioner's career transition into account. The USCIS Policy Manual 
addresses the implications of a career transition from athlete to coach:

the same area of expertise. However, many extraordinary athletes have gone on to be 
extraordinary coaches.

However, the transition from athlete to coach is somewhat separate from the antecedent questions of 
whether the Petitioner has satisfied the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Upon 
consideration of the record, we conclude that the Petitioner appears to have satisfied at least three of 
the criteria in his earlier career as a basketball player. He won awards in national championships, was 
the subject of published material in major media, and judged the work of others. Because the 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) requires the satisfaction of only three criteria, we need not discuss 
the other five claimed criteria here and hereby reserve them. 1 
Ordinarily, the next step would be a final merits determination, as described in Kazarian, to determine 
whether the Petitioner had established that he achieved sustained national or international acclaim. 
Such a determination would take into account evidence that the Petitioner has played basketball at a 
national or international level. At the same time, competing at the major league level does not 
automatically establish extraordinary ability. See Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. 
Comm'r 1994), citing 56 Fed. Reg. 60899 (Nov. 29, 1991). The level at which the Petitioner has 
competed is a relevant factor but not, by itself: a determining one. 
A final merits determination relating to the Petitioner's career as a player would show that, while the 
Petitioner has facially satisfied the requirements of some of the criteria, the relevant evidence does not 
clear the higher threshold of showing sustained national or international acclaim. For example, while the 
Petitioner performed judging activities to satisfy 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), he did so at d~---~I

As another example, the Petitioner has attracted some media attention, but not consistently and only rarely

But separate from the issue of acclaim, the Petitioner must establish that he seeks to enter the United 
States to continue work in the area of claimed extraordinary ability. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii) of the 
Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). Like sustained acclaim, intention to continue work in the area of claimed 
extraordinary ability is a core eligibility requirement. 
SCOPS concluded that most ofthe evidence ofrecord "is related to the beneficiary's accomplishments 
as a basketball player. However, the beneficiary seeks to continue his work as a basketball coach. 
The evidence does not appear to relate to the beneficiary's work in the field in which the classification 
is sought." 
We agree with SCOPS' determination that, although the Petitioner intends to work as a coach in the 
United States, the substantial majority ofthe evidence in the record relates to the Petitioner's former career 
as an athlete. Evidence of acclaim as an athlete does not establish acclaim or extraordinary ability as a 
coach. See Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 917 (N.D. Ill. 2002) (finding that the "distinction between 
extraordinary ability as a coach and a player is a reasonable one, entitled to deference," because playing 
and coaching "are markedly different roles"). 
In this case, the Petitioner's career as a competing athlete was quite recent, ending only about a year 
before he filed the petition. Therefore, the Petitioner must not only establish that he achieved national or 
international acclaim as an athlete, but that he sustained such acclaim as a coach. 
Because the Petitioner has identified that he seeks employment in the field of coaching, and the statute 
requires that he "seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability," 
the athlete to coach transition necessarily relies on demonstration of extraordinary ability, and thus 
sustained national or international acclaim, as a coach. If the evidence of record does not demonstrate 
that the Petitioner has sustained acclaim at the national or international level as a coach, then a more 
detailed discussion of whether the Petitioner previously earned such acclaim as an athlete would not 
change the ultimate outcome of this proceeding. 
Therefore, below we will consider the key question of whether the Petitioner has established sustained 
national or international acclaim as a coach. 
The Petitioner must meet eligibility requirements at the time of filing. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l). Here, 
when the Petitioner filed the petition in July 2023, he had not yet been employed specifically as a coach, 
as opposed to a player with incidental instructional responsibilities. The Petitioner does not claim to have 
held coaching credentials outside the United States. His resume indicates that he earned an associate' s 
degree in basketball coach education in Russia in 2022, shortly before he traveled to the United States. 
He received a coaching license from USA Basketball in November 2022, but was not employed in the 
United States until September 2023, when he began teaching fifth and sixth grade teams at a basketball 
academy in Florida. 
The Petitioner's U.S. employment as a coach began about two months after he filed the petition, and 
therefore cannot count toward eligibility at the time of filing.

III. CONCLUSION 
While SCOPS' denial pertained to the lack of evidence of sustained acclaim, SCOPS was correct to 
draw a distinction between the evidence regarding the Petitioner's career as an athlete and his intended 
future work as a coach. The Petitioner's sparsely documented coaching activities do not support a 
conclusion that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for classification 
as an alien of extraordinary ability as a basketball coach. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals who have already 
reached, and remain at, the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top, 
or who are embarking on new careers related to ones in which they had previously earned more 
recognition. 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree of recognition of his coaching work that rises to the level 
of sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the 
field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
