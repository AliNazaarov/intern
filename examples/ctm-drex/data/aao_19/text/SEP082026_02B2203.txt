Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur in the technology sector, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that while the Petitioner showed that he satisfied at least three 
of the initial evidentiary criteria, he did not establish that he has garnered sustained national or 
international acclaim for his achievements and is among the small percentage at the very top of the 
technology entrepreneurship field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § I03 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry into the United States 
will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec. , No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recogrut1on of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) states 
"[i]f the above standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may 
submit comparable evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision 
provides petitioners with the opportunity to submit comparable evidence to establish a person's 
eligibility, if it is determined that the evidentiary criteria described in the regulations do not readily 
apply to the person's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Since USCIS determined 
that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria, the 
issue before us is whether he demonstrated that he has garnered sustained national or international 
acclaim for his achievements and is among the small percentage at the very top of the technology 
entrepreneurship field. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must show that he satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed that he satisfied four of the ten criteria pertaining to 
published materials, judging, original contributions of major significance, and leading or critical role 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii),(iv), (v), and (viii), and that he demonstrated commercial success through 
the submission of comparable evidence pursuant to 8 CFR 204.5(h)(4). 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner met the three criteria that pertain to published materials, judging, 
and leading or critical roles, but found that he did not satisfy the original contributions of major 
significance criterion or provide comparable evidence establishing commercial success. Because 
USCIS found the Petitioner met at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria, they conducted a final 
merits determination. In doing so, USCIS concluded that the record did not demonstrate that the 
Petitioner garnered sustained national or international acclaim and that he is among that small 
percentage who bas risen to the very top of the field. Therefore, they denied the petition. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that USCIS imposed requirements not authorized in the regulations 
when reviewing his judging experience, relitigated a criterion the Petitioner was deemed to have met, 
and incorrectly "impos[ ed] a fixed-duration rule the regulation does not contain" when determining

B. Final Merits Determination 
As a preliminary matter we will address the Petitioner's reference to and reliance on Mukherji v. 
Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), a United States district court case that invalidated 
USCIS 's two-step final merits determination framework as unlawfully adopted without notice and 
comment rulemaking and ordered USCIS to approve the petition. 
Here, in contrast to the precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are 
not bound to follow a decision of a United States district court. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 
(BIA 1993). Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, 
unpublished opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att'yGen. United 
States, 147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non­
precedential opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 
n.5 (5th Cir. 2009); cf.Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 (considering the reasoning of district court 
decisions). We therefore determine that USCIS did not err in its analysis of the instant visa petition 
under the current two-step review framework provided in Kazarian and that we are not bound by the 
ruling ofMukherji v. Miller in the instant case. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1115. 
Accordingly, given the structure of the regulations and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, 
the satisfaction of at least three evidentiary criteria alone does not automatically or presumptively 
establish eligibility. In a final merits determination, we evaluate whether a petitioner has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, their sustained national or international acclaim, 2 
that they are among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that their 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. Therefore, we 
analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 3 In this matter, we conclude that 
the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility.

First, regarding the published material about the Petitioner, the record contains several articles from 
November and December 2024 and January 2025 discussing the Petitioner and the nutrition platform

Petitioner and his business partner and discuss their respective backgrounds as well as the ideas that

coverage recognizing his role as co-creator of a nutrition platform is indicative of a level of success 
consistent with being among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of his field. 
Further, when evaluating whether a petitioner has demonstrated sustained national or international 
acclaim, we consider whether such acclaim has been maintained. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(A)(l). "Sustain" is defined per Black's Law Dictionary (11th ed. 2019) as "to support or 
maintain, especially over a long period of time ... To persist in making (an effort) over a long period 
of time." Id. note 5. Here, the record shows that the press coverage of the Petitioner's digital health 
platform took place within approximately a three-month timeframe. The Petitioner did not 
demonstrate how this nominal press coverage reflects a career of acclaimed work in the field or meets 
the very high standard to present more extensive documentation than that required. See Bodhankar v. 
United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 
(N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the lack of press about the plaintiff was not consistent with national 
or international acclaim). For instance, the Petitioner did not show how the amount of his 
media coverage compares to others in his field who are among the very top. 
Thus, while the published material indicates that the Petitioner received some recent attention for his 
work regarding one of his projects,4 he has not established that such reporting is consistent with 
sustained national or international acclaim or a career of acclaimed work, nor has he established that

With respect to his original contributions, the Petitioner maintains that USCIS did not give sufficient 
weight to evidence of his work and achievements. Even if we concluded that the Petitioner has made

Petitioner claims that he "developed three distinct systems, each producing measurable results" via 
implementation of a "hard-discount grocery model"; a behavior-based credit and loyalty system that 
"supports lending decisions for consumers typically excluded from conventional credit"; and a

C. 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
Lastly, the Petitioner stresses his prior approval of an 0-1 nonimmigrant visa as an individual with 
extraordinary ability. However, the prior approval of an 0-1 petition is not determinative when 
evaluating eligibility for an immigrant petition for an alien of extraordinary ability. 9 We are not 
required to approve applications or petitions where eligibility has not been demonstrated merely 
because ofprior approvals that may have been erroneous. See Matter ofChurch Scientology Int 'I, 19 
I&N Dec. 593, 597 (Comm'r 1988). Each petition is separate and independent and must be 
adjudicated on its own merits, under the corresponding statutory and regulatory provisions. Here, for 
the reasons discussed, the Petitioner has not met her burden to establish that she is eligible for the 
requested immigrant classification. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
