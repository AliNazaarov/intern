Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an associate vice president of information security, seeks classification as an individual 
of extraordinary ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidence requirements for this classification by demonstrating 
his receipt of a major, internationally recognized award or by meeting at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
do not readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claimed to meet seven of the initial evidentiary criteria relating to lesser awards, 
membership, published material, judging, original contributions, leading or critical role, and high 
salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v), (viii), and (ix). SCOPS determined the Petitioner 
met only two of these initial evidentiary criteria for judging and leading or critical role. On appeal, 
the Petitioner asserts SCOPS erred in analyzing his evidence in support of high salary in relation to 
others in the field. 
Because the Petitioner has not shown that he received a major, internationally recognized award, he 
must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As stated 
above, SCOPS determined the Petitioner met two criteria relating to judging the work of others in the 
same field and performing in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a 
distinguished reputation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (viii). The record supports SCOPS' 
determinations on the criteria relating to judging, and leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation. The Petitioner provided evidence to establish that 
his work as Associate Vice President oflnformation Security atl Imeets 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). Also, the Petitioner provided sufficient evidence that he performed peer 
review of research articles in his field and that his work as a fellow and expert judge in the I I 
I Icompetition meets 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). For the reasons discussed below, the record 
does not support SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner did not meet the criterion for high salary. 
High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Comparative wage 
or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location should be submitted to 
establish that a petitioner's compensation is high relative to others working in the field. Id. 
2 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not meet this criterion because his 2022-2024 earnings 
statements (Form W-2) reflected wages of$151,214 and the evidence submitted of comparative data 
for other Information Security Officers "is not specific to his position ... as an Associate Vice 
President." On appeal, the Petitioner correctly asserts SCOPS erred by quoting an incorrect salary. 
Upon de novo review, the evidence establishes that the Petitioner has commanded a high salary in 
relation to others in his field. To meet this criterion, a petitioner must demonstrate that their salary or 
remuneration is high relative to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l)(criterion 9). Upon de novo review, we find that the Petitioner's 2024 salary 
demonstrates by a preponderance of evidence that he satisfies the criterion. Thus, we withdraw 
SCOPS' contrary determination. 
Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has established he meets the evidentiary criteria relating to judging, leading or critical 
role and high salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (viii) and (ix). As the Petitioner submitted the 
requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a 
preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, 1 that he is among the 
small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze 
a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his successes are 
sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.2 In 
this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility.

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for 
classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability." Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994). While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished 
to qualify for the classification sought, we find the record insufficient to demonstrate that he has 
sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the top of his field. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
