Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is an athlete who seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for 
Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did she demonstrate that she warranted an approval when 
considering the totality ofthe evidence. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 
291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in 
this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)( 4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable material if he or she is able to demonstrate that 
the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met four of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied each of the claimed criteria relating to prizes or awards, 
membership, published material, and performing in a leading or critical role, although we will 
withdraw the determination on one of those criteria. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
SCOPS made a favorable determination under this criterion. The Petitioner directly claimed the 
membership criterion based on her selection on various prominent diving teams. But as USCIS Policy 
reflects, this is a claim that better aligns with the comparable evidence regulatory provision at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual 
(stating that election to a national all-star or Olympic team is better considered under a comparable 
evidence claim for evidence of memberships in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)). However, the Petitioner 
did not make such a claim, and as a result, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination here. As 
the Petitioner still met at least three other criteria, this does not ultimately impact this case's outcome. 
We will still consider her membership claims in the final merits determination. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether she has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim and 
that she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that her 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits

l. Appellate Claims that Two-step Process is not Authorized 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 O; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. 
We start by disposing of the Petitioner's claim that a single district court opinion established the 
two-step process is ultra vires (i.e., beyond USCIS' legal authority). That opinion has no authority 
here. We are only bound to follow published case law from a U.S. court of appeals for sufficiently 
similar situations within the same circuit. Matter ofAnselmo, 20 I&N Dec. 25, 31 (BIA 1989); Matter 
ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715, 718 (BIA 1993). Cases before us that fall outside a U.S. court of appeals' 
jurisdiction carry no binding effect. Matter ofAnselmo, 20 I&N Dec. at 31 (finding administrative 
appellate bodies "are not required to accept an adverse determination by one circuit court of appeals 
as binding throughout the United States"). 
U.S. district courts' opinions carry even less of a binding effect as we are not required to follow their 
published decisions, not even within the same district. Matter ofDuarte-Gonzalez, 28 I&N Dec. 688, 
690 n.2 (BIA 2023). Although we may consider a district judge's reasoning as a useful aid in our 
analysis, we are not required to follow it as a matter of law. Matter ofN-A-G-C-, 29 I&N Dec. 662, 
665 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 718); see also Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 
831, 852 (2025) (concluding that as a matter of law, a district court's findings "extends only to the 
suing plaintiff-as evidenced by the fact that only the plaintiff can enforce the judgment against the 
defendant," and if another person or entity wishes to restrict the defendant's actions, they must file 
their own suit). 
But more than that, we do not agree with the district court's reasoning. The opinion the Petitioner 
references is Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026). 
There, the court noted that in 1995 the former Immigration and Naturalization Service proposed a 
two-step adjudicative process. The court also observed that the Office of Personnel Management's 
(OPM) docket showed that the 1995 notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) was classified as a 
substantive rule. Based on that classification of the entire NPRM, the court treated the two-step 
3 
process as the sole reason the proposed rnle was a substantive or legislative rnle, and it did not evaluate 
whether the numerous other provisions within the proposed rule might have been the basis for OPM's 
classification. 
On that understanding, the court concluded that the two-step adjudicative process constituted a 
legislative or substantive rule that could be implemented only through notice-and-comment 
rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). In doing so, the court did not fully engage 
with USCIS' s stated rationale for adopting the two-step process, namely that a federal circuit court 
required it, and that using one approach within that circuit and a different approach elsewhere was not 
prudent or feasible. USCIS' implementing memorandum discussed the circuit court's decision in 
detail and expressly adopted that approach. USCIS respectfully submits that this explanation satisfies 
any obligation to articulate a rational basis for the policy change. 
In addition, the Mukherji decision did not appear to address how that petitioner's satisfaction of the 
criteria in that case related to the method of adjudication. The court ordered approval of the petition 
without analyzing whether any favorable determinations made at step one had been reached under the 
prior approach in which USCIS did not conduct a qualitative sustained-acclaim analysis under each 
individual criterion. The five criteria the court found satisfied ( awards, published material, judging, 
contributions, and leading or critical role) thus were not adjudicated in the manner the court seemed 
to contemplate reinstating. USCIS respectfully submits that this omission was not harmless, because 
it bears directly on whether the court's remedy accords with the adjudicative framework it preferred. 
And about congressional intent, USCIS understands Congress to have intended that qualifying 
candidates for this immigration benefit be among those at the top of their field. The House Judiciary 
Committee explained: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small 
percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor." H.R. Rep. 
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at *6739 (House 
Committee Report). Mukherji, however, understood the legislative history differently, stating that the 
Conference Report on the Senate bill that ultimately became law "says absolutely nothing" about a 
requirement that persons seeking priority-worker extraordinary ability classification be part of "that 
small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field" or any similar 
formulation. H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, 101st Cong., 2d Sess. (Oct. 26, 1990). 
USCIS respectfully reads the legislative history differently. The "Senate Bill" material the court cited 
is from a Conference Report, while the "small percentage" language appears in a House Judiciary 
Committee Report. These documents serve different functions in the legislative process. When a 
conference report does not address a particular substantive issue, as is the case here, the committee 
reports that originally developed and explained the provision remain the most authoritative evidence 
of congressional intent. See Zuber v. Allen, 396 U.S. 168, 186 (1969) (noting that committee reports 
"represent the considered and collective understanding ofthose Congressmen involved in drafting and 
studying proposed legislation"); Garcia v. United States, 469 U.S. 70, 76 (1984) ( explaining that "[i]n 
surveying legislative history we have repeatedly stated that the authoritative source for finding the 
Legislature's intent lies in the Committee Reports on the bill"). 
The House Judiciary Committee Report, H.R. Rep. 101-723, supplies the substantive explanation of 
Congress's intent for the Immigration Act of l 990's extraordinary-ability classification, including the 
4 
"small percentage" formulation. As summarized by the Congressional Research Service, committee 
reports typically outline the need and purpose of legislation and may include statements of legislative 
intent to guide both the executive branch in implementation and the judiciary in interpretation. See 
Committee Reports: Required Contents, Congressional Research Service Products (Library of 
Congress) (Jan. 22, 2008). 
By contrast, the Conference Report, H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, primarily reconciles differences 
between the House and Senate versions and describes how those differences were resolved. Its focus 
is on the compromise text rather than on restating all underlying policy judgments that were already 
addressed in the committee reports. A conference report is just the final, official version of a 
compromise bill that only lists the specific legal text and the formal steps the House and Senate must 
take to pass it. Coeference Reports and Joint Explanatory Statements, CONGRESS.GOV (Jun. 11, 
2015), https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/98-382. In that light, the Conference Report's silence 
on the "top of the field" requirement is most reasonably understood as reflecting acceptance, rather 
than rejection, of the House Judiciary Committee's previously articulated intent. 
Accordingly, USCIS respectfully understands H.R. Rep. 101-723 to remain the controlling statement 
of congressional intent on this point. That understanding is reflected in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), which 
requires "a level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
Not only is the Petitioner incorrect in relying on this flawed analysis, but she also did not assert these 
claims in the proceedings before SCOPS and she may not advance them for the first time in this appeal. 
Whitton v. Dixon, No. 25-580, 2026 WL 1513270, at *2 (U.S. June 1, 2026) (declining to evaluate 
new claims, legal theories, or evidence that were not before the original deciding authority in the first 
instance); Santana-Gonzalez v. Bondi, 172 F.4th 736, 744 (9th Cir. 2026) (finding a filing party cannot 
raise new issues on appeal that it did not present to the lower trier of fact); Matter ofG-C-1-, 29 I&N 
Dec. 176, 177 n.2 (BIA 2025) (citing Matter ofH-L-S-A-, 28 I&N Dec. 228, 240 n.10 (BIA 2021) and 
noting that issues presented for the first time on appeal will not be addressed).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's 
burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that 
burden here.
