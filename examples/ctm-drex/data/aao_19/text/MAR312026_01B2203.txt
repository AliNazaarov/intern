Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a chief executive officer (CEO), seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b0(1)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner qualifies as an alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internally 
recognized award, or that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
111. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed to have satisfied eight of these criteria. In its 
decisions denying the petition, SCOPS determined that he satisfied two of the eight claimed criteria, 
relating to judging the work of others in the same or allied field and performing in leading or critical 
roles for organizations with a distinguished reputation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (viii). 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he submitted evidence satisfying the other six claimed criteria 
relating to awards, memberships, published materials about him, original contributions, scholarly 
articles, and high salary or other significantly high remuneration. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(iii), 
(v), (vi) and (ix). He contends that SCOPS disregarded or minimized probative evidence, applied 
standards beyond what the regulations and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) policy 
guidance require, and failed to conduct a final merits determination. 
Based on our de novo review, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for this 
highly restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will discuss below.

2. Membership in Associations 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.S(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility under 8 C.F.R. § 204.S(h)(3)(ii) based on his membership in the 
Forbes Business Council. SCOPS determined that the evidence does not establish that the association 
requires outstanding achievements of its members as judged by recognized national or international 
experts in their disciplines or fields. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS failed to properly consider the record and imposed a 
"more restrictive" standard. He contends that membership in the association is "selective and 
achievement based, not open to anyone who is willing to pay a fee." He also maintains that admission 
decisions are made by recognized experts in the field of business leadership and entrepreneurship. 
In support ofthese claims, the Petitioner submitted Forbes Council membership materials and a letter. 
In a September 2024 letter, Forbes Business Council's co-founder and chief operating officer (COO) 
confirms that the Petitioner is a member of Forbes Business Council. The letter states that Forbes 
Business Council is "an invitation-only organization for successful small and mid-sized business owners" 
and is "a professional community for top leaders that proactively connects them to each other as well as 
best-in-class resources based on their specific business needs." The letter explains that the minimum 
criteria for admission are the applicant"[ m Just be a senior-level executive with a successful company that 
[h]as at least $SOOK in annual revenue and/or [h]as at least $SOOK in financing." The letter further states 
that"[ e Jach application is reviewed by a selection committee composed ofexperts in business, leadership, 
and entrepreneurship," and that the Petitioner was invited to join because he has "a record of outstanding 
achievement in Food and Beverage Services," which "places [him] in the top echelon of business 
professionals and thus, warranted an invitation to join this prestigious council." 
However, the Petitioner has not shown that his membership in the Forbes Business Council meets this 
criterion because the record does not contain sufficient independent or corroborating evidence to 
substantiate his assertions. Specifically, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the association's 
membership criteria require "outstanding achievements of their members" within the meaning of the 
regulation, nor has he established that such achievements are evaluated by recognized national or 
international experts in their disciplines or fields. For example, the COO's letter, standing alone, is 
insufficient to establish that the association's membership criteria meet the regulatory requirements, 
including whether the membership is judged by recognized national or international experts in the field. 
Moreover, the record does not contain supporting documents such as bylaws, membership criteria, or 
other official organizational documentation describing the standards for obtaining membership in the 
associations. Although the Petitioner submitted Forbes Business Council materials and a letter, these 
4 
documents alone are insufficient to demonstrate that membership in Forbes Business Council requires 
outstanding achievements. 
Similarly, the record lacks evidence demonstrating that the individuals in the selection committee 
reviewing the Forbes Business Council membership applications possess recognized national or 
international expertise or that their evaluation are conducted pursuant to a standardized and well­
documented process. 
While we acknowledge that membership of Forbes Business Council may be important for 
professional development, the evidence is not sufficient to satisfy this criterion. It is the Petitioner's 
burden to submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates his claims. Statements made without 
supporting documentation are oflimited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the Petitioner's 
burden of proof Matter of Soffici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). Here, the Petitioner's 
membership in the Forbes Business Council is not sufficient to support fulfillment of this criterion.

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we decline to reach and hereby 
reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) 
(per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that 
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
10 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for 
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
