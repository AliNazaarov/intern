Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a wrestler, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1 l 53(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics through sustained national 
or international acclaim, and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner is the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, nor did he meet at least 
three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We 
review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics," which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Through their work, substantially benefit the country prospectively. 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise 
commensurate with individuals in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction ofat least three often lesser evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 Then, if a petitioner meets the initial evidence requirements, USCIS must 
conduct a final merits determination to assess whether the record-as a whole-shows a petitioner's 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th 
Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, iffulfilling 
the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The 
Petitioner is a Greco-Roman wrestler and intends to continue to compete in the United States. He 
contends that he has garnered recognition on a national and international scale and claims that his 
expertise will substantially benefit the United States. 
Because the Petitioner did not indicate or establish that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed to meet five ofthe ten evidentiary 
criteria: 
• (i) receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field; 
• (ii) membership in associations which require outstanding achievements of their members; 
• (iii) published material in major trade publications or other major media; 
• (iv) participation as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field; and 
• (v) original business-related contributions of major significance to the field. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner only satisfied the criteria relating to published material about 
the Petitioner in major trade publications or other major media. On appeal, the Petitioner continues to 
assert satisfaction of the remaining four criteria claimed and generally contends that the SCOPS' 
decision was erroneous. 
Upon de novo review, we agree with SCOPS' ultimate conclusion that the record does not establish 
the Petitioner is an alien of extraordinary ability. However, for the reasons below, we also withdraw 
its conclusion that the Petitioner meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) relating to published 
material about the Petitioner in major trade publications or other major media. 2

If a petitioner has shown that the published material is about him and his work, we will then determine 
whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication, or other major media. 
When evaluating whether a publication is a profession or major trade publication relevant factors 
include the intended audience (for professional and major trade publications) and the relative 
circulation, readership, or viewership (for major trade publications and other major media). 6 
The Petitioner submitted multiple articles published in various online publications, and a screenshot 
of an email indicating the Petitioner's participation in an event accompanied by multiple photographs 
of the Petitioner wrestling. SCOPS concluded, without discussion, that the evidence satisfies this 
criterion. Upon de novo review, we disagree and withdraw SCOPS' determination. 
First, the email indicating that the petitioner has "weighed in" at an event appears to be an individual 
email rather than a publication and therefore will not be considered. Moreover, several of the articles 
only report the results of competitions or events in which the Petitioner competed, and therefore name 
him as a participant, but are not "about" the Petitioner as required. For example, one article simply 
includes him as one of the individuals that would be competing in an upcoming event, without further 
discussion. The Petitioner has not established how the submission of articles reporting results from a 
sporting event or competition is consistent with the meaning of "published material about the alien" 
pursuant to the plain language of the regulation. See, e.g. Victorov v. Barr, 2020 WL 3213788, at *8 
(C.D.C.A. Apr.9, 2020) (finding a rational basis for the AAO's conclusion that articles about events 
in which a petitioner competed are not about the petitioner); Noroozi, 905 F.Supp.2d at 545 
( concluding that articles about a sporting team with brief mentions of an athlete are not considered to 
be about the athlete); see also Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 848 (C.D. Cal. 2022) 
(finding an article which reported on a sporting event and mentioned the alien only in identifying 
players who scored a goal was not about her). 
Moreover, while the remaining articles may discuss the Petitioner and his work, they nonetheless do 
not satisfy this criterion. Several of the articles submitted do not contain the author of the material as 
required by the regulation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). And while we have reviewed the letters 
submitted by First News and Newspost, which identify the authors oftheir articles about the Petitioner, 
the record does not otherwise establish that these publications are "professional or major trade 
publications or other major media" The letters do not indicate that these publications are for a limited 
intended audience or otherwise should be considered professional or major trade media, and beyond 
indicating the amount of views of each article, the letters do not provide the publications' circulation 
or other evidence to establish they can be considered "major media" as required. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For example, they do not explain the nature ofthese views 
(whether they indicate readership or individual views), and moreover, unsupported assertions 
regarding a publication's stature are insufficient to show that a medium qualifies under this criterion. 
See Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06 5105 SJO (C. D. CA July 6, 2007) aff'd 2009 WL 604888 (9th Cir. 
2009) ( concluding that self-serving assertions on the cover of a magazine as to the magazine's status 
is not reliable evidence that it is a major medium). Additionally, the Petitioner has not provided 
comparative evidence to establish that these views are indicative of "major media." For the same 
reasons, we cannot conclude that the article entitled '----------------------'

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement of a 
"major, international recognized award," or evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). As a result, we do not need to provide the type of final merits determination 
discussed in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record 
in the aggregate and conclude that it does not demonstrate that the Petitioner has established the 
acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
oftheir respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing toward that goal. See Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. at 954 ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the 
statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability designation 
is "extremely restrictive by design,"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very 
small percentage of prospective immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), 
No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 
(upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian 
theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals 
fail to win this designation")). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is otherwise consistent with a "career of acclaimed 
8 
work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also 
section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that they are one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1 )(A) and 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
