Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Exceptional Ability) 
The Petitioner, a cybersecurity engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that although the record establishes that the Petitioner satisfied 
at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required, the totality of the evidence did not show, 
in a final merits determination, that the Petitioner has received sustained national or international 
acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinaiy ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate recognition 
ofhis or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

As an initial matter, we consider the Petitioner's claim that SCOPS erred by "re-litigating" the initial 
regulatory criteria in its final merits determination and reversing its conclusions about those criteria. 
The Petitioner claims that, despite accepting four criteria as established, SCOPS then improperly 
imposed new requirements to dismiss these criteria in the final merits determination. For example, 
the Petitioner objects to SCOPS stating, in its final merits determination, that although the evidence 
shows that the Petitioner authored scholarly articles in the field, that the evidence does not show that 
he has "consistently" published in journals "with the highest impact factors" or in publications "which 
enjoy high circulation among national or international readers" or that the articles "advanced the field 
and set the [Petitioner] apart." The Petitioner asserts that this is in error because the regulatory criteria 
does not include these additional requirements, and that SCOPS therefore imposed novel substantive 
requirements beyond those in the regulation at step two. The Petitioner claims that SCOPS made the 
same error as it relates to judging the work of others and having a leading or critical role in an 
organization with a distinguished reputation. 
The Petitioner's claim relies on a mischaracterization ofKazarian and USCIS policy. In determining 
whether a petitioner has established extraordinary ability, we use a two-step analysis to evaluate the 
evidence. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.5 As described in the USCIS Policy Manual, 
the evidence evaluated in the first step analysis is also reviewed in the second step, where the officer 
must determine whether the person is one of that small percentage of the who has risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor, and that the person has sustained national or international acclaim.6 
Objectively meeting the regulatory criteria in the first step does not establish that the person in fact 
meets the requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability. 7 
Therefore, SCOPS' analysis, in a final merits determination, as to whether the evidence-including 
evidence that demonstrates initial regulatory criteria-shows that the Petitioner meets the 
requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability, is a proper application of this 
framework. In fact, the USCIS Policy Manual provides the following example that relates to 
publishing scholarly articles: 
Publishing scholarly articles in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media alone, regardless of the caliber, should satisfy the regulatory criteria in the first 
step of the analysis. However, the second step requires the officer to evaluate the 
person's publications to determine whether they were indicative of the person being 
one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and 
enjoying sustained national or international acclaim. 8 
This analysis is not, as the Petitioner claims, "re-litigating" the regulatory criteria of the first step in 
the second step's final merits determination. Rather, this analysis reflects the proper analysis in which 
the officer, at step one, determines only if the evidence objectively meets the regulatory criteria and, 
at step two, considers whether the evidence in its totality demonstrates sustained national or 
international acclaim.

The Petitioner also asserts on appeal that SCOPS erred by ignoring substantial evidence in the record, 
that SCOPS focused only on the weakest evidence in the record to determine that the Petitioner did 
not establish eligibility, and that SCOPS erred in how it considered sustained acclaim in the national 
security context. Overall, the Petitioner contends that the record establishes that he has risen to the 
top of the field. 
However, in considering the totality of the evidence, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his 
achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. 
1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 6739. Furthermore, the Petitioner has not shown that he has risen to that 
small percentage at the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The commentary 
for Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this 
regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required for 
lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704. For the reasons discussed below, we conclude that 
the Petitioner has not established that he enjoys a career that meets this very high standard. See Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386-87 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that the narrow definition reflects the 
stringency of the extraordinary ability standard). 
First, regarding the Petitioner's membership in associations, the Petitioner does not contest on appeal 
SCOPS' finding that the evidence is insufficient to establish this initial regulatory criterion. 9 In 
considering this evidence in a final merits determination, we conclude that the evidence related to the 
Petitioner's memberships is not reflective of garnering sustained national or international acclaim. 
The record includes evidence that the Petitioner is a senior member of the Institute of Electrical and 
Electronics Engineers (IEEE). The Petitioner provided background information about IEEE and the 
qualifications needed to be a senior member. The evidence shows that senior membership requires at 
least five years of"significant performance" in the field, as indicated by achievements or contributions 
such as publishing books or papers, developing courses, or acting as a team lead or engineer in charge. 
However, the Petitioner has not explained how these qualifications are reflective of acclaim consistent 
with this highly restrictive classification. See, e.g., Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 820 (E.D. 
Pa. 2025) (upholding that the petitioner's IEEE membership did not show extraordinary ability 
because the eligibility requirements were not stringent enough and IEEE senior membership did not 
reflect sustained national or international acclaim).

eligibility for the benefit sought. 16 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing

major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an

international acclaim or that it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more 
accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he 
is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).
