Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a product designer in the field of financial services, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 
1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien bas extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories of evidence listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
In the letter 
the Petitioner initially filed in support of the petition, she asserted she is "one of the top 
Product and [User Interface (UI)/User Experience (UX)] Designers both in the United States and 
across nations." Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, 
internationally recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed five criteria relating to memberships, 
judging, original contributions, leading or critical role, and high salary. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), 
(iv), (v), (viii), (ix). USCIS determined the Petitioner established two ofthe regulatory criteria relating 
to judging and leading or critical role. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (viii). Because USCIS granted 
only two of the claimed initial criteria, USCIS did not undertake a final merits determination. On 
appeal, the Petitioner maintains she meets the evidentiary criteria relating to memberships, original 
contributions, and high salary. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (v), (ix). For the reasons discussed 
below, the Petitioner does not demonstrate she meets at least three categories of evidence. 1 
A. Memberships 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts

The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based on her certified membership with the 
Association of Registered Graphic Designers (RGD), as well as full membership with the Chartered

of membership, as well as a letter from the RGD president. In the letter, the RGD president states that 
"RGD Certified Membership is not broadly attainable and is reserved for designers who have reached 
a senior, high-impact level of professional practice." She explains that the selection criteria includes: 
(1) at least seven years of combined relevant education and professional design experience; (2) a 
portfolio submission of professional case studies; (3) a professional knowledge assessment; (4) an 
evaluator-led portfolio review; and (5) a consensus-based certification decision by experienced RGD 
certified evaluators. The Petitioner also submitted a printout from the RGD website listing the 
certification evaluators, which does not include the evaluators complete profiles. The Petitioner, 
however, has not shown that the requirements for certified RGD membership reflect outstanding 
achievements. For instance, the Petitioner does not establish that accumulating years of combined 
education and experience or the submission of a portfolio of case studies represents outstanding 
achievements in the field. The Petitioner also does not provide the criteria for the portfolio review by 
the evaluators or any information on the professional knowledge assessment. While the Petitioner 
highlights that the RGD president states that certified RGD membership is "RGD's "highest 
professional credential and is "awarded only to designers who have demonstrated outstanding 
achievement, leadership, and sustained professional impact over the course of their careers," the RGD 
president's letter is not accompanied by corroborating documentation and is not probative. 
Regarding CSD, the Petitioner also provided a congratulatory email showing that she has full 
membership with CSD. In addition, the Petitioner submitted information about CSD including its 
membership criteria and bylaws. According to the submitted documentation, full membership in CSD 
requires: (1) over three years of experience in design; (2) a portfolio submission of case studies; (3) 
evidence of commitment to professional practice; and (4) an assessment by CSD specialists. In 
addition, the Petitioner submitted background information for seven CSD members, along with the 
CSD Genetic Matrix "which was devised following years of research by the Society and others as to 
what constitutes a 'designer'." Here, the Petitioner also has not established that the requirements for 
full CSD membership require outstanding achievements. The Petitioner does not show that 
accumulating three years of experience in design or the submission of a portfolio of case studies 
represents outstanding achievements. And while the Petitioner submitted the CSD Genetic Matrix, 
she does not explain how meeting the four competences - creativity, professionalism, skills, and 
knowledge - establishes outstanding achievements. 
Without more, the Petitioner has not established that certified RGD membership or full CSD 
membership requires outstanding achievements. See Kumar v. Dir. of US. Citizenship & Immigr. 
Servs., No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding plaintiff's 
documentation did not reflect that membership requires outstanding achievements).

impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level ofmajor significance. See Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an original contribution may include, 
but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l).

C. High Salary 
The criterion relating to high salary requires "evidence that the alien 
has commanded a high salary or 
other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the 
field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). A credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an 
alien "has commanded" such salary or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax 
returns and pay statements. Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's 
occupation and location should be submitted to establish that his or her compensation is high relative 
to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner stated on the Form I-140 that her job title is "lead product designer" and that her position 
corresponds to the U.S. Department of Labor's standard occupational classification for web and 
digital interface designers. The Petitioner provided a copy of her 2024 Form W-2 Wage & Tax 
Statement indicating that she was paid $166,488 a year. In addition, she submitted a letter of 
employment stating that she has been the lead product designer for smce 
March 2025 and that her salary is $185,000 a year. 
In support of this criterion, the Petitioner submitted an excerpt from the U.S. Bureau of Labor 
Statistics' Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics which states the 90th percentile salary for 
web and digital interface designers in Florida for May 2024. In addition, the Petitioner submitted 
salary information for lead product design positions from ZipRecruiter.com, Salary.com, and 
Indeed.com, which show the salaries for the top earners. But the data in these wage surveys do not 
indicate it was for the Petitioner's field of financial services. As for the Glassdoor.com salary 
information, it shows that lead product design positions in the field of financial services earn "$124 K 
- $165K/yr Base pay" and that the total pay range is "$153K - $219K/yr." Notably, the high end of 
the salary range is $219,000, which is more than $30,000 of the Petitioner's salary. The evidence 
therefore does not establish that she receives total remuneration that is "significantly high." 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not established by a preponderance of the evidence that she meets this 
criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
6 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final 
merits determination in this matter. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability.
