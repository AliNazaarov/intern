Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a financial risk specialist who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did he 
demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility to 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met six of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and original 
contributions but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes or awards, membership, 
published material, or performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that 
he meets the previously claimed evidentiary criteria except for prizes or awards. Because he no longer 
claims that criterion on appeal, it is waived. Matter ofR-B-E-, 29 I&N Dec. 499,499 n.1 (BIA 2026) 
( citing Matter ofP-B-B-, 28 I&N Dec. 43, 44 n.1 (BIA 2020); Matter ofN-A-I-, 27 I&N Dec. 72, 73 
n. l (BIA 2017), aff'd sub nom., Ali v. Barr, 951 F.3d 275 (5th Cir. 2020)). After reviewing all the 
evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not met at least three criteria. 
l. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision in a statute is to be 
given effect and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another 
provision or to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the 
Petitioner's work has significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must 
have already been realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must 
submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. 
SCOPS granted this criterion based on the Petitioner's initial filing claims and evidence and they did 
not offer an explanation of how his evidence satisfied the requirements. A review of the material 
reveals the Petitioner made several claims of how his contributions that may have been original were 
of major significance in the field. But the material he offered in support of those claims primarily 
2 
consisted of evidence demonstrating the originality of the contributions that also reflected he was 
given credit for it, and contractual documents showing various entities utilized his work. What is 
absent from the record is documentation establishing that his original work impacted the field and that 
any of those contributions were of major significance. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v); see generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policyrnanual. The Petitioner doesn't 
identify any support letters or media attention that shows his original contributions significantly 
impacted the field. Because we are unable to assess SCOPS' reasoning for their favorable finding on 
this issue, we cannot evaluate whether their assessment was correct and we withdraw their 
determination that this criterion was satisfied. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that his contributions have had a sufficiently appreciable 
impact in the field, and as a result he has not met this criterion's requirements. 
2. "Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work 
of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
This criterion requires not only that the Petitioner was selected to serve as a judge, but also that he is 
able to produce evidence that he actually participated as a judge. Additionally, these duties must have 
been directly judging the work of others in the same or an allied field in which the Petitioner seeks an 
immigrant classification within the present petition. The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying 
all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
Similar to the previous requirement, we do not agree with the determination that the Petitioner has 
satisfied this criterion and we will withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination. 
The Petitioner claimed that he judged one event involving university students in a regional 
competition. The Petitioner does not adequately explain how judging the performance of 
undergraduate students constitutes judging "the work of others in the same or an allied field." 
Undergraduates are not professionals and are not yet "in the field" as this criterion requires. See Sarin 
v. United States, No. 2:22-CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. July 27, 2023) 
( upholding the determination that a petitioner's judging of students was not in the field of acting or in 
an allied field of acting). In fact, material from the organization the Petitioner provided for the record 
reflects the competition is meant "to instill high standards of ethical concepts in the next generation of 
investment professionals, helping them prepare for successful careers in the future." 
Contrast the Petitioner's performance claimed here with serving as a member of a Ph.D. dissertation 
committee that makes the final judgment as to whether a candidate's body of work satisfies the 
requirements for a doctoral degree, as evidenced by departmental records could serve as qualifying 
evidence under this criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)( 1 ). The possibility 
exists for serving on such a dissertation committee to qualify under this criterion's requirements, as 
Ph.D. candidates are often considered working professionals in their field-particularly in certain 
areas-and they are actively contributing to academic and professional knowledge in their discipline. 
Nevertheless, the Petitioner failed to document for the record that his performance for the I

3. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that he is a member of an association in he field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 
2780815, at *3-4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner claimed two memberships, neither of which meets this criterion's requirements. The 
first is the Chartered Alternative Investment Analyst Association that has three types of members. 
Affiliate members must pass an exam but do not yet meet the specific education or work experience 
requirements for full membership. Full members must hold a bachelor's degree with one year of 
relevant professional experience, or alternatively, possess four years of professional experience in a 
related financial field. And retired members are former full members who are no longer employed 
full-time and can revert to full member status if they return to the workforce. Based on this 
organization's described criteria, these memberships are granted for passing exams and meeting 
baseline education or experience requirements rather than for outstanding achievements. It also falls 
short as the Petitioner has not established that this organization utilizes nationally or internationally 
recognized experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the 
achievements are outstanding. 
The second membership falls under the CF A that has two levels of membership, regular and affiliate. 
But both fall short in the same areas as the previous group as its regular membership requires passing 
an exam, possessing a bachelor's degree or an equivalent, and completing 4,000 hours of qualifying 
professional work experience over at least 36 months. And affiliate membership is for those who do 
not yet meet the exam or experience requirements. So, neither requires outstanding achievements, and 
the record lacks evidence demonstrating the CF A utilizes nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements ofthis criterion.

B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that the Petitioner has not satisfied any of the claimed criteria we discussed in this 
decision. While he argues and submits evidence for two additional criteria on appeal relating to 
published material at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and performing in a leading or critical role at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii), it is unnecessary that we make a decision on these additional grounds because he 
cannot numerically meet the required number of criteria. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial 
evidentiary requirement ofthree criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the remaining issues. 
Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) 
(finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary 
to the ultimate decision)); see also Matter ofF01joe, 29 I&N Dec. 463,475 n.6 (BIA 2026) (declining 
to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). It is also unnecessary 
that we provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(l )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). The 
two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rnle that does not violate the Administrative Procedure 
Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 
2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 
3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 
(2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the congressional record, the 
governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme intended to ensure only those 
at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other federal court has offered an 
alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough analysis of the legislative and 
agency history. Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in 
this matter. Patel, 596 U.S. at 332. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
