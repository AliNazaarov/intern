Appeal of Texas Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner - an athlete, referee, and coach in the sports of horseback archery and polo - seeks 
classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an 
alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this 
category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document 
recognition of their accomplishments in their fields. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition. The Director concluded that the 
Petitioner did not demonstrate that her U.S. entry would substantially benefit the country or her 
claimed extraordinary ability in her field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Director did not 
consider the evidence in its totality and introduced requirements unstated in regulations or policy. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that she established substantial benefits of her proposed U.S. entry and met the 
evidentiary criterion regarding receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for 
excellence in her field. But, because she did not satisfy this visa category's minimum number of 
evidentiary requirements, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country. 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 1 
Ifa petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the other statutory requirements, USCIS must then 
make a final merits determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a 
petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them 
among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th 
Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing 
statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. Substantial Benefit to the United States 
An alien with extraordinary ability must "substantially benefit prospectively the United States." 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) of the Act. Neither the Act nor regulations define the phrase "substantially 
benefit." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(3). But U.S. immigration authorities have 
interpreted it broadly. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 956 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding 
that, "given the enormous popularity of golf in this country," a successful professional golfer's 
immigration would substantially benefit the United States in the future). 
The Director's request for additional evidence (RFE) asked the Petitioner for "objective documentary 
evidence demonstrating that [her] entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States." 
( emphasis in original). The Director found counsel's arguments in the RFE response insufficient 
because an attorney's unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 
22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("[S]tatements in a brief, motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence 
and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight.")

2. Published Material about the Petitioner 
As previously indicated, to meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[p ]ublished material about 
the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work 
in the field for which classification is sought. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include 
the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." Id. 
USCIS first determines whether published material relates to a petitioner and their specific work in 
their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. USCIS will consider 
published material covering a broader topic if the material includes a "substantial discussion" of a 
petitioner's work in their field and mentions them in relation to the work. Id. 
USCTS next determines whether a publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or other major media. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. 
Relevant factors include, for professional and major trade publications, the intended audience and, for 
major trade publications and other major media, the relative circulation, readership, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of six published interviews with her between 2014 and 2023 and three 
2011 articles about her in the Iranian sports newspaper. The Director agreed that the material relates 
4 
to the Petitioner and her work in her field. But the Director found that the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate that the material appeared in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Director overlooked evidence that a newspaper that 
published two interviews with her - one in December 2017 and one in January 2018 - is a major 
medium in Iran. She states that the government-owned, daily newspaper "is recognized as one of the 
top three to five media outlets in Iran, which underscores its significance as a major publication." 
The Petitioner submitted a July 2024 printout from an online visibility management platform with 
information about the newspaper. The Director, however, discounted the evidence "because the article 
does not list the author, and the circulation data is not during the same timeframe as the publication of 
the article." 
As previously indicated, this evidentiary criterion requires published material to include an author's 
name. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) ("Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation.") The newspaper's 2018 published interview with the 
Petitioner lacks the author's name. But the 2017 published interview names the "journalist" who 
questioned the Petitioner. The Director therefore improperly discounted the 2017 interview for lack 
of the author's name. 
The Director found the July 2024 online data about the newspaper insufficient because it did not 
necessarily reflect the paper's circulation at the time it published the Petitioner's interview in 
December 2017. USCIS determines whether a publication qualifies as a "major" medium based on 
its relative circulation. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. The Director therefore 
properly required more accurate information regarding the newspaper's relative circulation at the time 
of the interview, more than six years before publication of the online data. 
The Petitioner submitted a copy of an online article indicating that - in January 2018, just a month 
after the newspaper's first published interview with her - Iran's general directorate of internal press 
and news agencies ranked the newspaper among the top eight in the country. But the information does 
not demonstrate that the directorate based its rankings on relative circulation, the sole factor in USCIS' 
determination of a major medium. The directorate stated that the ranking is "aimed at enhancing the 
quality ofcontent and format ofthe country's newspapers." The directorate said that "[t]he Qualitative 
Evaluation Council, in 5 sessions, provided the necessary scores on the qualitative components listed 
in the table ofindices." Then, after receiving objections from newspapers, the directorate held "review 
sessions" and made amendments before finalizing the rankings. The record does not include the 
indices table. Thus, we do not know whether the "qualitative components" that the council considered 
included relative circulation. The record also does not show whether the newspapers' relative 
circulation otherwise affected their rankings. Because the January 2018 newspaper rankings may 
disregard or give little weight to the publications' relative circulation, the rankings do not establish the 
newspaper as a major medium for purposes of this evidentiary criterion. 
The Director also found insufficient evidence that the sports newspaper that published the 2011 articles 
about the Petitioner qualifies as a major medium. The Petitioner, however, contends that the Director 
5 
disregarded evidence that the newspaper constitutes a "professional" publication. She argues that she 
submitted evidence that the publication "specifically targets sports professionals and enthusiasts." 
As previously indicated, USCTS determines professional and major trade publications based on their 
intended audiences. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3. The Petitioner 
submitted copies of pages in the sports newspaper and online information indicating its exclusive 
publication of sports articles and photographs. We therefore agree that the newspaper targets sports 
workers and enthusiasts. 
But the definitions ofthe term "profession" in the Act and regulations inform our determination of the 
target audience of a "professional" publication. The term "profession" includes architects, engineers, 
lawyers, physicians, surgeons, and certain teachers. Section 10l(a)(32) of the Act. The term also 
encompasses "any occupation for which a United States baccalaureate degree or its foreign equivalent 
is the minimum requirement for entry in the occupation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(k)(2). Sports workers and 
enthusiasts are not among the professions listed in section 10l(a)(32) ofthe Act, nor do those activities 
require at least bachelor's degrees or their equivalents. The Petitioner therefore has not established 
the sports newspaper as a professional publication. Also, professional and major trade publications 
"are read by people in a particular profession or trade, rather than the population at large." Braga v. 
Poulos, No. CV 06-5105 SJO FMOX, 2007 WL 9229758, at *6 (C.D. Cal. July 6, 2007, ajf'd, 317 F. 
App'x 680 (9th Cir. 2009). Sports enthusiasts include many members of the population at large. For 
this additional reason, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the sports newspaper constitutes a 
professional publication. 
The Petitioner also contends that the Director overlooked an Iranian news agency's status as a major 
medium. The news agency published an on line interview with the Petitioner in 2019. She states that, 
when considering the agency as a major medium, "USCIS selectively focused on a single, 
supplemental reference to Wikipedia while ignoring multiple authoritative reports from reliable 
sources." See, e.g., United States v. Lawson, 677 F.3d 629, 650 (4th Cir. 2012) ("Given the open­
access nature of Wikipedia, the danger in relying on a Wikipedia entry is obvious and real.") 
As the Petitioner contends, the decision indicates that, in finding insufficient evidence that the news 
agency constitutes a major medium, the Director solely relied on the agency's Wikipedia entry. But 
the Petitioner's remaining evidence does not establish the agency as a major medium. The Petitioner 
submitted online documentation that the agency, according to one source, has the 31st most popular 
website in Iran and attracts almost one million daily visitors. This evidence, however, was published 
in 2024 and may not accurately reflect the viewership of the agency's website at the time it published 
the September 2019 interview with the Petitioner. She therefore has not established that the news 
agency was a major medium in Iran. 
For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner has not submitted published material in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field. 
3. The Remaining Criterion 
The Petitioner has met one of the evidentiary criteria but contends that she meets only one more. She 
therefore cannot satisfy the required minimum of three evidentiary criteria. See 8 C.F.R. 
6 
§ 204.5(h)(3). Thus, we need not reach and hereby reserve consideration of her argument that she 
submitted evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) of her performance in a leading or critical role 
for organizations with distinguished reputations. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to 
their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner demonstrated that her U.S. entry would substantially benefit the United States and met 
the evidentiary requirement regarding receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards 
for excellence in her field. But she has not satisfied the requested immigrant visa category's minimum 
amount of evidentiary criteria. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
