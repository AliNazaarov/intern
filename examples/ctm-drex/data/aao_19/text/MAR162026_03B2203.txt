Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a vaccine technology researcher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F .R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Here, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that her evidence 
reflects published material about her and her work. Rather, the documentation shows coverage of her 
work without any indication about her, besides simply being listed as one of the authors listed in the 
reference sections. USCIS determines whether the published material was related to the person and 
the person's specific work in the field for which classification is sought. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The published material should be about the person, relating to the 
person's work in the field. Id. See also Kinuthia v. Rosenberg, No. CV 17-10255-LTS, 2018 WL 
1243955, at *5 (D. Mass. Mar. 8, 2018), ajf'd sub nom., Kinuthia v. Velarde, No. 18-1244, 2019 WL 
11671989 (1st Cir. July 3, 2019), cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 2643, 206 L. Ed. 2d 714 (2020) (finding it 
is not enough that the material merely relates to the applicant's work); Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 
3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *3-4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) (upholding published material 
must be specifically about the applicant and focus on his personal work in the relevant field, not merely 
relate to the applicant's work, mention his name, or include his photo); Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. 
Ctr., United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001, at *3 (5th Cir. Feb. 
11, 2025) ( finding the plain language ofthe criterion requires the publication to be about the alien and 
his work in the field); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *4 (D. Neb. Mar. 
10, 2025) (finding the regulation and USCIS policy clearly intend for evidence under this criterion to 
say something substantive about the petitioner and her work). 
Because the Petitioner did not establish that her evidence reflects published material about her, we 
need not evaluate the other regulatory requirements for this criterion. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 
U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not show she fulfills this criterion.

On appeal, the Petitioner's brief contends: 
... JoVE is a well-known journal that spans various scientific disciplines, and the 
invitation was extended to [the Petitioner] because of her subject-matter knowledge. 
The title or subject of the specific paper was likely within an allied biomedical field. 
Even if the email did not spell out the article's topic, it is a reasonable inference that a

We are not persuaded by the Petitioner's assertions. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) 
requires "[ e ]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the 
work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought." The 
petitioner must show that the person has not only been invited to judge the work of others, but also 
that the person actually participated in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field 
of specialization. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). 
Here, the Petitioner makes speculations and assumptions without supporting the record. The Petitioner 
did not demonstrate that JoVE "spans various scientific disciplines," "[t]he title or subject of the 
specific paper was likely within an allied biomedical field," "it is a reasonable inference that a vaccine 
technology expert would be asked to review work in immunology, biotechnology, or related fields," 
and "USCIS has previously recognized that fields like immunologyj Iand public health 
are allied in the context ofvaccine research." The Petitioner does not point to submitted evidence, nor 
does the record contain documentation corroborating any of the Petitioner's assertions. Counsel's 
unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 
(BIA 1998) ("statements in a brief: motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not 
entitled to any evidentiary weight"); MRC Energy Co. v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 3:19-
5 
CV-2003-K, 2021 WL 1209188, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Mar. 31, 2021) (finding the plaintiff's burden to 
identify specific items in the record to support its position). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. In this case, the Petitioner must show that she participated "as a 
judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field." At initial filing, the Petitioner provided 
emails requesting and thanking her for the review of an article entitled, "Characterization of 
Mycobacterium tuberculosis protein Rv3444c by ChIP-seq" for JoVE. Thus, in order to demonstrate 
eligibility for this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that her review of the article was either in the 
same or an allied field of her specialization, vaccine technology/research. However, she did not do 
so. The Petitioner did not show that the article involved vaccine technology/research or is an allied 
field. Furthermore, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that Jo VE covers her particular field or an allied 
field. See Sarin v. United States, No. 2:22-CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. 
July 27, 2023) (upholding plaintiff's judging of an intra-school personality contest was not in the field 
of acting or in an allied field of acting). 
Without farther information or documentation, the Petitioner did not establish that she participated as 
a judge in her field or an allied field. We are also not persuaded that "any research she was selected 
to review would be allied to her field." The Petitioner did not demonstrate that simply reviewing any 
type of an article for any type of a journal would automatically show eligibility as an allied field, nor 
did she show that any research is allied to her field. Finally, as correctly determined by SCOPS, the 
additional review articles submitted in the RFE response occurred after the initial filing ofthe petition. 
An applicant or petitioner must establish that he or she remains eligible for the benefit sought from 
the time of filing and continuing through adjudication. 8 C.F .R. § 103 .2(b )( 1 ). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner did not demonstrate she satisfies this criterion. 
D. Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes major, 
significant contributions in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner's brief argues: 
Publication and Citation Record: At the time of filing, [the Petitioner's] work had 
accrued 114 citations in the scientific literature. In just the few months between filing 
and the RFE response, that count rose to 127 (though USCIS did not credit the post­
filing increase). 
Even 114 citations, especially in the life sciences within a short span of years is 
substantial - it far exceeds the citation counts of many peers at her career stage. More 
importantly, it is evidence that number independent researchers have found her work 
important enough to cite in their own publications. Citations are a quantitative proxy 
for significance: her ideas are propagating through the field. For context, one of her 
6 
TB vaccine papers was cited by researchers in at least 10 different countries' institutes, 
demonstrating international impact. 
... 114 citations is a strong indicator of significant impact, especially given that [the 
Petitioner's] publication history only spans the past few years (her earliest papers are 
from around 2018). By comparison, many extraordinary ability cases have been 
approved with similar or even lower citation counts when accompanied by 
corroborating evidence. What matters is not an absolute number but the context: Here, 
clearly yes - over a hundred instances of other scientists citing her is proof of that. ... 
The Petitioner's brief makes numerous assertions without referencing or pointing to evidence that 
supports its claims, such as "[ e ]ven 114 citations, especially in the life sciences within a short span of 
years is substantial," "it far exceeds the citation counts of many peers at her career stage," "one ofher 
TB vaccine papers was cited by researchers in at least 10 different countries' institutes," "114 citations 
is a strong indicator of significant impact," and "many extraordinary ability cases have been approved 
with similar or even lower citation counts when accompanied by corroborating evidence." Counsel's 
unsubstantiated assertions do not constitute evidence. See S-M-, 22 I&N Dec. at 51; see also MRC 
Energy Co., 2021 WL 1209188, at *6. 
Moreover, publications and presentations are not sufficient under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent 
evidence that they were of "major significance." See Kazarian v. USCIS, 580 F.3d 1030, 1036 (9th 
Cir. 2009), ajf'd in part, 596 F.3d 1115. Evidence that the person's work was funded, patented, or 
published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily establish, on its 
own, that the work is of major significance in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l). Here, as discussed further below, the Petitioner has not shown that the field views any 
of her publications or conferences and conferences as original contributions of major significance in 
the field. 
This criterion requires a petitioner to establish original contributions ofmajor significance in the field. 
Thus, the burden is on the petitioner to identify the original contributions and explain how or why they 
are considered to be of major significance in the field. Here, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how 
her cumulative number of citations represents contributions of major significance in the field. 
Moreover, aggregate citation figures tend to reflect a petitioner's overall publication record rather than 
identifying which research or findings the field considers to be majorly significant. An example that 
published research provoking widespread commentary on its importance from others working in the 
field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' work in that field, may be 
probative ofthe significance ofthe person's contributions to the field ofendeavor. See 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2)

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the roles criterion (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)), 
we need not reach this ground because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement 
of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 
2023), aff'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Trump, No. 23-2029, 2025 WL 3491324 (1st Cir. Aug. 12, 2025) 
( declining to consider a criterion when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we 
decline to reach and hereby reserve this issue. See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.
