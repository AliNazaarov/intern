Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a technical program manager, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens who can demonstrate 
their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us 
on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' decision is 
based on procedural errors, a failure to apply controlling policy, and an arbitrary disregard for evidence 
provided. The Petitioner maintains that his evidence supports eligibility for the EB-1 classification. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) ("EB-1 
petitions, nicknamed Einstein visas, are coveted visas reserved for only the most talented noncitizens 
with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
sustained acclaim and recognition of the beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement, that is, a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit this 
evidence, then they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items, such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence 
to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

1. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the association for which he claims 
present or past membership requires that members have outstanding achievements in the field, as 
judged by recognized experts in that field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Associations may have multiple levels of membership, and the 
level of membership afforded to the person must show that to obtain that level of membership, 
recognized national or international experts judged the person as having attained outstanding 
achievements in the field for which classification is sought. Id. Relevant factors that may lead to a 
conclusion that the person's membership in the associations was not based on outstanding 
achievements in the field include instances where the person's membership was based on a level of 
education or years of experience in a particular field, the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an 
association's publication, or a requirement for employment in certain occupation, as commonly seen 
with union membership or guild affiliation for actors. Id.

3. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
To satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that he has made original contributions in the 
field and that the original contributions are of major significance to the field. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's 
original work constitutes major, significant contributions to the field. Id. Evidence that the person's 
work was funded, patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will 
not necessarily establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance to the field. Id. For 
example, published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others 
working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' work in the 
field, may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of endeavor. Id. 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contributions may provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of major 
significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed 
significance. Id.

Regarding the Petitioner's publication and presentation of his work at the I !conference, the 
Petitioner did not sufficiently demonstrate that this evidence shows original contributions of major 
significance in the field. According to the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), an alien's 
contribution must be not only original but also of major significance. To be considered a contribution

have reproduced or otherwise applied the petitioner's results. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual,

that discuss in-depth the Petitioner's findings or credit the Petitioner with influencing or impacting the

conference presentation is frequently cited by other researchers or technical program managers, has 
significantly impacted the field, or otherwise rises to the level of contributions of major significance 
in the field. While presentation of the Petitioner's work demonstrates that his findings were shared

presentation, the Petitioner has not established the impact or influence of his presentation once 
disseminated in the field. Therefore, the Petitioner's documentary evidence is not reflective of having 
a significant impact on the field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
With regard to letters from various individuals, the opinions of the Petitioner's references are not 
without weight and have been considered. We may, in our discretion, use advisory opinions statements 
submitted as expert testimony. See Matter of Caron International, 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 
1988). However, we are ultimately responsible for making the final determination regarding an alien's 
6 
eligibility for the benefit sought. Id. The submission of reference letters supporting the petition is not 
presumptive evidence of eligibility; we may evaluate the content of those letters as to whether they 
support the alien's eligibility. See id. at 795-796; see also Matter ofV-K-, 24 I&N Dec. 500, n.2 (BIA 
2008) (noting that expert opinion testimony does not purport to be evidence as to "fact"). Thus, the 
content of the references' statements and how they became aware of the petitioner's reputation are 
important considerations. Even when written by independent experts, letters solicited by an alien in 
support of an immigration petition are of less weight than preexisting, independent evidence that one 
would expect of a technical program manager or an engineer who has made original contributions of 
major significance in the field. Cf Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d at 135 (concluding that USCIS' 
decision to give little weight to uncorroborated assertions from professionals in the field was not arbitrary 
and capricious).

In order to fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must first show that he has performed in a leading or 
critical role for an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). For a leading role, we look at whether the evidence establishes that the Petitioner was a 
leader within the organization or establishment or a division or department thereof Id. For a critical 
role, we look at whether the evidence establishes that the Petitioner has contributed in a way that is of 
significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a 
division or department of the organization or establishment. Id. 
Second, the Petitioner must establish that the organization or establishment for which he held a leading 
or critical role has a distinguished reputation. Id. The relevant size or longevity of an organization or 
establishment is not a determining factor but is considered together with other information to 
determine whether a distinguished reputation exists. Id. Other relevant factors for evaluating the 
reputation of an organization or establishment include the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage. Id. USCIS Policy Manual interprets "distinguished" as "marked by eminence, distinction, 
or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person" based on the definition of"distinguished" in Merriam­
Webster's online dictionary. Id.

The record lacks position appropriate compensation surveys, organization justifications to pay above

B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a major, one-time achievement 
or documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not 
automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954. Here, 
the Petitioner has not shown the significance ofhis work is indicative ofthe required sustained national 
or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(1 )(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered 
national or international acclaim in his field and that he is one of the small percentage who has risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons we have discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
