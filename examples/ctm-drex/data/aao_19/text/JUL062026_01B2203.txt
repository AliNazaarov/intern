Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS) USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de 
novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we 
will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met six of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied the criterion relating to judging but that he had not satisfied 
the criteria associated with membership, original contributions, the authorship of scholarly articles, 
performing in a leading or critical role, or commanding a high salary or remuneration. On appeal, the 
Petitioner maintains that he meets each of those evidentiary criteria. After reviewing all the evidence 
in the record, we conclude he has not satisfied at least three criteria. 
l. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that they are a member of an association "in the field for which classification is 
sought." Next, the petitioner must demonstrate both of the following: (1) that the associations utilize 
nationally or internationally recognized experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to 
determine if the achievements are outstanding, and (2) that the associations use this outstanding 
determination as a condition of eligibility for prospective membership. It is insufficient for the 
association itself to determine if the achievements were outstanding, unless nationally or 
internationally recognized experts in the Petitioner's field who represent the association render this 
determination. It is also insufficient for the Petitioner to indicate that they were admitted to the 
association because of their outstanding achievements; the Petitioner must show that the association 
requires outstanding achievements of all prospective members at the alien's level ofmembership. The 
2 
Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at *3-4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner argues that his fellowship level membership meets this criterion's requirements because 
that level is the organization's most selective, it is reserved for distinguished engineers, and 
membership admission decisions undergo a peer-review process by existing fellows. On that basis, 
the Petitioner alleges this fellowship is an association in the field that limits membership to those with 
outstanding achievements as judged by experts satisfying each ofthe required elements under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
Even though the Petitioner presumes that those judging membership admittance have demonstrated 
achievement in their field, that doesn't speak to whether those judges are nationally or internationally 
recognized which is explicitly mandated in this criterion's plain language. Although the Petitioner 
notes that experts perform the peer review judging for new member admittance, he has not established 
that the judges have achieved the requisite level of recognition. Without evidence reflecting such 
national or international recognition of the panel members, this organization cannot assist the 
petitioner in demonstrating that he has satisfied the requirements of this criterion. 
2. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The denial decision offers little analysis for its determination stating: "The totality of the record 
establishes that the petitioner has/had a leading, but not critical role at organizations or establishments 
of distinguished reputation." Here, the Petitioner has not met the requirements of this criterion. 
For a leading role, users evaluates whether the evidence establishes that the person is (or was) a 
leader within the organization or establishment or a division or department thereof. See generally 
6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. A title, with appropriate 
matching duties, can help to establish that a role is ( or was), in fact, leading. Id. And for a critical 
role, users looks at whether the evidence establishes that the person has contributed in a way that is 
of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a 
division or department of the organization or establishment. Id. 
As it relates to the distinguished reputation requirement, the Petitioner only claimed that the two 
companies themselves enjoy a distinguished reputation rather than for a department or division. So, 
he must demonstrate that his role was either leading or critical for the entire company at either entity. 
But he instead argued that he served in leadership positions central to the core architecture, privacy 
infrastructure, media systems, and artificial intelligence foundation of both companies. Absent is an 
explanation-either from him or from those submitting letters on his behalf-of how the role was 
leading or critical to the company as a whole. 
The letters discussed his role relating to systems and projects, but none detail how those systems or 
projects were critical to the company's success. Nor do they detail how his role on the systems or 
projects was considered as leading for the company as a whole. We therefore withdraw SCOPS 
statement that "the record establishes that the petitioner has/had a leading ... role at organizations or 
establishments of distinguished reputation." 
3 
So even though SCOPS did not grant this criterion, we do not agree with the wording they included in 
the denial, and we conclude the Petitioner has not satisfied this criterion for alternative reasons. 
3. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires evidence that the Petitioner has received a "high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field," supported 
by objective earnings data showing that the Beneficiary's pay exceeds that of individuals performing 
similar work during the same period. 
Courts and precedent confirm that the comparison must be to others in the same specific field and role, 
not to broader or mismatched occupational groups. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 955 (Assoc. 
Comm'r 1994); Skokos v. DHS, 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011); Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-
CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at *6 (M.D. Fla Sept. 30, 2025); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 
965,968 (N.D. Ill. 1996); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444--45 (N.D. Ill. 1995). 
USCIS policy similarly cautions that broad occupational groupings may not provide an accurate 
comparison, and courts have upheld USCIS in rejecting salary data drawn from related but distinct 
occupations or single-role positions to assess a multi-role position, and in requiring geographically 
appropriate comparators. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)( I). Average or median 
salary information for those performing work in a related but distinct occupation with different 
responsibilities is not a proper basis for comparison. Kapoor v. USCIS, 763 F. Supp. 3d 247, 258 
(E.D.N.Y. 2025); Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 9 (D. Mass. 2023); Mussarova v. Garland, 562 
F. Supp. 3d 837, 853 (C.D. Cal. 2022). Comparisons also must be in the same geographic area to be 
relevant. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l); Berardo v. USCIS, No. 3:19-CV-
01796-SB, 2020 WL 6161459, at *6 (D. Or. Oct. 20, 2020). 
Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed his total compensation package from two different companies, 
but on appeal he only claims one of those entities. A petitioner's claims before SCOPS that they do 
not advance on appeal are deemed waived. Matter of C-L-R-, 29 I&N Dec. 726, 728 (BIA 2026) 
(citing Matter of V-A-K-, 28 I&N Dec. 630, 630 n.2 (BIA 2022)). The Petitioner provided his base 
salary for his current employer that for 2025 was $280,000 and he included a projected bonus, a 
one-time signing bonus, and restricted stock units amounting to a much larger figure. SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. 
Initially, the Petitioner provided evidence comparing his salary to the Software Developers Standard 
Occupational Classificational (SOC) code, but in the request for evidence (RFE) response adjusted his 
claims to a more appropriate SOC code for Computer and Information Systems Managers. The 
Petitioner's base salary is approximately $5,500 above the Level IV wage rate listed for this SOC 
code, location, and timeframe. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational Employment and 
Wage Statistics survey assigns the wage levels within four categories: 
• Level 1 is the average of the lowest paid one-third in an occupation, or approximately the 17th 
percentile; 
4 
• Level 2 is approximately the 34th percentile; 
• Level 3 is approximately the 50th percentile, or the overall average wage for an occupational 
classification; and 
• Level 4 is the average of the highest-paid two-thirds, or approximately the 67th percentile. 
So, the Petitioner's compensation rests slightly above the Level IV wage rate around the 70th 
percentile, not above the 90th percentile as he alleges. We note the Petitioner's 90th percentile figure 
originated from O*NET under the incorrect SOC code for Software Developers and that is not a 
relevant comparison. 
First, the Petitioner relies on his total compensation package and claims that is high compared with 
the base salary of others performing similar work. But in doing so, he conflates the regulatory 
elements and only in a way that favors him. The regulation differentiates between a standard salary 
and other forms of remuneration, but the Petitioner did not stick to a salient comparison here. 
Comparing an alien's total compensation, which includes various bonuses and stock options, against 
even the upper percentiles of other's base salary is comparing apples-to-oranges. It artificially 
conflates guaranteed earnings with highly variable conditions that skew much higher and this creates 
an illogical, inflated, and fundamentally invalid benchmark of earnings. To make a legally sound 
comparison, one must measure like with like. Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 
5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 
2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding a petitioner must show that salary reports reflect an 
apples-to-apples, rather than an apples-to-oranges). 
So, instead of the invalid comparison the Petitioner presents, we will compare his base salary with the 
evidence he provided for others performing similar work during the same timeframe. On appeal, the 
Petitioner discusses the evidence he provided from levels.fyi showing his total compensation package 
was above the 90th percentile. The evidence holds diminished evidentiary value as portions of the 
website's content is illegible as if it was "whited out," and we are unable to determine the relevance 
that missing content. Additionally, the figure the Petitioner relies on for himself was as a new hire 
and that not only included his base salary, but also a signing bonus, an annual bonus, and restricted 
stock units. While the levels.fyi evidence included "a base [salary], stock and bonus," the Petitioner 
did not provide evidence to demonstrate what constituted the bonus for levels.fyi. and whether it also 
included a signing bonus factored into its 90th percentile figure. As a result, this does not appear to 
be an apples-to-apples comparison. See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, at 
*6. 
In summary, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of 
this criterion. 
4. "Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy through the 
submission of evidence. The first element is that the Petitioner is an author of scholarly articles in 
his field in which he intends to engage once admitted to the United States as a lawful permanent 
resident. We consider these articles to fall within two distinct areas. 
5 
One area is within the academic arena in which a scholarly article reports on original research, 
experimentation, or philosophical discourse. It is written by a researcher or expert in the field who is 
often affiliated with a college, university, or research institution. In general, it should have footnotes, 
endnotes, or a bibliography, and may include graphs, charts, videos, or pictures as illustrations of the 
concepts expressed in the article. The next area lies outside of the academic arena in which a 
scholarly article should be written for learned persons in that field. "Learned" is defined as "having 
or demonstrating profound knowledge or scholarship." Learned persons include all persons having 
profound knowledge of a field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
The second evidentiary element this criterion requires is that the scholarly articles appear in one ofthe 
following: (1) a professional publication, (2) a major trade publication, or (3) in a form of major 
media. Relevant factors a petitioner should demonstrate include the intended audience (for 
professional and major trade publications) and the relative circulation, readership, or viewership are 
high relative to similar publications (for major trade publications and other major media). See 
generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(B)( l ). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying 
each of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner claimed he served as the lead author on two articles that appeared on a blog posted on 
his previous employer's website. When he filed the petition, the Petitioner noted that his previous 
employer's confidentiality policy did not permit the authors of the content on the blog to display their 
name with the posting and that letters from former coworkers detailed that he contributed to the 
blogposts. SCOPS' RFE informed him that such letters were insufficient to meet his burden of proof 
under this criterion, but he did not provide probative evidence in his RFE response to demonstrate 
eligibility here. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion 
because the blogposts were not scholarly in nature and the evidence was insufficient to establish that 
he penned the biogs as they do not list his name as an author. 
Now on appeal, the Petitioner claims that SCOPS did not meaningfully engage with his explanations 
or properly assess them. But they did evaluate his claims and evidence in the RFE response and found 
them wanting. Simply because SCOPS did not treat his claims and evidence in the manner he wished 
does not mean they ignored them and committed an error. The final determination ofwhether evidence 
satisfies the requirements of a regulation or meets the burden of proof lies with USCIS. See Matter of 
Caron International, 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988) (finding that the appropriate entity to 
determine eligibility is USCIS); see also Matter of Skirball Cultural Ctr., 25 I&N Dec. 799, 806 
(2012). SCOPS is not required to interpret evidence in the manner a petitioner advocates. Matter of 
Landers, 29 I&N Dec. 240, 245 (BIA 2025); Matter ofG-C-I-, 29 I&N Dec. 176, 179-80 (BIA 2025); 
Matter ofM-D-C-V-, 28 I&N Dec. 18, 32 (BIA 2020); Matter ofJ-C-H-F-, 27 I&N Dec. 211, 217 
(BIA 2018); Matter ofZ-Z-O-, 26 I&N Dec. 586,592 (BIA 2015); Matter ofD-R-, 25 I&N Dec. 445, 
454-55 (BIA 2011) (explaining that triers of fact may make reasonable inferences from direct and 
circumstantial evidence in the record as a whole and are not required to accept a respondent's account 
where other plausible views of the evidence are supported by the record). 
Here, the Petitioner was unable to produce probative evidence that preponderantly satisfied this 
criterion's requirements. We do not need to look beyond the fact that the record does not establish 
that he authored the blogposts. It is therefore unnecessary that we delve into what type of publication 
6 
the material appeared in, or whether they were scholarly in nature and intended for learned persons in 
the field. Matter of Vizcaino Aybar, 29 I&N Dec. 736, 738 (BIA 2026) (finding it unnecessary to 
decide on other eligibility requirements when one issue is dispositive ofthe appeal). We also note that 
not all material found on internet sites, such as a blog, "may be equivalent to material with stringent 
vetting and high standards for publication." Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, No. 3: 18-CV-0 1200-H-AGS, 
2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019). 
In the end, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements of 
this criterion. 
B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner claims he meets at least three criteria, because he has only 
satisfied one criterion and his arguments fail on the remaining criteria discussed above, that means he 
cannot numerically meet the required number of at least three criteria and it is unnecessary for us to 
make a decision on any of his additional claims. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary 
requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve his additional claims or issues. 
Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) 
(finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary 
to the ultimate decision)); see also Matter of Vizcaino Aybar, 29 I&N Dec. at 738 (finding it 
unnecessary to decide on other grounds when one is dispositive of the appeal). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 0; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 
2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
7 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before and since the Mukherji court made it. 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. Patel, 
596 U.S. at 332. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
