Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, asystems engineer specializing in education technology, seeks classification as an alien 
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 
1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by apreponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de nova. Matter of Christo 's , Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 {D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is seeking to be classified as an alien of extraordinary ability based on his work as a 
systems engineer in the field of education technology. He intends to establish abusiness which would 
provide universities with an e-learning platform that integrates with academic and administrative tools. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, the issue to be addressed on appeal is whether the Petitioner satisfied at least three 
of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS determined that although 
the Petitioner met two of the claimed evidentiary criteria, leading or critical role at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) and high salary or other renumeration at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), he did 
not establish eligibility for the third claimed category, original contributions of major significance at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). On appeal, the Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion. Upon de nova 
review, the Petitioner has not established he meets the requirements for original contributions of major 
significance or otherwise satisfies at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria. 1 
For the first time on appeal, the Petitioner claims eligibility and submits new evidence for two 
additional criteria, membership in associations at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and participation as ajudge 
of others' work at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). The Petitioner did not assert eligibility for these two 
criteria either at the initial filing of the petition or in response to SCOP's request for evidence. Where 
a petitioner has been put on notice of a deficiency in the evidence and has been given an opportunity 
to respond to that deficiency prior to issuance of the adverse decision, new eligibility claims will not 
be considered on appeal or on motion. See Matter of Soriano, 19 l&N Dec. 764, 766 (BIA 1988). We 
will not consider this newly submitted evidence for the new criteria claims in our decision.

For the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), the Petitioner must submit evidence of his "original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
First, we determine that a petitioner's contributions in their field were original. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Next, we examine whether the 
original contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
The Petitioner claims his role in creating the Doctor CV platform demonstrates his original business­
related contributions of major significance in his field of education technology. A recommendation 
letter from the founder and CEO of Doctor CV describes Doctor CV as "an [artificial intelligence] 
platform that analyzes and improves users' resumes to improve their job opportunities." The Petitioner 
maintains his education technology strategies, his project management skills, and his ability to 
coordinate multidisciplinary teams were essential to the development of the platform. In support of 
the criterion, the Petitioner submitted the letter from Doctor CV's founder and CEO, as well as articles 
and website information about Doctor CV and grants received to develop the platform.2 
The evidence in the record, however, does not demonstrate the Petitioner's original contributions in 
his field, or that his contributions were of major significance in his field. The recommendation letter 
only provides general statements about the Petitioner's contributions, stating he was "instrumental in 
the initial stages of conception and development of the platform" between 2018 and 2019 and "[h]is 
mastery in the design and implementation of educational technology strategies, his experience in 
project management and his ability to coordinate multidisciplinary teams make him an ideal 
candidate." While letters explaining the nature and significance of a petitioner's contributions may be 
valuable to evaluating the claimed original contributions and significance, such letters should 
specifically describe the person's contribution and its significance to the field. Id. Here, the letter and 
other evidence in the record do not detail the Petitioner's original contributions in developing the 
platform, or his contribution's significance in his field of education technology. 
To establish the major significance of his contributions, the Petitioner focuses on Doctor CV having 
been recognized for its unique and innovative value to students and individuals seeking to improve 
their resumes and employment opportunities. The submitted articles about Doctor CV and the 
printouts from Doctor CV's website describe how Doctor CV's founder and CEO won two grants 
supported by the governments of Peru and Chile in order to develop the platform, and how the 
governments and others have used the platform. 
The articles and other documents in the record, however, do not discuss the Petitioner's original work 
or the significance of his original work. Instead, the evidence indicates that the Petitioner's 
contributions were limited to helping to develop Doctor CV, rather than to his field of education 
technology. A petitioner must show that their original contributions rise to the level of major

B. Summary and Reserved Issue 
Although SCOPS determined the Petitioner meets the criteria for leading or critical role at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) and high salary or other renumeration at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), the 
Petitioner has not established he meets the third claimed criterion relating to original contributions of 
major significance at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). Because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial 
evidentiary requirement for three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we need not provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Accordingly, we 
will reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not 
support a finding that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the 
classification sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. 
Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league 
level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) 
( finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't 
of Homeland Sec. (Hamal 11), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 
2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very 
small percentage of prospective immigrants"); See also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), 
No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non­
Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished 
individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding 
that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for the visa 
as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(1)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has garnered national 
or international acclaim in the field, and he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

Ill. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
