Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a teacher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F .R. 
§ 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

At the outset, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) relates to the authorship of scholarly articles 
rather than 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), claimed by the Petitioner above, which pertains to published 
material about the alien and the work in the field. Regardless, we are not persuaded by the Petitioner's 
arguments. The Petitioner did not demonstrate that meeting the judging and scholarly articles criteria 
automatically shows eligibility for the original contributions criterion. Each criterion contains 
distinctive regulatory requirements. As indicated, the regulation 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires 
"[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of 
others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought" and the 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media," while the 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence ofthe alien's original scientific, scholarly, 
artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
Furthermore, the original contributions criterion contains a qualitative element whereas the judging 
and scholarly articles do not. Thus, simply making original contributions in the field does not satisfy 
the criterion unless those contributions are of major significance. In contrast, there are no qualitative 
elements for the judging or scholarly articles criterion, such as judging majorly significant events or 
authoring majorly significant scholarly articles. Accordingly, we are not persuaded that "USCIS could 
not have approved authorship or judging without acknowledging that the field regards the petitioner's 
work as scientifically significant." Again, the significance of an alien's judging or authorship is not 
an issue for evaluating the evidentiary criteria but for an evaluation of the totality of the evidence 
under a final merits determination. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. For the same reasons, we are 
also not persuaded that judging the work of others or authoring scholarly articles automatically 
demonstrates the alien "has achieved recognition within the professional community for its originality, 
merit, and influence," as asserted by the Petitioner. 
The Petitioner also contends that his publications and "presentations at global conferences farther 
show that peers rely on his expertise - the clearest sign of ongoing professional validation." 
Publications and presentations are not sufficient under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that 
they were of"major significance." See Kazarian v. USCIS, 580 F.3d 1030, 1036 (9th Cir. 2009), aff'd 
in part, 596 F.3d 1115. Evidence that the person's work was fonded, patented, or published, while 
potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily establish, on its own, that the 
work is of major significance in the field. 4 Here, as discussed farther below, the Petitioner has not 
shown that the field views any of his publications or academic presentations and conferences as 
original contributions of major significance in the field.

C. Roles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
USCIS determines whether the person has performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, 
establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. For a leading role, 
USCIS looks at whether the evidence establishes that the person is ( or was) a leader within the 
organization or establishment or a division or department thereof. A title, with appropriate matching 
duties, can help to establish that a role is (or was), in fact, leading. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For a critical role, USCIS looks at whether the evidence establishes that 
the person has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization 
or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. 
Id.

D. Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field."9 As 
evidence of his salary, the Petitioner submitted the previously discussed letter from K-K- who claimed 
that "[the Petitioner's] monthly salary was approximately 1.600 USD based on the exchange rate at 
that time." However, the Petitioner did not offer any supporting evidence, such as paystubs, payroll 
records, or tax documentation to corroborate his monthly salary. Moreover, the letter does not reflect 
the Petitioner's specific foreign amount, nor does it indicate what foreign exchange was used and when 
the exchange rate was applied to show "at the time." As such, the Petitioner did not offer probative 
evidence to establish his monthly salary as the head of the physics department atc=J 
Furthermore, the Petitioner did not present sufficient comparable salary information to demonstrate 
that he commanded a high salary in relation to other university department heads. Instead, the 
Petitioner submitted average monthly salaries for secondary school teachers and principals in 
Kazakhstan and salaries of public employees in Kazakhstan, including school principals, deputy 
principals, and university teachers. The Petitioner did not compare his monthly salary to other 
university department heads rather than to secondary school teachers and principals and university 
teachers. See Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 955 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a 
professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); Skokos, 420 F. App'x at 713-14 (finding 
salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); 
Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus 
other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) ( comparing salary of 
NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not show he meets this criterion.

E. Comparable Evidence 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) states that "[i]f the above standards do not readily apply to 
the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the 
beneficiary's eligibility." This regulatory provision provides petitioners the opportunity to submit 
comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, ifit is determined that the evidentiary criteria 
described in the regulations do not readily apply to the person's occupation. 10 When evaluating such 
comparable evidence, officers must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to 
the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria 
listed in that regulation. 11 
The Petitioner argues that the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii), which requires "[e]vidence of 
the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases," does not readily apply 
to his occupation and his "oral presentation, publication, and invited participation at international 
conferences" should be considered as comparable evidence under the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). Although we agree that the display criterion does not apply to the occupation of an 
educator or teacher, we have already considered the evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). In fact, 
the Petitioner's brief specifically lists his "International Academic Presentations and Conference 
Participation Showcasing Pedagogical Innovation" under his original contribution argument. 
Moreover, the Petitioner's presentations may be more applicable for consideration as comparable 
evidence under the scholarly articles criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not demonstrate eligibility for comparable evidence under the 
display criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above, 
including through comparable evidence. Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of 
extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been 
recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied 
the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is 
an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits under the statute. See 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) 
(finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final 
merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) 
(holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision).
