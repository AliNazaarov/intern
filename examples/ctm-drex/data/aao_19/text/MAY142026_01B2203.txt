Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a digital marketing specialist, requests classification under the employment-based, 
first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). Successful 
petitioners in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and 
extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
two often initial evidentiary criteria - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that she satisfied four additional evidentiary requirements and demonstrated 
her extraordinary ability in her field. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we find that she satisfied the requisite number of evidentiary criteria. But, because we conclude 
that she has not established acclaim and recognition sufficient to merit classification as an alien with 
extraordinary ability, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (holding that USCIS' two-step analysis "is 
consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. The Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - her receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. She must therefore meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of her: 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in her field or an allied one; and 
• Authorship of a scholarly article in her field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi).

1. Awards 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this evidentiary requirement, USCIS first determines if a petitioner - as opposed 
to their employer or a company they own - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion One. USCIS next determines whether an award is for excellence in the 
relevant field and is nationally or internationally recognized. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that she won an ' award in 2021 
from a Russian music television channel. SCOPS, however, found insufficient evidence that the award 
was nationally or internationally recognized. 
In its decision, SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's submission of an expert opinion letter from a 
co-founder of an international music label and noted that the letter "describe[s] the award's selection 
process, jury composition, and media reach." But the letter also indicates the award's significance, 
describing the prize's "national scale" and its reputation as "one of the highest forms of professional 
recognition for specialists in music marketing and promotion." Thus, the letter constitutes independent 
evidence of the award's national recognition. 
The Petitioner submitted documentation of her receipt of a nationally or internationally recognized 
prize or award for excellence in her field. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B) ("[A]lthough some of 
the regulatory language relating to evidence occasionally uses plurals, it is entirely possible that the 
presentation of a single piece of evidence in a specific evidentiary category may be sufficient.") We 
will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
2. The Remaining Claimed Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner also claims that she submitted evidence regarding: published materials about her and 
her work; her original contributions ofmajor significance in her field; and her performance in a leading 
or critical role. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), (viii). But she has already satisfied the requisite 
number of evidentiary requirements. We therefore need not determine whether she met the additional 
claimed criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies 
need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
The Petitioner has met the required number of evidentiary criteria. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). USCIS 
must therefore conduct a final merits detennination. SCOPS did not make such a determination. We 
will therefore make one now. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
To establish eligibility for the requested immigrant visa category, the Petitioner must demonstrate that 
she has sustained national or international acclaim and that her achievements have been recognized in 
her field, identifying her as one of the small percentage who has risen to the field's very top. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). Congress indicated that "a career of acclaimed work in 
3 
the field" can demonstrate an alien's extraordinary ability. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (Sept. 19, 
1990). 
When making a final merits determination, USCIS must consider any potentially relevant evidence, 
even if it does not fit one of the regulatory criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). The Petitioner must explain the significance of her 
submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that she has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim and recognition in her field. Id. The petition's approval or denial depends on the type and 
quality of evidence submitted. Id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues: 
The record contains substantial evidence of sustained professional recognition, original 
contributions adopted by independent entities, scholarly authorship, and reliance by 
multiple organizations on [her] expertise. When considered in the aggregate, this 
evidence establishes that [she] has achieved sustained acclaim and has risen to the top 
of the field. 
We will now review the record for evidence of the Petitioner's claimed sustained acclaim and 
recognition in her field. 
1. Awards 
As previously indicated, the Petitioner's 2021 ~--------------~' generated 
some national recognition for her. But she has not demonstrated that a single nationally recognized 
award reflects an alien with extraordinary ability or a career ofacclaimed work in her field as Congress 
contemplated. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("In all cases, the petitioner must provide 
sufficient context regarding the ... evidence and considerations to demonstrate that the evidence meets 
the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary ability in the totality of the 
circumstances.") Also, a single award does not indicate "sustained" acclaim. See section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
2. Published Materials About the Petitioner 
The Petitioner submitted copies of six online articles about her and her work, published between 
November 2021 and March 2024. The articles' publishers include: two Russian entertainment 
magazines; an international media platform; a U.S. weekly magazine about business leaders and 
pioneers; and a weekly Russian newspaper. 
These articles reflect some recognition of the Petitioner's achievements in her field. But she has not 
demonstrated that the published material elevates her into the small percentage at her field's very top. 
See Hamal v. US. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. 
June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 
1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (affirming USCIS' finding that a petitioner did not show how "his 
press coverage is indicative of a level of success consistent with being" at his field's top).

3. Judge of Others' Work 
The Petitioner demonstrated that she served as a judge at the 2022 ~--------~ on the 
Russian music television channel. Her selection as a judge reflects some acclaim in her field. But the 
Petitioner has not explained how this single judging event establishes her claimed status among the 
small percentage at her field's top. See Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at 
*23 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding USCTS' finding that a petitioner did not show how she 
"judged to such an extent, or at a high enough level, that her judging surpasses the majority ofthe field 
and places her in the top echelons of [her field]"). Also, the single judging event does not reflect 
"sustained" acclaim as the Act requires. See section 203(b )(l )(A)(i) of the Act. 
4. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
As evidence of original contributions of major significance, the Petitioner first submitted a copy of a 
scholarly article she wrote about blogging in 2024. As SCOPS found, however, she did not 
demonstrate that the article influenced her field. The record lacks evidence, for example, that others 
in her field cited her article. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(2) (stating that a petitioner's high 
rate of citations relative to others in their field may indicate their position among the small percentage 
at the field's very top). 
In response to SCOPS' request for additional evidence, the Petitioner further submitted materials 
regarding a marketing methodology she developed. She stated that her methodology "unites 
diagnostic modeling with behavior analytics, structural competitive assessment, and ratio-based 
contingent engineering to create an operational system that generates quantifiable and expandable 
business results." She provided copies of 15 user agreements she entered into with customers of her 
marketing system in 2024 and 2025, and 16 letters from customers praising her system and her work. 
Despite receiving recognition from customers for her marketing system, the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated her receipt of acclaim or recognition in her field for the system's development. The 
record lacks independent evidence placing her among the small percentage at the digital marketing 
field's very top. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991 LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. 
Va. Apr. 20, 2021) ("[I]t is generally expected that an individual whose accomplishments have 
garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have received recognition for his or her 
accomplishments well beyond the circle of his personal and professional acquaintances"). 
5. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
As previously indicated, the Petitioner submitted evidence that a scholarly journal published an article 
she wrote about blogging in 2024. She has not established, however, that the article generated national 
or international acclaim or recognition. The record, for example, lacks evidence that others in her 
field cited her article or that the journal that published it is highly ranked in the field. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(8)(2) (stating that a petitioner's high rate ofcitations relative to others in their field 
and a journal's high ranking in the field may indicate the petitioner's position among the small 
percentage at the field's top). 
5 
6. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
The Petitioner submitted letters from various customers praising her marketing roles on their behalf. 
Some ofthe customers stated that she made substantial contributions to their businesses. For example, 
an influencer "attribute[ d] much of my success on social media to the exceptional expertise of [the 
Petitioner]." The influencer stated that the Petitioner's work from 2020 to 2022 increased both the 
influencer's followers and revenues by 60%. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that her marketing roles have received sustained 
acclaim or recognition in her field. See Agrawal, 2025 WL 2879217, at *24 (affirming USCTS' finding 
that a petitioner's evidence did not describe how she garnered sustained national or international 
reputation through her roles). 
In sum, the record identifies the Petitioner as a young, talented marketer with a potentially bright future 
in her field. She met the initial evidentiary criteria for the requested immigrant visa category and 
demonstrated her receipt of some acclaim and recognition in her field. But, even considering the 
evidence in the aggregate, she has not established her receipt of sustained acclaim and recognition 
sufficient to place her among the small percentage at her field's very top. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability, reserving this 
immigrant visa category "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very top of their 
field of endeavor." Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991 ). Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner has not established her claimed 
extraordinary ability in her field. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary 
ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a 
petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not 
automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner satisfied the requisite number of evidentiary criteria for the requested immigrant visa 
category. But she did not demonstrate her receipt of sustained acclaim and recognition sufficient to 
warrant classification as an alien with extraordinary ability. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
