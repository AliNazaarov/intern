Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a biophysics researcher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw USCIS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with 
the following analysis. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of ten lesser criteria or comparable evidence. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and ( 4). 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits determination 
to assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that 
the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 
772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

The Petitioner claimed to satisfy three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), pertaining to participation as a judge of the work ofothers; original contributions 
of major significance; and authorship of scholarly articles. users concluded that the Petitioner met 
two ofthe criteria, pertaining to judging and authorship ofscholarly articles. 1 On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains that he has also satisfied the third claimed criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), which calls 
for evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field. Specifically, the Petitioner contends that the reception 
of his published work establishes the major significance of his original contributions. 
Evidence under this criterion can include letters and affidavits about the work and documentation that the 
original work has been cited at a level that indicates major significance in the field. See, generally, 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. We agree with the Petitioner 
that users does not appear to have given sufficient weight to this evidence. 
The Petitioner initially submitted a Google Scholar printout showing 2,762 citations ofhis articles. Later 
submissions show significantly higher citation totals. In the denial notice, users acknowledged the 
citation figures but then made general assertions about the collaborative nature ofscience and "moderately 
valuable research." users also acknowledged the Petitioner's submission of several letters from 
independent researchers discussing his work, but did not discuss the contents ofthose letters in any detail. 
We note that the Petitioner's most-cited article is a review article that summarizes existing literature, 
rather than reporting new research. The Petitioner himself distinguishes between "original research and 
review articles" when discussing scholarly publications. But even without this article, the record shows 
heavy citation of some of the Petitioner's original research articles. 
The record appears to establish, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, that the Petitioner has made original 
contributions of major significance in his field. As such, the Petitioner has established he meets three 
initial criteria and has satisfied the first part ofthe two-step adjudicative process described in Kazarian. 
We will withdraw users' decision, because that decision was based on the determination that the 
Petitioner had satisfied fewer than three of the initial evidentiary criteria. As noted above, where a

The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
In rendering the final merits determination, USCIS may wish to consider certain factors when 
determining the extent of the Petitioner's recognition and acclaim. 
The Petitioner's peer review of manuscripts facially satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) 
because it involves judging the work of others in his field. But nothing in that regulatory criterion speaks 
to the level of acclaim and recognition required to engage in, or resulting from, such judging. Therefore, 
it is not only relevant, but necessary, to consider the circumstances under which the Petitioner undertook 
his peer review activity. The record shows that the Petitioner received his first peer review invitation in 
July 2020 when he was still a graduate student. One publisher.I ~ndicated in its email messages 
that the Petitioner submitted applications to conduct peer reviews. Most of the Petitioner's peer review 
work has been for a small number ofpublishers. Verifiable information about how those publishers select 
peer reviewers, such as the minimum requirements for such reviewers, might shed light on whether 
reviewing for those publishers is a hallmark of sustained national or international acclaim. 
Also, when considering the extent to which the Petitioner's acclaim and recognition have been sustained, 
USCIS may wish to consider whether the high citation rate of the Petitioner's earlier work in China has 
persisted with his more recent research in the United States. And, where possible, it is important to 
distinguish between the reputation and recognition of a particular research institution and that of an 
individual researcher at that institution. 
Ultimately, the outcome of the case depends on the objective documentary evidence, rather than on the 
Petitioner's claims. For example, the Petitioner establishes that researchers "in 40 countries and 
territories" have cited his work. But the Petitioner cites no supporting source for his assertion that "in 
[the] academic world, only top researchers with national and international acclaim in their field have their 
scholarly articles read and commented [sic] by others internationally." Statements in a brief, motion, or 
Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight. Matter ofS-M-, 
22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998). 
ORDER: USCIS' decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
