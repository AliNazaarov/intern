Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an enterprise architect, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the record 
establishes that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required, 
the totality ofthe evidence did not show, in a final merits determination, that the Petitioner has received 
sustained national or international acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top 
of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinaiy ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate recognition 
ofhis or her achievements in the fie ld through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 20 10). 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 201 1). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) -
(x). The Petitioner initially claimed to have met eight criteria: 
• (i), receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence; 
• (ii), membership in associations which require outstanding achievements of their members; 
• (iii), published material about the individual in professional or major media; 
• (iv), participation as a judge of the work of others; 
• (v), original contributions of major significance to the field; 
• (vi), authorship of scholarly articles in the field in professional or major trade publications or 
other major media; 
• (viii), leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• (ix), commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration in relation to others in 
the field. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met four of these claimed evidentiary criteria: membership in 
associations in the field which require outstanding achievements of their members; participation as a 
judge of the work of others; authorship of scholarly articles; and commanding a high salary relative to 
others. While SCOPS discussed and provided analysis to why the Petitioner did not establish that he 
has made original contributions ofmajor significance to the field or that he has had a leading or critical 
role for distinguished organizations, SCOPS did not analyze or discuss whether the Petitioner

B. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS erred by not finding that the record established the 
Petitioner's original contributions of major significance, that he has had a critical role in distinguished 
organizations, that he has received nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards, or that 
he has been the subject of published material about his work. The Petitioner also asserts that SCOPS 
erred in its final merits determination by placing too much emphasis on whether his recognition has 
been "long standing," that SCOPS erred by "devaluing" the initial criteria that were established, and 
by considering the categories of evidence in isolation rather than in totality. 
As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, 2 that he is one of that small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's 
accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his successes are sufficient to 
demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of 
the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.3 In this matter, 
we determine the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility.

development. He is currently employed as a cloud center of excellence (COE) leader atl I 
where he states that he has led large-scale architecture and modernization programs for Fortune 500 
client companies. Prior to his current position, he worked on enterprise platform architecture at other

regulatory that the Petitioner met in its final merits determination. The Petitioner claims that, after 
finding that the Petitioner established some of the initial regulatory criteria, SCOPS "strips these 
findings of probative weight by revisiting and diminishing" in a way inconsistent with the prior

The Petitioner's claim relies on a mischaracterization ofKazarian and USCIS policy. In determining 
whether a petitioner has established extraordinary ability, we use a two-step analysis to evaluate the 
evidence. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.4 As described in the USCIS Policy Manual, 
the evidence evaluated in the first step analysis is also reviewed in the second step, where the officer 
must determine whether the person is one of that small percentage of the who has risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor, and that the person has sustained national or international acclaim.5 
Objectively meeting the regulatory criteria in the first step does not establish that the person in fact 
meets the requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability. 6 
Therefore, SCOPS' analysis, in a final merits determination, as to whether the evidence-including 
evidence that demonstrates initial regulatory criteria-shows that the Petitioner meets the 
requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability, is a proper application of this 
framework. In fact, the USCIS Policy Manual provides the following example that relates to judging: 
Participating in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization alone, regardless of the circumstances, should satisfy the regulatory 
criteria in the first step of the analysis. However, the second step requires the officer to 
evaluate the person's participation to determine whether it was indicative of the person 
being one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor and enjoying sustained national or international acclaim. 7 
This analysis is not, as the Petitioner claims, "devaluing" the regulatory criteria of the first step in the 
second step's final merits determination. Rather, this analysis reflects the proper analysis in which 
the officer, at step one, determines only if the evidence objectively meets the regulatory criteria and, 
at step two, considers whether the evidence in its totality demonstrates sustained national or 
international acclaim.

Regardless of whether the evidence establishes the regulatory criteria of original contributions of 
major significance or leading or critical roles at distinguished organizations, we conclude that this 
evidence is not sufficiently persuasive to demonstrate that the Petitioner's contributions are indicative 
of sustained national or international acclaim. Although the Petitioner provided some additional 
documentary evidence, such as company profile information and evidence of a speaking engagement 
at a conference, the Petitioner relies primarily on letters of support. The letters generally highlight the 
Petitioner's work and state the author's opinion that the Petitioner is a talented enterprise architect. In 
describing the Petitioner's work, the letters, at most, indicate that the Petitioner is a competent, 
respected figure within the field. 9 
However, they do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that the Petitioner is 
viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, to be among the upper echelon or that he 
has garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 1 :20-cv-991 
(LMB/TCB), 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the plaintiff's 
letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his accomplishments to show sustained 
national or international acclaim). Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's work, they do not 
reflect a career ofacclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international 
acclaim. See Bodhankarv. USCIS, No. l:19-CV-706 (MAD/CFH), 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. 
Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the submitted letters failed to establish the plaintiff's contributions resulted in 
significant acclaim).

or attention based on his earnings, setting himself apart from others in the field or placing him in the 
upper echelon. See id. (finding that the plaintiff's high salary was not so high as to indicate that he 
has risen to the very top of the field).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
