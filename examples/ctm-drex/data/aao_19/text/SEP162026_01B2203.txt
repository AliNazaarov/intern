Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Exceptional Ability) 
The Petitioner, a technology entrepreneurship entity, seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Beneficiary had a 
major, internationally recognized award, or that he met at least three ofthe ten regulatory criteria listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Beneficiary is the founder and Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of the petitioning entity, which is 
a financial technology platform that offers banking solutions to small and medium businesses in Africa 
and in the United States. The Beneficiary currently holds 0-1 nonimmigrant status and intends to 
continue to work in this capacity in the United States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must demonstrate that the Beneficiary satisfies at least three ofthe 
alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed the 
Beneficiary satisfied eight criteria. USCIS concluded that the Beneficiary satisfied only two of the 
claimed criteria - those related to judging and performing in a leading or critical role - and therefore 
did not establish his eligibility for the classification. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (viii). On appeal, 
the Petitioner claims the Beneficiary meets the remainder of the claimed criteria which are related to 
awards, membership, published material about the individual, authorship of scholarly articles, display, 
and high salary. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(iii), (vi)-(vii), and (ix). After reviewing the evidence in the 
record, we conclude the Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary meets the initial evidence 
requirement for this classification. Therefore, he is not eligible for classification as an individual 
possessing extraordinary ability in the field of technology entrepreneurship. 
1. Evidence of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." The 
Petitioner's claim regarding this criterion is based on its receipt of over $5. 9 million in funding from 
venture capital (VC) firms. In the denial, USCIS determined that the Petitioner has not established 
that a particular instance of funding has the requisite national or international recognition and 
concluded that evidence of funding received by the Beneficiary's company is not sufficient to meet

The Petitioner claims the Beneficiary is eligible for this criterion because he received "funding awards 
from respected business and technology experts" at "prominent" VC firms, such asl II I, among other such firms. The Petitioner provided a letter from A-W-, an 
entrepreneur who stated that as a result of the Beneficiary's "rare skills as founder and CEO of 
he has garnered " lobal media covera e ... and raised over $8 million in funding awarded by top

levels." However, the evidence does not demonstrate that this fonding constitutes a nationally or 
internationally recognized prize or award for excellence in the field. Although the record contains 
evidence regarding the overall difficulty of obtaining this fonding and approval rate of 1-2%, 
the issue is whether particular fonding, or that of any "prominent" VC firm, is a nationally or 
internationally recognized award for excellence. Because this regulatory criterion requires nationally 
or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field, simply receiving prizes or 
awards in the field is insufficient. Similarly, merely receiving seed fonding or venture capital does not 
satisfy this criterion unless the Petitioner establishes that such fonding or venture capital is nationally 
or internationally recognized for excellence in the field. 
Here, the Petitioner did not show that the limited press coverage dedicated to its receipt of venture 
capital fonding - namely, an article that made a passing reference to the Petitioner as a I 
backed startup" - reflects national or international recognition for excellence in the field. Moreover, 
A-W-'s letter does not discuss the national or international recognition of funding by any of the VC 
firms he mentioned but rather references the amount of fonding the Petitioner received and the 
reputation of "top firms" who contributed towards the fonding. Although the record contains articles 
about the companies that provided fonding to the Petitioner and printouts from the companies' 
respective websites, the Petitioner does not sufficiently explain or show how the evidence 
demonstrates the national or international recognition of the specific venture capital fonding as a prize 
or award for excellence in the field. 
The Petitioner also does not establish that receiving VC fonding qualifies as a prize or award. In 
response to the request for evidence, the Petitioner cited an unpublished district court decision, Zizi v. 
Cuccinelli, 2021 WL 2826713 (N.D. Cal 2021), which found that USCIS was arbitrary and capricious 
in concluding that venture capital fonding did not satisfy the lesser prizes or awards criteria. We note 
that in contrast to the broad precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we 
are not bound to follow the published decision of a United States district court in cases arising within 
the same district. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). The reasoning underlying a district 
judge's decision will be given due consideration when it is properly before us; however, the analysis 
does not have to be followed as a matter of law. Id. at 719. In addition, the Petitioner submitted an 
archived screenshot from USCIS' website entitled, "Understanding O-lA Requirements," which is 
not relevant in this proceeding. And even if we were to consider that venture capital could be deemed 
a prize or award, this would not mean that any and all venture capital, from any source, would be 
accepted as a nationally or internationally recognized prize or award.

3. Published Materials About the Beneficiary

4. Authorship of Scholarly Articles in the Field 
Turning to the scholarly articles criterion, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for 
"[e ]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media." In academia, a scholarly article reports on original research, 
experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally peer reviewed, and may be published in 
professional or scientific journals or conference presentations. In other fields, a scholarly article 
should be written for others having profound knowledge in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). To determine ifthe publication qualifies as a professional or major trade 
publication or other major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership 
relative to other media in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id. 
The Petitioner previously submitted definitions from www.merriam-webster.com of "scholarly" 
("characteristic of, or suitable to learned persons") and "learned" ("characterized by or associated with 
learning"), asserting that the type of media in which an article is published can inform its scholarly 
intent. The Petitioner also submitted interview transcripts and informational material about two 
podcasts - Techpoint Africa Podcast and Founders Connect- where the Beneficiary was interviewed 
and asked to provide "his learned philosophical opinions and perspective regarding the challenges and 
opportunities of his industry." The Petitioner claimed that "industry knowledge-sharing by 
occupational STEM professionals has relevance to this criterion," highlighting the informational 
material on the Techpoint Africa website that describes it as "a digital media company that amplifies 
the best innovations out of Africa through its publications, data, and events." However, the Petitioner 
has not established that the content of the podcast interviews is scholarly in nature. For instance, in 
the Techpoint Africa Podcast, the Beneficiary discussed the benefits of having access to financial 
services and the importance of education and financial literacy. Likewise, the Founders Connect 
interview also includes content not specifically geared to learned persons in the field - namely, the 
Beneficiary's discussion of his personal and professional background, including where he grew up, 
where and what he studied in college, and his "top three lessons" based on his professional 
experiences. The Petitioner does not explain how these discussions support the claim that the 
respective podcasts were intended for learned persons in the field of technological entrepreneurialism. 
The Petitioner also provided information about Techpoint Africa and Founders Connect but it did not 
provide evidence, such as circulation data for the podcasts that aired the Beneficiary's interviews, to 
establish that the podcasts qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). In addition, the Petitioner highlights the 
Beneficiary's speaking engagements and involvement as an "expert panelist" at various industry 
events, noting that corroborating evidence of his participation was previously submitted. However,

5. Artistic Display 
Next, we will address the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii), which 
requires "[e]vidence of the 
display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to 
demonstrating that the displayed work was the alien's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) 
where the work was displayed must be artistic exhibitions or showcases. While Merriam-Webster's 
online dictionary definition of "exhibition" includes public showings other than those that are artistic 
in nature, the plain language ofthe criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and explicitly requires that 
the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in nature. 
The Petitioner contends that the Beneficiary's participation inl Iwhere he presented 
his company's work-product before a group of investors, is comparable evidence to a display of his 
work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). We may consider 
non-artistic exhibitions or showcases as part of a properly supported claim of comparable evidence. 
As we stated earlier, a Petitioner can submit comparable evidence to meet a criterion that does not 
readily apply to the person's occupation. When we evaluate comparable evidence, we consider 
whether the regulatory criterion is readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether 
the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in the regulation. 
But in all cases, exhibitions must be public showings. The Petitioner has not established that 
participation inl lwas a public showing. The record includes an article titled, II which states that I is a private, invitation-only event" and that "[t]here is no 
continuous 'list' of investors who are always invited" but rather that" investors are invited based on 
their recent investment history with The record reflects that _____is for "specially 
selected investors and press" and is a "private, invitation-only event." Here, the record does not address 
how a presentation before curated investors and media compares to a public exhibition or showcase. 
Therefore, we conclude the Petitioner has not met this criterion with its comparable evidence. 
6. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
Lastly, we will address the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), which seeks "[e]vidence that the 
alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to 
others in the field." A credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has 
commanded" such salary or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and 
pay statements. Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation 
and location should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working 
in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For entrepreneurs or founders 
of startup businesses, officers consider evidence that the business has received significant funding 
from government entities, venture capital funds, angel investors, or other such funders in evaluating 
the credibility of submitted contracts, job offer letters, or other evidence of prospective salary or 
remuneration for services. Id.

The Petitioner provided a letter confirming the terms ofthe Beneficiary's employment, which includes 
a 50% equity stake in the Petitioner that is claimed to be valued at $24 million based on "its most 
recent $48 million valuation." The Petitioner clarifies that the Beneficiary's wages "are not claimed 
under this criterion" and contends that the Beneficiary's equity share in the company is significantly 
high remuneration in relation to others in the field of technology entrepreneurialism. 
The plain language of this criterion requires consideration of the value of the remuneration 
commanded in order to determine whether it is significantly high in relation to others in the field. 
Here, the Petitioner claims that the remuneration commanded is $24 million based on a $48 million 
valuation. Supporting evidence includes a document titled "Summary of Terms for Sale of Series A 
Preferred Stock Financing of [the Petitioner]," which states that the financing terms are based on "a 
fully-diluted post-money valuation of US $48,000,000." The Petitioner did not provide sufficient 
evidence establishing the significance of the term "post-money valuation." The Petitioner provided 
an article defining "post-money value" as "the amount that a given pre-money value infers the 
company to be worth at the moment immediately following an investment." With a note of sarcasm 
that includes a reference to "an ethereal portal flanked by golden unicorns wearing VR headsets," the 
article explains the unreliable process of determining pre-money value, stating that it "is often not 
derived from accounting measures such as revenue, free cash flow or EBITDA," and "is often highly 
negotiated, driven by market forces and inherently speculative." Because the Petitioner does not 
adequately support the $48 million "fully-diluted post-money valuation" which serves as the basis for 
the Beneficiary's proposed $24 million remuneration, we will not consider that figure. 
Further, the Petitioner provided seven SAFEs between the Petitioner and seven VC firms. All seven 
agreements state that "[t]he 'Post-Money Valuation Cap' is $35,000,000," which indicates that the 
Petitioner can be valued at no more than $35 million. This clause indicates that there is an 
inconsistency between these agreements and the Petitioner's claimed "post-money valuation" of $48 
million. The record does not contain independent, objective evidence resolving this apparent 
incongruity. See Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988). 
Importantly, the Petitioner has not provided a relevant basis for comparison of the Beneficiary's 
remuneration, whether actual or speculative, to that of others in his field. 
For all of the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary meets 
this criterion. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F.Supp.3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, 
evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). We therefore 
8 
decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
C. 0-1 Classification 
As previously indicated, the record reflects that the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification 
reserved for nonimmigrants of extraordinary ability. However, the prior approval does not preclude 
USCIS from denying an immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a different standard -
statute, regulations, and case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 
1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 
2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, 
at *l (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). Many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior 
nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 (D.D.C. 
2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. US. Dep 't ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 (D.D.C. 1999), aff'd sub nom. 
IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep 't ofJust., INS, No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 1999); Fedin 
Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), aff'd, 905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1990). 
Furthermore, our authority over SCOPS, the office adjudicating nonimmigrant visa petitions, is 
comparable to the relationship between a court of appeals and a district court. Even if SCOPS has 
approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf of an individual, we are not bound to follow that finding 
in the adjudication of another immigrant petition. See Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. 
CIV. A. 98-2855, 2000 WL 282785, at *2 (E.D. La. Mar. 15, 2000). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary meets at least three of ten evidentiary criteria 
under 8 C.F.R. § 2045.5(h)(3) and therefore does not meet the initial evidence requirements for 
classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
