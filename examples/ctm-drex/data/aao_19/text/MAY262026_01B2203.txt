Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, finding the Petitioner met the initial 
evidentiary requirements of the classification but his evidence did not show sustained national or 
international acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. 
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, In c., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204. 5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).1 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCJS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation 
is frrst counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Vis inscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a national of Brazil and is the beneficiary of an 0-1 nonimmigrant visa. Pursuant to 
his Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers, his occupation is that of a finance controller 
and he is the chief executive officer (CEO) o~ Ia financial startup. According 
to his personal statement dated November 2025 , he is a person of extraordinary ability in business, 
specializing in strategic financial leadership, multinational controllership, and innovative financial 
process transformation. He submitted documents indicating he has a three-year degree in accounting 
and a professional certificate in administration. 
The Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally recognized 
award under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). He must therefore satisfy at least three of the regulatory criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Based on our de novo review, the Petitioner has not met this showing. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria Waived 
The Petitioner did not assert in the record below or on appeal that he meets the criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iv), (v), (vi), (vii), or (x) regarding receipt of lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards, participation as a judge of the work of others, original 
contributions of major significance, authorship of articles, display of his work, and commercial 
successes in the performing arts. We therefore deem the Petitioner's eligibility under these six criteria 
waived. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 
I&N Dec. 657, 658 n.2 (BIA 2012))(An eligibility ground that is not raised on appeal is waived.)

The Petitioner included documents about ANEF AC, I l and I I and evidence of his 
membership within each. However, the Petitioner did not provide membership requirements for 
I I and I I and therefore has not met his burden in demonstrating that they require 
outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts

The Petitioner's documents show that he is an associate member of ANEFAC and according to the 
"General Regulations for ANEF AC Membership Admission," the associate member category is: 
"intended for professionals with a record of extraordinary achievements and prominent 
performance in the corporate environment, who cumulatively demonstrate: a minimum 
of ten (10) years of relevant professional experience ... ; experience in multinational 
companies . . . ; concrete contribution to a significant revenue increase in the 
organizations where they have worked ...." 
First, we acknowledge the associate member category "is intended for" those "with a record of 
extraordinary achievements," beyond the three subsequent requirements for membership. However, 
the ANEFAC membership regulations provide no further description of what constitutes a "record of 
extraordinary achievements" such that the Petitioner has sufficiently demonstrated that ANEF AC 
requires outstanding achievements of its members. 2 
Second, the associate member category is "granted upon unanimous approval by all members of the 
Admission and Categories Council." The Council is comprised of six associate members, one 
contributing member3 and two distinguished members who are appointed by the Executive Board and 
all Council members "must have proven experience in institutional selection processes, association 
management, and corporate ethics." However, this information does not demonstrate that the 
members of the Council are recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields.

2. High Salary 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner established he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), 
requiring him to establish he commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for 
services, in relation to others in the field. A credible employment contract or job offer may establish 
that an alien "has commanded" such salary or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as 
tax returns and pay statements. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
Id. 
The Petitioner submitted his paystubs from I Ifor his position as "Controllership 
Director" for months in 2024 and 2025, indicating his monthly base salary was approximately 42,000 
Brazilian reals (R$). The Petitioner also provided data from Glassdoor.com, which appears to provide 
user-reported salary data and may not be as credible or reliable as an objective report of what 
employers actually pay workers. Id. Further, the wage data is for "controllership manager" salaries 
for all experience levels for the entirety of Brazil. However, the wage data from Glassdoor.com does 
not provide a description of the "controllership manager" occupation, nor does it specify what 
industries were included in the survey. Broad descriptions that include multiple occupations or 
multiple industries may not provide an accurate comparison to others in the field. Id. The Petitioner 
has therefore not demonstrated that the wage information for a "controllership manager" averaging 
salary data from all industries and all experience levels is an appropriate basis of comparison.4 As a 
result, the Petitioner has not met his burden to demonstrate this criterion and we withdraw SCOPS 
determination to the contrary. 
C. Issues Reserved 
SCOPS further determined the Petitioner met 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and (viii), requiring evidence 
of published material about the Petitioner in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the Petitioner's work in the field for which classification is sought; and that he 
performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished 
reputation. As the Petitioner would be unable to establish that he meets at least three of the ten 
threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve our analysis of these 
remaining criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies 
are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate 
decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to

show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 (discussing a two-step review, 
where if the required number of criteria are met by the documentation, then it is considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 
2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa"). Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby 
reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
We note that the record reflects that the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification reserved for 
nonimmigrants of extraordinary ability. Although USCIS has approved the 0-1 nonimmigrant visa 
petition filed on behalf of the Petitioner, the prior approval does not preclude USCIS from denying an 
immigrant visa petition which is adjudicated based on a different standard - statute, regulations, and 
case law. Many Form I-140 immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior nonimmigrant 
petitions. See, e.g., Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US v. 
US Dept. ofJustice, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22 (D.D.C. 1999); Fedin Bros. Co., Ltd. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 
1103, 1108 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), affd, 905 F. 2d 41 (2d. Cir. 1990). We are not bound by service center 
or district director's decisions. See, e.g., La. Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, 248 F.3d 1139 (5th Cir. 
2001) (per curiam). Further, under USCIS policy, the Petitioner's receipt of 0-1 nonimmigrant 
classification is relevant and can be considered an indicator that he is eligible for EB-1 classification, 
but it is not determinative. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3). However, as 
discussed above, the evidentiary deficiencies in the record do not support a grant of this petition. As 
such, we have considered the Petitioner's grant of 0-1 nonimmigrant visa classification, but the 
evidence remains insufficient to meet his burden to establish his eligibility for EB-1 classification. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
