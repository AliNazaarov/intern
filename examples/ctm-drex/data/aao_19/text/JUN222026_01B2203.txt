Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for 
Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner did not warrant an 
approval considering the totality ofthe evidence. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. We issued a notice of intent to dismiss (NOID) the appeal noting signature abnormalities 
throughout this record of proceeding. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 
(AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 
537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
USCIS requires a valid signature on certain documents filed with the agency (request). 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .2( a)(2). Unless specifically authorized, an individual representing a petitioner must personally sign 
his or her own request before filing it with USCIS. On the Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion, 
the Petitioner purportedly signed the forms. 
In reviewing the appeal, we observed that the various signatures located across the record of 
proceeding attributed to this individual differed visibly. On May 8, 2026, we issued a NOID to the 
Petitioner. Within this correspondence, we requested an explanation for these variances, along with 
sworn statements and copies of government-issued identification documents from the Petitioner and 
any other individual who signed the forms and documents in the record. 
Without knowing who signed the documents we cannot recognize the appeal to have been properly 
filed by an affected party with legal standing in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3(a)(l)(iii)(B)-(2)(i). In addition, without knowing who signed the documentation, we cam10t 
determine whether the petition was properly filed. See generally 8 C.F.R. § 103 .2(a). Responding to 
the NOID, the Petitioner provides a generalized set ofreasons why any person's signature might differ, 
but she is unable to explain why her signatures are so distinctly different. She states: 
Any variation in the visual appearance of my signature across documents is the result 
of ordinary and natural factors, including but not limited to: signing on different 
physical surfaces (such as paper documents resting on a desk versus paper resting on 
other surfaces); the use of different writing instruments; signing under different 
circumstances oftime, posture, and pressure; and the natural evolution and variation of 
my handwriting over time. 
Here, the Petitioner has not identified which, if any, of the purported "ordinary and natural factors" 
that supposedly caused every signature on the non-USCIS immigration forms to appear so distinct 
from each of her signatures on the USCIS forms. While we do not consider her NOID response to be 
unresponsive, she did not offer an adequate rebuttal to our allegation that it appeared she did not 
personally sign the relevant immigration forms. We acknowledge her sworn affidavit that she did sign 
the forms, but those claims do not preponderantly demonstrate that she personally signed them rather 
than someone from the document preparation service she used to file both the Form I-290B and the 
Form I-140. 
A trier of fact should consider the issues presented and provide a sufficiently reasoned analysis. They 
are not however, required to interpret evidence in the manner a petitioner advocates. Where two 
permissible views of evidence exist, a factfinder's choice between them cannot be considered 
erroneous. Matter ofG-C-I-, 29 I&N Dec. 176, 179 (BIA 2025); Matter ofD-R-, 25 I&N Dec. 445, 
455 (BIA 2011) (citing Anderson v. City ofBessemer City, NC., 470 U.S. 564, 574 (1985) remanded 
and aff'd in part 27 I&N Dec. l 05, 122 (BIA 2017). A trier offact should consider the issues presented 
and provide a sufficiently reasoned analysis. They are not however, required to interpret evidence in 
the manner a petitioner advocates. Matter ofM-D-C-V-, 28 I&N Dec. 18, 32 (BIA 2020); Matter of 
J-C-H-F-, 27 I&N Dec. 211, 217 (BIA 2018); Matter of Z-Z-O-, 26 I&N Dec. 586, 592 (BIA 
2015); D-R-, 25 I&N Dec. at 454-55 (explaining that an trier of fact may make reasonable inferences 
from direct and circumstantial evidence in the record as a whole and is not required to accept a 
respondent's account where other plausible views of the evidence are supported by the record); see 
also Matter ofE-F-N-, 28 I&N Dec. 591, 593 (BIA 2022) (noting that we are not required to adopt an 
applicant's explanation for an inconsistency ifthere are other permissible views of the evidence based 
on the record). 
Similarly, we are not required to accept a petitioner's assertions, even if plausible, where there are 
other permissible views ofthe evidence based on the record. Matter ofG-C-1-, 29 I&N Dec. at 179. As 
a result, even though the Petitioner's justification for the markedly different signatures-purportedly 
made by the same person-may be one possible explanation, we are not required to view it as the most 
likely scenario we will rely on within our determination. 
While we accept the Petitioner's response as one possible, but overly generalized explanation, we do 
not consider it to be one that establishes that she was the actual individual who signed both the Form 
I-140 and the Form I-290B. See generally 1 USCIS Policy Manual B.2(A), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Ifthe Petitioner does not establish that she was the person who 
signed the Form I-290B, we cannot recognize the appeal to have been properly filed by an affected

party with legal standing in these proceedings. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l )(iii)(B). Nor can we decide 
that the Petitioner properly filed this appeal, and we will dismiss this filing. See generally I USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, B.2(A). 
In visa petition proceedings, it is the petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration 
benefit sought. Section 291 of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1361. The Petitioner has not met that burden. The 
appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered an independent and 
alternative basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
