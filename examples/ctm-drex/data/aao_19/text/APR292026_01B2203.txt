Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an accounting and tax consultant who claims "extraordinary ability" under 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A), files a 
second round of combined motions to reopen and reconsider, contending that we erred in dismissing 
the first round. See In Re: 40852121 (AAO Jan. 23, 2026). Her motion to reopen does not meet 
applicable requirements, and her motion to reconsider does not establish her eligibility for the 
requested benefit. We will therefore dismiss the motions. 
I. LAW 
A motion to reopen must state new facts, supported by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). 
In contrast, a motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision misapplied law or policy 
based on the record at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). On motion, regulations limit 
our scope of review to the prior decision. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii) (referring to "the prior 
decision" and "the latest decision in the proceeding"). We may grant motions that satisfy these 
requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. A petitioner must establish 
eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 
2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Motion to Reopen 
The Petitioner's motion to reopen neither states new facts nor contains documentary evidence. Thus, 
the motion does not meet applicable requirements. See 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(2). We will therefore 
dismiss it. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4) ("A motion that does not meet applicable requirements shall be 
dismissed.") 
B. The Motion to Reconsider 
The Petitioner contends that our prior decision "mischaracterizes the record" by finding that her prior 
motion to reopen did not state new facts. She states that the motion included: her "personal declaration 
clarifying material facts;" "[ e ]vidence relating to continued professional activity;" "[ d]ocumentation 
of ongoing business operations;" and "[ e ]vidence of pursuit of [an] additional immigration 
classification." She says: "These submissions constitute new facts because they post-date the prior 
adjudication and demonstrate continued professional activity and recognition relevant to eligibility." 
She contends that: "[ e ]vidence of continued professional engagement is relevant to sustained 
acclaim;" "[ c ]larifying declarations addressing adjudicative concerns qualify as new factual 
assertions;" and "[ d]ocumentation not previously analyzed in the manner now presented cannot be 
deemed redundant without substantive evaluation." 
On reconsideration, we agree with the Petitioner that the statement in her personal declaration 
regarding her application "for another work visa under a different category" constitutes a new fact. 
But she has not explained its relevance to this petition. So, our error appears to be harmless. See 
generally Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) ( citing cases regarding harmless 
or scrivener's errors). The remainder of her personal declaration, however, discusses facts already in 
the record, including her: amount of time in the United States; bachelor's degree; work history; real 
estate and tax preparer's license; attendance at seminars; and activities as a loan processor and 
bookkeeper. 
The Petitioner's prior motion to reopen also included a copy of a 2018 tax registration certificate for 
her business. Her initial petition, however, also included this certificate. Also, contrary to her 
contentions, the eight-year-old document does not demonstrate her "continued professional activity" 
or "ongoing business operations." Thus, while our prior decision failed to recognize a new fact, we 
properly dismissed the motion to reopen for not establishing the Petitioner's eligibility for the 
requested benefit. 
The Petitioner also contends that our prior decision misinterpreted the term "new facts" in 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(2). She states: "The AAO limited its analysis to whether the specific document had 
previously existed in the record, rather than assessing whether the factual context and evidentiary 
reliance had materially changed." She asserts: "Courts have recognized that motions to reopen must 
be evaluated under a reasonable evidentiary standard and not dismissed on hyper[-]technical grounds." 
We generally interpret the term "new facts" to mean facts that are material to the issues raised on 
motion and that have not been previously submitted in a proceeding. The Petitioner has not pointed 
to any examples of how we allegedly misinterpreted the term. Also, while she refers to "courts," she 
has not cited any specific cases supporting her argument. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3) (requiring a 
motion to reconsider to "be supported by any pertinent precedent decisions to establish that the 
decision was based on an incorrect application of law or Service policy"). The Petitioner therefore 
has not established our misinterpretation of the term "new facts." 
Regarding the Petitioner's prior motion to reconsider, she contends that our prior decision should have 
included a final merits determination in her case, assessing whether she demonstrated "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognition for her achievements sufficient to establish her 
claimed extraordinary ability in her field. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; see generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. She states: 
2 
The AAO concluded [that] the Petitioner met only one [evidentiary] criterion but did 
not properly evaluate all submitted evidence under the totality of the circumstances. 
The decision reflects a piecemeal rejection of evidence without meaningful cumulative 
analysis. This is inconsistent with established precedent requiring a holistic review. 
The petition's approval, however, initially requires the Petitioner's receipt of "a major, international 
recognized award" or satisfaction of at least three of ten other evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). She has not demonstrated her receipt of a major, international recognized award, 
and she met only one of the other evidentiary criteria. Thus, because she did not satisfy initial 
evidentiary requirements, we did not need to make a final merits determination considering all the 
evidence in its totality. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
The Petitioner also contends that: 
The AAO appears to have applied an elevated standard approaching "beyond 
reasonable doubt," rather than the required "preponderance of the evidence." The 
Petitioner bears the burden to show eligibility by a preponderance - meaning more 
likely than not - not to eliminate all doubt. 
Our review of the record, however, shows that our prior decision applied the proper preponderance of 
the evidence standard. The Petitioner has not provided an example ofwhere we did not. She therefore 
has not demonstrated that we applied a higher standard of proof. 
The Petitioner claims that we should have addressed her arguments about her adjustment of status 
application. See section 245(a) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). She states: "While eligibility for 
adjustment itself may be outside AAO review, the legal implications of lawful presence and 
admissibility are relevant to overall eligibility analysis and discretionary considerations." 
Immigrant visa petition proceedings, however, are not the proper forum for admissibility 
determinations. Matter of 0-, 8 I&N Dec. 295, 296-98 (BIA 1959); see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual B.5(C)(l). The Petitioner has not demonstrated that her admissibility affects her eligibility 
for classification as an alien with extraordinary ability. She therefore has not established that we erred 
in declining to address her adjustment arguments. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner's motion to reopen does not meet applicable requirements, and her motion to reconsider 
does not establish her eligibility for the requested benefit. 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed. 
FURTHER ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
