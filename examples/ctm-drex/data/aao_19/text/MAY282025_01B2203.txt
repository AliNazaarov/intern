Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an embossing artist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F .R. 
§ 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

C. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes major, 
significant contributions in the field. 7 
The Petitioner references his submission of letters and specifically points to letters from D-O-A- and 
A-A-M-. For instance, the Petitioner's "excellent sense of taste and artistic flair is his genius, which 
allowed him to create such works" (D-O-A-), and he "was able to achieve an unusual and attractive 
visual effect of creating an interplay decorations of his handmade plates and cups" (A-A-M-). 8 The 
letters, however, do not show original contributions of major significance in the field. Instead, the 
letters praise the Petitioner's talents or indicate various works without farther elaborating and 
explaining how those talents or works constitute original contributions of major significance. See 
Skokos v. United States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 209CV00193RLHPAL, 2010 WL 11538054, at 
*4 (D. Nev. Jan. 13, 2010), aff'd sub nom. Skokos v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712 
(9th Cir. 2011) (holding that while a skilled professional in the field, there was no evidence to suggest 
the plaintiff's professional contributions are original or that they have been ofmajor significance); see 
also Victorov v. Barr, No. CV-19-6948-GW-JPRX, 2020 WL 3213788, at *9 (upholding that while 
support letters praising the plaintiff's style of play may be sufficient to satisfy the originality 
requirements, they did not demonstrate that his contributions were of major significance in the field); 
Gangasani v. Wilkinson, No. CV H-20-2124, 2022 WL 2047374 (S.D. Tex. June 6, 2022) (upholding 
testimonial letters focused on his skills rather than his impact in the field as a whole). 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of 
major significance, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed 
significance.9 Submitted letters should specifically describe the person's contribution and its 
significance in the field and should set forth the basis of the writer's knowledge and expertise. 10 In 
this case, the letters lack specific, detailed information explaining how the Petitioner's work caused 
original contributions of major significance in the field. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 135 
(upholding reference letters' lack of specifics did not show major significance of contributions). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not establish that he satisfies this criterion.

D. Leading or Critical Roles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 11 The 
Petitioner argues that he "plays a critical role in in [sic] the Association of the handicraftsmen of the 
republic of Uzbekistan I ~ He references a letter from M-J- who indicated the 
Petitioner's participation in four exhibitions and claimed that his "continuous participation in such 
significant events testifies to the recognition of [his] professional achievements and contribution to the 
development of art and culture." Further, the Petitioner points to a letter from A-M- confirming the 
Petitioner's membership with I I and "he was promoted many times by international 
festivals and celebrations." Moreover, the Petitioner indicates his submission of documentation 
acknowledging his participation at festivals. 
For a critical role, officers look at whether the evidence establishes that the person has contributed in 
a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities 
or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. 13 Here, the letters do not 
contain detailed, specific information evidencing the Petitioner's critical role. Instead, the letters make 
broad statements without farther elaborating and explaining how the Petitioner performed in a critical 
role. Although the letters and certificates acknowledge the Petitioner's partici ation at festivals, they 
do not reflect that such participation resulted in the success or standing of The 
letters, for example, do not describe how the Petitioner was responsible for ._________, 
accomplishments or achievements in the field. See Goncharov v. Allen, No. 3:21-CV-1372-B, 2022 
WL 17327304, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 29, 2022) (upholding petitioner's letters did not show his role 
was critical to the success of the organization). 
In addition, USCIS determines whether the organization or establishment, or the department or 
division for which the person holds or held a leading or critical role, has a distinguished reputation. 14 
Websters online dictionary defines "distinguished" as "marked by eminence! distinction or 
excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." 15 Here, the Petitioner did not show that_ I

promote development ofrich and diverse arts and applied arts ofUzbekistan." However, the Petitioner 
did not establish how this shows thatl lhas distinguished itself or otherwise enjoys an 
eminent reputation. 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he meets this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 
F.3d at 1119-20. Accordingly, we reserve this issue. 16 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 17 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary 
ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087 at *l (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount 
of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance 
of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent 
with a career ofacclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) ofthe Act. While the Petitioner 
need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the 
record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
