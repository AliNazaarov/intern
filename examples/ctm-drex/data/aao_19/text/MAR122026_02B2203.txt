Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner - a player, coach, trainer, and referee in the Brazilian sport of footvolley - requests 
classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an 
alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this 
category must submit evidence of "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively 
document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS found that the Petitioner met the 
requisite three of ten initial evidentiary requirements for the requested category. But, in a final merits 
determination, SCOPS concluded that the record did not establish sufficient acclaim and recognition 
to demonstrate his purported extraordinary ability in his field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that 
SCOPS overlooked evidence and misapplied law to facts. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that he did not establish sustained acclaim or his asserted place among 
the small percentage at his field's very top. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternate evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step review process to be 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

B. The Initial Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of: 
• His receipt of nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in his 
field; 
• Published materials about himself and his work; and 
• Commandment of a high salary in his field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii), (ix). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' erred in finding insufficient evidence of his 
performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii). The Petitioner, however, has already met the required number of evidentiary 
criteria. We therefore need not consider whether he satisfied an additional one. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
We will next review SCOPS' final merits determination. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. USCIS must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that his footvolley activities have generated some national and 
international acclaim. He won twoc=]awards in thel ~ Two 
articles in major Brazilian media also focused on him and his efforts to popularize the sport in the 
United States. Further, the Petitioner co-organized a three-day U.S. footvolley competition that 
attracted 150 teams and about 1,000 spectators and founded two successful training centers, one in 
Brazil and one in the United States. 
3 
But the record supports SCOPS' final merits determination. Contrary to the Act, the Petitioner has 
not demonstrated his receipt of "sustained" acclaim. The word "sustained" means 'to support or 
maintain, especially over a long period of time." Sustained, Black's Law Dictionary (11th ed. 2019). 
The record indicates that most of the Petitioner's acclaim has come only recently towards the end of 
his ten-plus-year career. Thee=] awards are from 2023 and 2024, and the articles about him were 
published in 2022 and 2025. The record also lacks "extensive documentation" of recognition of the 
Petitioner's achievements, see section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act, and evidence sufficiently placing 
him among the small percentage at his field's very top. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
On appeal, the Petitioner accuses SCOPS of "predetermining" types of extraordinary-ability evidence 
that it believes he should have submitted. Specifically, SCOPS questioned how two I lawards 
and two online articles about the Petitioner over his ten-plus-year career establish his extraordinary 
ability in footvolley. See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990) (stating Congress's expectation 
that an alien with extraordinary ability have a "career of acclaimed work in the field"). The Petitioner 
notes that, under USCIS policy: 
An officer may not limit the kind of evidence the officer thinks the person should be 
able to submit and deny the petition if that particular type of evidence ... is absent, if 
the person nonetheless submitted other types of evidence that meet the regulatory 
requirements for the classification. 
6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). 
SCOPS, however, did not limit the type of evidence the Petitioner submitted. Rather, SCOPS 
permissibly questioned the amount of evidence he provided. As previously indicated, this requested 
immigrant visa category requires "extensive documentation" ofrecognition ofachievements in a field. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; see also Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 
56 F.R. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991) ("The intent of Congress that a very high standard be set for 
aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more 
extensive documentation than that required under the current law for [other employment-based 
immigration categories].") Also, as previously indicated, "the petitioner must explain the significance 
of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or 
international acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). 
The Petitioner has not explained how his awards and the published materials about him place him in 
the small percentage at his field's very top. Thus, SCOPS permissibly questioned whether the 
evidence sufficiently establish his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner also contends that SCOPS made contradictory findings regarding evidence that he 
commanded a high salary in relation to others in his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). He notes 
that, while SCOPS found that he met the evidentiary requirement, the decision's final merits 
determination faults him for: 
not establish[ing] how his income compares to others at the very top of his field or that 
he has received notoriety or attention based on his salary that separates him from others 
in his field or placing him at the very top. 
4 
Contrary to the Petitioner's contention, however, SCOPS' findings regarding his salary do not conflict. 
SCOPS found that he met the initial evidentiary criterion by showing that his U.S. coaching salary 
exceeds the average coaching salary in his geographical area. He satisfied the requirement because 
the evidence showed that his salary was high "in relation to others in the field." See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix). But, in the final merits determination, SCOPS had to consider whether the 
Petitioner's coaching salary places him among the small percentage at his field's top, not merely in 
relation to others below that small percentage. Thus, SCOPS properly faulted him for not providing 
sufficient context to demonstrate that his salary places him among the small percentage at the field's 
top. See Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 820 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (affirming USCIS' finding that, 
while a petitioner's high salary reflected recognition of his skillset, "the record did not show that his 
earnings were so high as to indicate that he is one of a small percentage at the top of his field, or that 
his salary [reflected] sustained national acclaim"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) 
("In all cases, the petitioner must provide siifjicient context regarding the above evidence and 
considerations to demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's 
extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances.") (emphasis added). 
The Petitioner farther challenges SCOPS' finding regarding the numerous support letters on his behalf. 
SCOPS concluded: 
The letters do not show the petitioner has received recognition on a national or 
international scale, consistent with being among that small percentage at the very top 
of the field. The record does not support the conclusion that the petitioner has been 
widely influential in his field, that his contributions have had a significant impact, or 
that his contributions have resulted in his sustained acclaim or placed him among the 
small percentage of individuals at the very top of his field. 
The Petitioner contends that SCOPS: 
create[ ed] novel requirements nonexistent in the regulations. Nowhere in the 
regulations does it state that the Petitioner must show that the petitioner has been widely 
influential in his field, that his contributions have had a significant impact, or that his 
contributions have resulted in his sustained acclaim or placed him among the small 
percentage ofindividuals at the ve,y top ofhis field, as suggested by the officer in this 
case. 
(emphasis in original). See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ("[N]either 
USCIS nor [the] AAO [Administrative Appeals Office] may unilaterally impose novel substantive or 
evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5.") 
We disagree. As previously indicated, the regulations define the term "extraordinary ability" as "a 
level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have risen to the 
very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Thus, as SCOPS stated, the Petitioner had 
to show that his footvolley activities "placed him among the small percentage of individuals at the 
very top of his field." Also, as SCOPS stated, the Act requires the Petitioner to show that "his 
contributions have resulted in his sustained acclaim." See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act (requiring 
5 
an alien's extraordinary ability to be "demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim"). 
Thus, the record does not demonstrate that SCOPS held the Petitioner to novel requirements. 
The Petitioner argues that SCOPS "fail[ ed] to consider the totality of evidence within the record." He 
contends that evidence shows that: "he has been leading this sport in the United States;" he organized 
an important U.S. championship; and thel !invited him to work for 
it. 
But the Petitioner has not established that his achievements as a footvolley professional in the United 
States place him among the small percentage at his field's top. The Petitioner has not contextualized 
his expertise level within the entire field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("In all 
cases, the petitioner must provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence and considerations 
to demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary 
ability in the totality of the circumstances.") 
The Petitioner contends that SCOPS overlooked multiple letters on his behalf. He states: "The letters 
submitted with the initial file and the response to the RFE [ request for additional evidence] clearly 
state [his] recognition as one of the pioneers in the field, and his recognition as a professional at the 
very top of the field." 
We agree that SCOPS appears to have overlooked some support letters and aspects of others. These 
materials not only attest to the Petitioner's playing and coaching abilities but also praise his training 
and teaching skills. For example, one letter-writer, a Brazilian professional soccer player, stated that 
he trained with the Petitioner in the United States and left "in top form." But the materials do not 
ultimately explain how the Petitioner ranks among the small percentage at his field's very top. See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("[T]he petitioner must explain the significance of the submitted 
evidence, and how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise.") 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. at 30704. The legislature indicated that this immigrant 
visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very top of their 
field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner met the initial evidentiary requirements. But, considering the 
evidence as a whole, he did not establish receipt of sustained acclaim or his purported place among 
the small percentage at his field's very top. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for 
extraordinary ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined 
that [a petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 T&N Dec. 953, 
954 (Acting Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier 
leagues do not automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not established his receipt of sustained acclaim or his asserted place among the 
small percentage at his field's very top. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial.
