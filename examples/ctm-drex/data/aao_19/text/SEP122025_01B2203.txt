Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a physical therapist and entrepreneur, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner met the 
initial evidence requirements of this classification through evidence of either a major, internationally 
recognized award or by meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that , 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a physical therapist and also seeks to work as an entrepreneur by organizing a mobile 
therapy clinic. She has substantial professional background in many areas of physical therapy, 
particularly hydrotherapy and Pilates. She specializes in treating sports injuries, orthopedic, 
rheumatologic, and neurological conditions in both the young and elderly populations. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not meet any of the three 
evidentiary criteria set forth in her petition. On appeal, the Petitioner renews her claim that she meets 
the eligibility criteria for memberships in associations in the field that demand outstanding 
achievement, performing a leading or critical role in distinguished organizations, and high salary in 
relation to others under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (viii), (ix). After reviewing the entirety of the 
evidence in the record, we conclude that she has not established that she meets the initial evidentiary 
requirements for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
Asserting that SCOPS imposed a stricter standard of proof than required in the regulations, the 
Petitioner contends the analysis in the decision was flawed. However, she does not point to specific 
examples of this within the denial. Except where a different standard is specified by law, the 
"preponderance of the evidence" is the standard of proof governing immigration benefit requests.

Analyzing the leading or critical role in distinguished organizations criterion set forth at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), we determine that the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility. To 
satisfy this criterion, the evidence must objectively meet the parameters of the applicable regulatory 
descriptions. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B). "[N]either USCIS nor [the] AAO may 
unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth." Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596F.3d1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010). 
Regarding the leading or critical role eligibility, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has 
performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, establishment, or a division or department 
of an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. A 
leading role means that a petitioner is ( or was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division, 
or department. Id. In contrast, a critical role means that a petitioner "has contributed in a way that is 
of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a 
division or department of the organization or establishment." Id. 
USCIS next determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. The word "distinguished" means "marked by eminence, 
distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, 
www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant factors include not only the relative size or longevity of an 
organization but also other information such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. 
The Petitioner submitted recommendation letters from hospital physicians, clients, a professor, an 
assistant, and colleagues. The record contains more than 10 letters of recommendation as well as an 
article and letter from I Ithat was submitted in response to a request for evidence (RFE). 
The Director found insufficient evidence of both the Petitioner's performance in a leading or critical 
role and the distinguished reputation of an organization. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that evidence of her outstanding work as a physical therapist 
demonstrates eligibility for this criterion. She points to the letters from physicians stating that her plan 
to create a mobile clinic would further overall improvement in healthcare in the United States and that 
she has positively contributed to the rehabilitation ofmany patients because ofher expertise in physical 
therapy. The letter from the physical therapist assistant and the therapist at I Iin Brazil 
also detail the Petitioner's qualifications and skills as a physical therapist. In addition, the record 
contains letters from the Petitioner's patients explaining how her knowledge, abilities and dedication 
to her work as a therapist helped them recover. Further, the Petitioner submitted a recommendation 
letter from one of her former professors stating that she "was a distinguished student in all the subjects 
that I tutored."

"A title, with appropriate matching duties, can help to establish that a role is ( or was), in fact, leading." 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. The letters of support indicate that the Petitioner is a 
physical therapist trained in a diverse array of therapeutical methodologies and has stayed abreast of 
cutting-edge methodologies in the field. However, these letters do not indicate how the Petitioner has

explains that the Petitioner has "played a key role in enhancing the quality and growth of the 
organization, consistently demonstrating commitment, excellence, and determination." However, the 
letter lacks details explaining how the organization grew as a result of the Petitioner's leading role. 
The writer does not discuss leadership duties or specific contributions that were significant to the 
outcome ofthe organization. Therefore, the Petitioner has not met this portion ofthe leading or critical 
role of an organization with a distinguished reputation. 
Similarly, the record does not indicate that the organizations with which the Petitioner is associated 
are distinguished. Although the letter writers speak highly of the Petitioner's skills and abilities as a 
therapist, they do not explain that the organization in which she was working has an outstanding or 
distinguished reputation. The article submitted about I Iindicates that it offers specialized 
physiotherapy to prominent sports clubs and has widespread recognition. Quality and specialized care 
for elite athletes does not equate to a distinguished organization. See Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-
360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *8 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) ("Successful businesses can be expected to 
have satisfied customers and the positive esteem of peers. But it takes more to have a 'distinguished 
reputation."'). 
Although the Petitioner claims eligibility for two additional criteria on appeal, relating to membership 
in associations in the field which demand outstanding achievement of their members at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(ii) and evidence of a high salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), we need not analyze these 
additional grounds. Because her argument fails on the leading or critical role criterion, it is 
unnecessary for us to make a decision on the additional grounds because she cannot numerically meet 
the required number of criteria. We therefore reserve the Petitioner's appellate arguments regarding 
these additional evidentiary criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision); see also Matter ofL-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to 
reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). Accordingly, we need 
not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not 
support a finding that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the 
classification sought. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
farther analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
4 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a pet1t10ner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
