Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a media producer, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S .C. § l l 53(b )(1 )(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record does not satisfy at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). If a criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily apply to 
an occupation, petitioners may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner described the endeavor as a plan "to continue to work in the field ofmedia & production 
in the USA, specifically in NY ... at _____________________ 
We note that the Petitioner's "detailed plans in the United States" directly conflict with other 
information in the record, such as her curriculum vitae, which casts doubt on the reliability and 
sufficiency of her plan description specifically, and on the reliability and sufficiency of the record in 
general. See Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582,591 (BIA 1988) (providing that doubt cast on any aspect

curriculum vitae indicates that she worked as a financial specialist from 2009-18 and that she did not

USCIS concluded that the record satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) but not the criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii), (v), (vii)-(x), noting that the Petitioner did not assert eligibility 
under the remaining criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that the record satisfies the criteria at 
8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii)-(v), (vii)-(x), but not the other criteria. For the reasons discussed 
below, the record does not satisfy at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R.

nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor."

endeavor include, but are not limited to, the criteria used to grant the prize or award, the national or

On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that her _______ Award for Best Informative Health

For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner has not sufficiently established that she satisfies this criterion. 
B. Published Material About the Alien 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Marketing materials, even seemingly objective content in 
major print publications that the alien or their employer paid for, created for the purpose of selling the 
alien's products or promoting their services are generally not considered published material about that 
person. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
USCIS observed that the Petitioner submitted apparent marketing materials, which omit the identity 
of the author( s) as required by the plain language of this criterion. Therefore, USCIS found that the 
record does not satisfy this criterion. 
3 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that "the regulation's 'author' reference is an identifying feature to 
help verify the published material; it does not impose a categorical requirement that each qualifying 
publication must list a personally named reporter." She also states that Turkish media typically publish 
articles without identifying individual authors, and she asserts that the publications' "absence of an 
individual byline is not a characteristic [she] can control and should not be held against her as an 
evidentiary defect." 
We consider the terms "author" and "publisher" using their ordinary, common meanings. See, e.g., 
Perrin v. United States, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979) ("A fundamental canon of statutory construction is 
that, unless otherwise defined, words will be interpreted as taking their ordinary, contemporary, 
common meaning ...."). Merriam-Webster defines "author" as "the writer of a literary work ( such as 
a book)." Author, Merriam-Webster.com, www.merriam-webster.com. In contrast, Merriam-Webster 
defines "publisher" as "one that publishes something especially: a person or corporation whose 
business is publishing." Publisher, Merriam-Webster.com, www.merriam-webster.com. 
We are not persuaded by the Petitioner's claim that USICS should interpret the regulation's specific 
requirement that evidence of published material about an alien "shall include the ... author of the 
material" as merely requiring the evidence to show its publisher-a concept for which she provides 
no legal or policy support. Relying on the commonly accepted meaning of the term "author," the 
regulation specifically requires petitioners to submit evidence that includes the writer of the published 
material, not merely the identity of the person or corporation whose business is publishing, the 
commonly accepted meaning of the distinct term "publisher." 
We acknowledge that the Petitioner submitted examples of articles that show they are published 
without identifying individual authors in Turkey despite having individual authors attributed when the 
same articles are published elsewhere, and that she submitted letters from workers in Turkish media 
who assert this is the norm in Turkey. However, we are not persuaded by the Petitioner's statement 
that published materials' omission of the author(s) should not be considered an evidentiary defect that 
precludes eligibility. Again, she does not identify any legal or policy support for the notion that the 
regulation requires petitioners to submit evidence that includes the writer of the published material 
unless the only material they purport is about them does not identify the individual who wrote it. The 
regulation's language that the evidence "shall include the ... author" does not contemplate exceptions 
in the event that the evidence does not include the author. 
Here, the Petitioner submitted various documents with certified English translations. Because none 
of the articles identify the individual who wrote the material, they do not satisfy the commonly 
accepted meaning of the regulation's plain language. Rather, as USCIS explained, the documents are 
indistinguishable from marketing material for which the Petitioner or her employer paid to be 
published. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Because the record does not 
contain published material about the Petitioner that includes the author of the material, we need not 
address whether the sources qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media. 
For the foregoing reasons, the record does not satisfy this criterion.

C. Original Contributions of Major Significance in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
USCIS observed that the record does not establish how media the Petitioner has produced has been of 
major significance in the field of media production. More specifically, USCIS noted that the record 
does not substantiate claims that the Petitioner has developed "a new production model," such as by 
evidence that her production framework has been adopted in the field. Therefore, USCIS concluded 
that the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she need not show that others in the field have adopted her 
production framework, and she claims that she developed and operationalized a programming model 
at a time when it "did not exist 'in this form' in the Turkish broadcast landscape." 
The record does not support the Petitioner's claim that she has created a "model [that] has become a 
new benchmark in health-oriented media production in Turkey." More specifically, the record does 
not show that entities other than her own create or have created "health-oriented media production in 
Turkey." Although this issue is material to the originality of the media the Petitioner has produced, it 
does not demonstrate that she created a model for a new benchmark followed by others in the field, as 
she asserts, given that the record does not show that there are other health-oriented media producers 
in Turkey, whether emulating her model or otherwise. Moreover, although the Petitioner references 
the bygone era of media seven years ago, the record does not establish what "the Turkish broadcast 
landscape" was prior to the Petitioner's media production beginning in 2019, in order to show whether 
programming models in fact "did not exist 'in this form"' at that time, as she alleges. 
The Petitioner submitted opinion letters; however, they provide limited information. As a matter of 
discretion, we may use opinion statements submitted by a petitioner as advisory. Matter ofCaron Int 'l, 
Inc., 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988). However, we may give an opinion less weight ifit is not 
in accord with other information in the record. Id. We are ultimately responsible for making the final 
determination regarding an individual's eligibility for the benefit sought; the submission of expert 
opinion letters is not presumptive evidence of eligibility. Id.; see also Fed. R. Evid. 702 (providing 
that an expert opinion may "help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in 
issue," not that it is evidence of fact itself). 
The opinion letters provide generalizations about media the Petitioner produced, but the letters are not 
supported by objective evidence about how media the Petitioner produced has had significance in the 
field. For example, an opinion letter from a columnist for the Sabah newspaper alleges that the 
Petitioner "has significantly enhanced public awareness and understanding of complex medical issues 
effectively demystifying topics that were once inaccessible to the average viewer." However, the 
record does not contain probative evidence of any particular medical issue, the extent of the public's 
awareness prior to the dissemination of media produced by the Petitioner, the extent of the public's 
awareness after such media was distributed, and a causal nexus between those media and any 
measurable change in public awareness. As another example, an opinion letter from another media 
producer claims that the Petitioner's company has "set[] benchmarks in the industry," but neither it 
nor the remainder of the record provides more detail about any particular aspect of media production 
5 
for which her company set a benchmark other than generally referenced "high standards" and its 
abstractly "innovative approach." As a further example, an opinion letter from an associate professor 
of medicine claims that the Petitioner's productions "have become widely recognized for bringing 
leading physicians and medical institutions into the public sphere in a clear, accurate, and engaging 
format." However, neither the opinion letter nor the remainder of the record substantiates that any 
particular portion ofthe Turkish-or any other-public recognizes media the Petitioner produced, and 
finds it to be clear, accurate, and engaging, as alleged. The other opinion letters bear similar 
deficiencies. 
Because the opinion letters do not address-and are not supported by-documentary evidence of fact 
in the record, they improperly purport to be evidence of fact themselves rather than helping us 
understand evidence of fact. See Fed. R. Evid. 702; see also Matter of Caron Int'!, Inc., 19 I&N 
Dec. at 795; 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(8)(i) (providing that USCIS must base its decision on information in 
the record, not information outside the record); 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l 1) (providing that a response to 
a request for evidence (RFE) is considered a request for a decision based on the record at that time). 
Accordingly, the opinion letters bear reduced probative value. See Matter ofCaron Int 'l, Inc., 19 I&N 
Dec. at 795. 
Based on the foregoing, the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
D. Leading or Critical Role 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
title, with appropriate matching duties, can help establish whether a role is, or was, leading. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). In determining whether an individual has 
performed in a critical role, we consider whether the record establishes the individual has contributed 
in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's 
activities or those of a division or department thereof. See generally id. Merriam-Webster defines 
"distinguished" as "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster.com, www.merriam-webster.com. Factors for determining whether an organization or 
establishment has a distinguished reputation include, but are not limited to, the relative size or 
longevity ofthe organization or establishment, the scale ofits customer base, relevant media coverage, 
credible national rankings, and funding. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

F. Commercial Success in the Performing Arts 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) requires "[e]vidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner does not participate in performing arts and that the record does 
not show how her work has had commercial success, such as through sales receipts; therefore, USCIS 
concluded the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that this criterion does not readily apply to her occupation because 
she produces media but she does not appear on camera and perform, citing 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4).4 
She further claims that, in "modem screen-based creative professions ... commercial success is 
measured through commissioning, distribution, and monetization rather than ticket sales" and that 
"financial evidence submitted show[ s] thatl Igenerated substantial revenue in 2024 from 
the production and broadcasting activities led by [ the Petitioner], revenue that functionally 
corresponds to 'receipts' in the producer context." 
Even ifthis criterion did not readily apply to the Petitioner's occupation, considering comparable, non­
physical distribution methods such as television broadcasts and internet streaming services, the record 
does not show commercial success. The record contains copies ofcommercial invoices for advertising 
fees, training video production fees, other program fees, editing and montage process fees. However, 
the record does not contextualize these fees to establish how they may indicate commercial success. 
For example, the record does not establish what other media production companies' fees are, to show 
whether the Petitioner's company's fees are above, below, or at the average industry rate. As another 
example, the record does not establish the total production costs associated with the given invoices­
whether on a project basis or in the aggregate-to show whether the Petitioner's company operated at 
a profit or a loss to create the content for which they received the fees and, thus, whether creating the 
content was a commercial success or failure. 
The Petitioner also submitted static images of videos streamed on the internet, with the number of 
times the videos were viewed. However, we note that the selective sampling of static images ofvideos 
does not provide insight into the amount of content she claims her company produced. And, more to 
the point, the record does not elaborate on how her company monetized viewership of the videos 
depicted to show how any given number of views indicates any particular degree of commercial 
success, as opposed to merely some degree of public awareness. 
Based on the foregoing, the record does not satisfy this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
