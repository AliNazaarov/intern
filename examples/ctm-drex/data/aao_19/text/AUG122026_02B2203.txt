Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a deputing general manager in real estate development and urban renewal projects, 
seeks first preference immigrant classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner has achieved 
sustained national or international acclaim and is among those individuals in the small percentage at 
the very top of her field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we conclude that USCIS did not offer a complete and accurate analysis ofthe submitted evidence. We 
will therefore withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with 
the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( I )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. The alien must seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area 
of extraordinary ability and the evidence must show that the alien's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of a beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Alternatively, the petitioner can submit 
evidence that the alien beneficiary meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly 
articles. If those standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once the initial evidence requirements have been met, the next step is a final merits determination, in 
which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of their field of endeavor. 
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022); 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
As previously indicated, USCIS 's decision did not offer a complete analysis or adequately explain the 
deficiencies in the evidence. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l)(i); see also Matter ofM-P-, 20 I&N Dec. 786 
(BIA 1994) (finding that a decision must fully explain the reasons for denying a motion to allow the 
respondent a meaningful opportunity to challenge the determination on appeal). 
In dismissing the appeal, USCIS addressed the three criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (iv), 
and (v), which require evidence of published material about the Petitioner in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media, the Petitioner's participation as a judge of the work of others 
in the field, and evidence of the Petitioner's original contributions of major significance in the field, 
respectively. However, while the record shows that the Petitioner claimed to have met five of the ten 
evidentiary criteria, she did not previously claim to have participated as a judge of the work of others 
in her field and only makes this claim for the first time on appeal, after USCIS made a favorable 
finding on this issue in the denial. 
In fact, in addition to the criteria pertaining to published material about the Petitioner and original 
contributions of major significance, which are listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and (v), the 
Petitioner claimed that she met the three criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), (viii), and (ix), 
which require authorship ofscholarly articles in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with a distinguished reputation, and 
commanding a high salary or other significantly high remuneration, respectively. The Petitioner did 
not list the judging criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) among her claimed list of criteria. 
Accordingly, USCIS's determination that the Petitioner "served as a reviewer" and therefore met the 
judging criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) is not consistent with the Petitioner's initially assertions 
or those made subsequently in response to a request for evidence. It is also worth noting that USCIS 
did not discuss the evidence within the scope of the judging criterion to explain what documentation 
was considered in reaching the favorable determination.

Further, users proceeded with a final merits determination, finding that the Petitioner "met at least 3 
of the 10 regulatory criteria," even though it made a favorable finding regarding only the judging 
criterion, which the Petitioner did not previously claim, while making unfavorable findings regarding 
two other criteria - published material about the Petitioner and original contributions of major 
significance. 
Also, despite the favorable finding regarding the judging criterion, users only made a brief reference 
to "the beneficiary's judging record" without discussing the facts, if any, that reflect the existence of 
such a record or explaining the basis for the favorable determination. Importantly, while users 
discussed the leading or critical role and the salary criteria in a final merits determination, it did not 
state that the Petitioner met these criteria nor did it clarify which three of the five claimed criteria the 
Petitioner is deemed to have met. This lack of clarity is particularly critical, given that one of the 
judging criterion which users determined as having been met, had not been included as one of the 
Petitioner's five claimed criteria. It therefore appears that the Petitioner may not have "met at least 3 
of the 10 regulatory criteria," as users has determined. 
Regardless, because users's decision did not adequately analyze the facts of the matter and clearly 
apply the regulatory standards, we will remand the matter for entry of a new decision. Our decision 
to withdraw the decision in no way indicates that the petition should be approved. However, ifusers 
reaches an adverse decision, the basis for that decision must be adequately explained. Id. users 
should request any additional evidence warranted and allow the Petitioner to submit such evidence 
within a reasonable period of time. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
