Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Exceptional Ability) 
The Petitioner, a research associate, seeks first preference immigrant classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 
8 U.S.C. § l 153(b )(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can show their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner's evidence did 
not satisfy at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). This matter is before us on 
appeal, which we review de novo. Matter ofChristo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). 
The Petitioner must establish her eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 
25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter this country to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
"Extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of 
the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a 
multi-part analysis. First, an alien can show sustained acclaim and the recognition of achievements in 
the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award) or 
qualifying documentation that satisfies at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
(including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial regulatory evidence requirements, we then consider the totality 
of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination).

Because the Petitioner has not indicated, and the record does not otherwise show, receipt of a major, 
internationally recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. She 
specifically claimed eligibility for three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (v), (vi), and SCOPS

remaining two criteria as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (v) relating to judging and original 
contributions of major significance, respectively, and denied the case, concluding that the Petitioner's 
documents did not satisfy at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. 2 
On appeal, she maintains that the record satisfies the above three criteria and alleges that SCOPS failed 
to properly consider all relevant evidence. We conclude that the evidence does not demonstrate that 
she has made original contributions of major significance in her field, and it thus does not satisfy the 
regulatory criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). Without satisfying this criterion, the Petitioner 
cannot establish that she has met at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria as regulation requires. 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires threshold evidence of"the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) first determines whether the alien has made 
original contributions in the field, and if so whether they were of "major significance." See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, F.(2)(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f-chapter-2. 
The Petitioner reasserts that she has made original contributions of major significance in the field of 
neurotrauma research. In support of this assertion, and to meet the regulatory criterion, she primarily 
relies on her coauthored papers published over the years since she obtained her Ph.D. in 2016; related 
citation records and ratings; experience in research, peer review, and conference presentation; and four 
recommendation letters from her colleagues. SCOPS did not address whether the Petitioner has made 
original contributions in the field, but concluded that the evidence did not demonstrate that her 
contributions were of major significance in the field. For the reasons discussed below, we agree. 
As stated, to satisfy 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v)'s criterion, the Petitioner must show not only that she 
has made original contributions, but that they have been of major significance in the field. For 
instance, she may present evidence that shows that her research findings have been widely discussed,

The Petitioner references her coauthored articles as another form of evidence that she claims satisfies

Further, open source information that generally compare her citation records with other "average

Lastly, the Petitioner reiterates that the fact that she has peer-reviewed other researchers' papers and 
was invited to speak at reputable conferences also show a major, significant contribution to the field. 
However, she did not provide evidence that she was in fact a speaker at any of the conferences she 
claims are well-respected in the field. Even if she actually made respectable conference appearances, 
the record lacks evidence about the nature of her presentation and of what (if any) impact her research 
work may have had in the field as a whole through such speaking engagements that may be indicative 
of major significance. Although there is evidence that she has peer-reviewed about a dozen drafts 
submitted for potential publication, the record indicates she may have self-registered and volunteered 
to serve as a peer reviewer, rather than being invited. Even if she was invited to serve as a peer 
reviewer for renowned journals through strict merit-based selection criteria and process, evidence of 
which is not included in the record, she does not provide any detail, or submit probative evidence on, 
the nature and extent of her peer review experience or the claimed quality and resulting impact of the 
drafts she reviewed. It is also unclear how general peer review experience specifically relates to her 
endeavor as a SCI and neurotrauma scientist and how being a peer reviewer, a common task for most

While we acknowledge the Petitioner's evidence, she did not submit sufficient probative evidence that 
her coauthored research findings during the last 12 years received any field-wide recognition or 
accolades; were adopted or rigorously replicated by objective parties; won any grant fundings; resulted 
in patents; or provoked widespread discussions in the field that may indicate high influence and 
significance of her work as a research associate. 
Considered together, the evidence consisting of the citations to the Petitioner's coauthored 
publications, the related citation statistics, and the recommendation letters from her colleagues, 
establish that the Petitioner has been a productive and successful research associate in the field, and 
that her published data and findings have been cited and relied upon by others in their own research. 
However, the evidence does not demonstrate that her contributions in the field of SCI and neurotrauma 
research were of major significance. The Petitioner therefore has not met her burden of satisfying the 
threshold evidentiary criterion as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring them to establish 
sustained national or international acclaim and present extensive documentation to demonstrate their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. Here, the 
Petitioner's inability to meet the above regulatory criterion means that she is unable to satisfy the initial 
evidentiary threshold at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).4 Therefore, we cannot conclude that she is an alien of 
extraordinary ability and need not farther analyze the merits under the Act. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-1120; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, F.Supp.3d, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) 
(finding that the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").
