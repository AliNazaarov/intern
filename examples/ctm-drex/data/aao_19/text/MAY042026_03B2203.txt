Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner - a vice president/information lead architect in the financial services industry who 
focuses on data compliance, data governance, and risk management - requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS found that the Petitioner met the 
requested visa category's initial evidentiary requirements. But SCOPS concluded that he did not 
demonstrate acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage at his field's 
very top. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' final merits determination lacked analysis. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that the record does not establish the Petitioner's claimed extraordinary ability in 
his field. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner earned a bachelor oftechnology degree in computer science from 
a university in his home country of India. He has since gained about 15 years' experience in 
interpreting and analyzing data to develop business solutions. Since 201 7, he has worked in the United 
States for his current employer, a financial services company. He states that his work has helped the 
company to effectively monitor transactions for fraud, improve its customer identification process, 
and automate data validation and reporting. He says that his work has not only improved the 
company's operational efficiency and data integrity, but has also ensured its adherence to global 
financial standards and reduced its risk exposure. 
The Petitioner states that, in the short term, he seeks to continue working for his U.S. employer, 
designing and implementing compliance and risk management frameworks driven by artificial 
intelligence (AI). He says that, in the long term, he intends to work in executive, technical business 
positions for major U.S. enterprises. He states: "By driving innovation in regulatory technology and 
risk management, I aim to create a more secure, transparent, and fraud-resistant financial ecosystem." 
B. The Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of his: 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in his field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for his services. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi), (ix). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he also met the following five evidentiary requirements:

• Nationally or internationally recognized awards or prizes for excellence in his field; 
• Membership in associations in his field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• Published material about him and his work in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media; 
• Original contributions of major significance in his field; and

The Petitioner, however, has already met the requisite minimum amount of three evidentiary criteria. 
We therefore need not determine whether he has met any others. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S.

We will next proceed to the final merits determination. 
e. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. users must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
l. Judge of Others' Work 
The record shows that the Petitioner "peer reviewed" research papers of more than 20 others for an 
international journal and served as a reviewer at a computing and electronics conference. He also 
submitted evidence that he judged international awards for excellence in artificial intelligence and 
cybersecurity. On appeal, he contends: "[T]he record shows sustained reliance on [his] expert 
judgment by independent institutions that curate, screen, and validate technical work and professional 
achievement." 
The record, however, lacks evidence that his judging activities generated qualifying acclaim and 
recognition. For example, the Petitioner has not shown that the journal for which he reviewed research 
papers ranks highly compared to others. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 
(E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding users' finding that a petitioner did not demonstrate their claimed 
extraordinary ability when they did not offer evidence, for example, of having "served in editorial 
positions for distinguished journals or publications"). 
Also, the Petitioner has not established that the research papers he reviewed for the journal and at the 
conference were in his stated field of financial data compliance, governance, and risk management. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act (requiring acclaim and recognition "in the field"); cf 8 e.F.R. 
3 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) (requiring evidence of participation as a judge of others' work "in the same or an 
allied field ofspec(fication for which class(fication is sought") (emphasis added). 
Further, the record shows that all the Petitioner's judging activities occurred in 2024 or 2025. Thus, 
contrary to the Act, these activities would not generate "sustained" acclaim throughout his career. See 
section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring "sustained national or international acclaim"); 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(A)(l) ("In determining whether the beneficiary has enjoyed 'sustained' national or 
international acclaim, the officer should consider that such acclaim must be maintained."); see also 
Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *10 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding 
that a petitioner's 15 peer reviews within three months of the petition's filing did not reflect "a career 
of acclaimed work in the field") ( emphasis added). 
2. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his authorship or co-authorship of five research articles. He 
argues that his published articles show that his "work product is sufficiently rigorous and original to 
pass independent review by knowledgeable evaluators" and that he "is contributing to field-wide 
discourse." 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that all his research articles are in his stated field of 
financial data compliance, governance, and risk management. Three articles appear to focus on the 
use of "deep learning" to predict stock market fluctuations, and one concerns chess as a "visualization 
tool" for corporate leadership. See section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring acclaim and 
recognition "in the field"). 
Also, the Petitioner submitted evidence that, at the time of the petition's filing, researchers had cited 
his five scholarly articles a total of only one time. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) (stating that 
evidence of a high total citation rate relative to others in the field may indicate extraordinary ability). 
Further, all the articles indicate their publication in 2024 or 2025. Thus, they would not generate 
"sustained" national or international acclaim as required by the requested immigrant visa 
classification. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act; see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(l ). 
3. High Salary 
The Petitioner demonstrated that his base annual salary of$165,000 exceeds the average annual salary 
of $94,082 for data engineers in his geographical area. He argues: 
High remuneration is a highly probative "market validation" factor in EB-1 A 
adjudications because it reflects what sophisticated employers are willing to pay for a 
beneficiary's expertise relative to available alternatives. In fields like enterprise data 
governance and financial compliance (where errors can carry regulatory, reputational, 
and systemic risk), high compensation, more than a function of tenure, reflects the 
value and scarcity of the skillset.

We do not find, however, that the Petitioner's high salary, alone, establishes his claimed extraordinary 
ability. See Hamal v. United States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *3 
(D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (affirming USCIS' finding that "salary evidence alone was insufficient to 
establish [ a petitioner] as a singularly exceptional artist due to the other gaps in his evidence"); 
Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) 
("[W]hile Plaintiff's salary is high, it is not so high as to indicate that he has risen to the very top of 
his field.") 
4. Awards 
The record shows the Petitioner's receipt of three international business technology awards. He 
contends: 
[T]he awards evidence materially strengthens the totality showing of sustained acclaim 
because it is a form ofexternal, merit-based recognition that corroborates [his] standing 
beyond his employer. The fact that multiple independent awards programs recognized 
[him] further supports that his achievements are visible and distinguished within the 
professional ecosystem relevant to his field. 
(Emphasis in the original). 
The Petitioner's awards indicate recognition from the international organizations that issued them. 
But he has not shown that, beyond the issuing organizations, the awards generated national or 
international acclaim. See Donskoy v. USCIS, No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *5 
(N.D. Cal. Nov. 11, 2021) ("[W]hat matters for the purpose of the visa classification is whether the 
award brought [a petitioner] individual acclaim."). 
Also, all the awards indicate their issuance in 2025. Thus, they would not demonstrate "sustained" 
national or international acclaim. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act; see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(A)(l). 
5. Memberships in Associations 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in four professional computing and technology­
related associations, including his senior membership in the Institute of Electrical and Electronics 
Engineers (IEEE). But he has not demonstrated that these memberships generated national or 
international acclaim. See Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 820 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (upholding 
USCIS' finding that a petitioner's IEEE membership did not show extraordinary ability because its 
eligibility requirements were not stringent enough and IEEE senior membership did not reflect 
sustained national or international acclaim). 
Also, the record indicates that the Petitioner obtained membership in all the groups in 2025. Thus, the 
memberships would not reflect "sustained" acclaim. See section 203(b )( I )(A )(i) of the Act; see

6. Published Material 
The Petitioner submitted copies of two articles published in the United States about him and his work. 
But he did not demonstrate that the publication of two articles about him over a 15-plus-year career 
sufficiently reflects an alien of extraordinary ability in his field. See Hamal v. US Dep 't ofHomeland 
Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal 
v. United States Dep 't of Homeland Sec., supra (upholding USCIS' finding that a plaintiff did not 
show that "his press coverage is indicative of a level of success consistent with being" at the top of his 
field). 
Also, both articles about the Petitioner indicate their publication in 2025. Thus, they would not 
generate "sustained" acclaim. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(A)(l). 
7. Contributions to His Field 
As proof of contributions to his field, the Petitioner submitted letters and a copy of an Indian patent 
application. The materials indicate his invention ofan AI framework for identifying abnormal patterns 
in credit card transactions to prevent financial fraud. 2 Evidence also shows that he developed a 
platform offering advanced automation of regulatory reporting and real-time understanding of 
potentially fraudulent financial behavior. 
Letters from university professors and executives describe the Petitioner's contributions as significant 
in his field. For example, an Indian university rector of commerce and management studies stated that 
results from the pilot deployment of the Petitioner's platform showed that it significantly improved 
compliance processing times and precision in anomaly detection. The rector claims that "several 
organizations" in the financial and enterprise data sectors have implemented parts of the Petitioner's 
platform and that academic researchers have referred to the platform's data governance and 
explainability modules as "a case study in practical AI deployment." Also, an Indian computer science 
and engineering professor asserted: "[The Petitioner's] solutions represent first-of-their-kind 
deployments that are now used as a blueprint across the provision of global, enterprise-scale financial 
services." 
The Petitioner, however, did not corroborate the letters' information. The record lacks documentary 
evidence that organizations beyond his employer have implemented parts of his platform or that, at 
the time of the petition's filing, academic researchers had referenced his platform. See Bodhankar, 
2020 WL 777211, at *5 ("Letters written on Plaintiffs behalf describe Plaintiff's contributions to the 
field as original and unique, yet they fail to establish that they resulted in significant acclaim."); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(3) ("[T]he statements made by the witnesses should be 
corroborated by documentary evidence in the record.") The Petitioner therefore has not demonstrated 
that these contributions have generated acclaim and recognition sufficient to place among the small 
percentage at his field's top.

management, we will not discuss it.

8. Leading or Critical Role 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has played a critical role in improving his employer's anti­
money laundering and "know your customer" operations. But the record falls short ofestablishing his 
claimed place among the small percentage at his field's very top. The record lacks sufficient detail 
and context to demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) 
("In all cases, the petitioner must provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence and 
considerations to demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's 
extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances.") 
In sum, the Petitioner's evidence of acclaim and recognition is insufficient to demonstrate his claimed 
place among the small percentage at his field's very top, and the record shows that he received most 
of his accolades over the past two years, preventing a finding of the requisite "sustained" national or 
international acclaim. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). The legislature indicated 
that this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the 
very top of their field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner has met the minimum number of evidentiary 
criteria and has demonstrated his receipt of some acclaim and recognition in his field. But, considering 
the evidence as a whole, he has not established his claimed place among the small percentage at his 
field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386 (5th Cir. 2022) ("[G]iven the lofty bar for 
extraordinary ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined 
that [a petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 
954 (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not automatically meet 
the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not established his claimed place among the small percentage at his field's very 
top. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
