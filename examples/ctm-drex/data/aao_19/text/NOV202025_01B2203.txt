Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a post-doctoral researcher, requests classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § l l 53(b )(1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as 
required. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(b)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

For the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), the Petitioner must submit evidence of his "original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, or business-related contributions ofmajor significance in the field." First, 
we determine whether a petitioner has made original contributions in their field. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Next, we examine whether 
the original contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
The Petitioner identified his work "minimizing the weight of aircraft wings by designing internal 
structures with curvilinear spars and ribs," using electron beam free form fabrication (EBF3), the 
addition of cobalt to speed up rhenium deposition, determination of the speed of diffusion of proton 
and copper ions under various conditions, and development of the MadDE algorithm for numerical 
optimization to comprise the original contributions he has made to the field. And his self-provided 
list is supported by publications and citations. So, we conclude the record demonstrates the Petitioner 
has made original contributions to his field. 
But our analysis cannot stop there. We also must evaluate if the claimed original contributions have 
exerted major significance to the Petitioner's field. And it is here that the Petitioner's claim falters 
because the evidence in the record does not establish that his original contributions have major 
significance in his field. For example, the independent advisory opinions only provide general 
statements about the Petitioner's contributions, stating for example that he has "addressed complex 
challenges" with EBF3, "improved the original control parameters" using the LSHADE algorithm and 
MadDE's tripartite attributes, or "optimize[d] the electrodeposition of copper and other metals." But 
the mere explanation ofthe nature of a petitioner's contributions will not also describe the significance 
of the person's contribution to the field and if that significance can be classified as "major." And 
although the authors of the independent advisory opinions the Petitioner submitted express a personal 
appreciation for the Petitioner's contributions, they do not describe the level of significance the 
Petitioner's contributions have exerted on the Petitioner's field writ large. 
The same can be said about the scholarly publications memorializing his contributions and the citation 
history stemming from the scholarly publications that the Petitioner leans on to showcase his claimed 
assertion of the major significance of his contributions. Whilst publication and citation can 
demonstrate some noteworthiness of the claimed contribution to the field, it is not sufficiently 
preponderant by itself to the question of whether the claimed contribution has exerted major

significance on the field. And the Petitioner's reliance on bibliometrics in the form of Google Scholar, 
Clarivate Analytics' Essential Science Indicators (ESI), and OpenAlex data does not demonstrate the 
significance ofthe contributions the Petitioner claims. For example, the Google Scholar data is silent 
about how many citations listed therein were self-citations. And the ESI data claimed one of the 
Petitioner's articles was amongst the top 10% of most cited articles in engineering but did not indicate 
whether self-citations were factored into determination. Moreover, the documentation from ESI the 
Petitioner submitted states that "[ c ]itation frequency is highly skewed, with many infrequently cited 
papers and relatively few highly cited papers. Consequently, citation rates should not be interpreted 
as representing the central tendency of the distribution." Further, the Petitioner's assertion that data 
he claimed was derived from OpenAlex reflecting that he was in the "top 1 %" of his field is not 
supported by evidence that demonstrates how OpenAlex collects, collates, and evaluates its data. So, 
in sum, we cannot glean the significance of the Petitioner's claimed original contributions from the 
citation data he submits because it does not reliably describe the level of discourse or commentary the 
original contributions have generated in the Petitioner's field. 
Similarly, the "global influence" the Petitioner claims based on the citation of his work "across 25 
countries" is not contextualized with material, relevant, or probative evidence in the record. For 
example, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how citation by scholars "across 25 countries" 
demonstrates the significance of original contributions. Nor does the Petitioner establish that citation 
by unaffiliated scholars is related to the significance of his original contributions. 
Moreover, the record contains descriptions of what the National Aeronautics and Space 
Administration, U.S. Army Combat Capabilities Development Command, Badische Anilin- und 
Sodafabrik (BASF) and the National Science Foundation are. And in the case of BASF, there is some 
evidence of correspondence by BASF with the Petitioner and his colleague. But, as a whole, the 
evidence does not describe the claimed fonding the Petitioner received, its amount, or any other 
pertinent detail to permit an evaluation of its significance. And the record does not demonstrate why 
fonding from the constellation of sources the Petitioner identifies would demonstrate the level of 
significance of his contributions. 
A petitioner must show that his or her original contributions rise to the level of major significance in 
the field as a whole. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (citing Visinscaia v. 
Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134). The Petitioner did not establish his original contributions are of major 
significance in his field. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner did not show he meets this criterion. 
B. Summary and Reserved Issue 
Although SCOPS determined the Petitioner meets the criteria for participation, either individually or 
on a panel as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field contained at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv), the Petitioner has not established he meets the criterion relating to original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field 
contained at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). He therefore cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement 
for three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Consequently, we reserve evaluation ofthe Petitioner's 
claim of authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or 
4 
other major media under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) and need not provide the type of final merits 
determination referenced in Kazarian. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) 
(holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision. 
Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not 
support a finding that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the 
classification sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. 
Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league 
level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) 
(finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't 
o_fHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316 at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), ajf'd, 
2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan 41, 2023) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very 
small percentage of prospective immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), 
No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3,2020) (citing Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non­
Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished 
individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F.Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding 
that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for the visa 
as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress, H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has 
garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that he is one of the small percentage who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
