Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an animation director, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish the Petitioner met the 
requirements for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. The matter is now before us 
on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 20 l 0) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijalv. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner indicates that he is an animation director and intends to contribute to the growth and 
innovation of the animation industry in the United States. Because the Petitioner has not asserted or 
established that he has received a major, internationally recognized award at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), 
he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the evidence submitted with the initial petition and in 
response to a request for evidence, did not establish eligibility for the benefit sought. SCOPS 
determined the Petitioner did not satisfy any of the five claimed evidentiary criteria, membership, 
published materials, display, leading or critical role, and salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), (vi), 
(viii), and (ix), respectively. The Petitioner asserts on appeal that he meets these five criteria and that 
the record establishes his eligibility for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. He 
contends that SCOPS erred in the analysis of the evidence, disregarded counsel's legal explanations, 
and failed to accept the use of comparable evidence. Upon review, we determine SCOPS properly 
concluded the Petitioner did not show his eligibility for at least three of the claimed criteria and did 
not meet the requirements for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
A. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l),

B. Leading or Critical Roles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." For 
a leading role, a petitioner must establish that the person is ( or was) a leader within the organization or 
establishment or a division or department thereof. For a critical role, a petitioner must establish that the 
person has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. 1 
The Petitioner requested eligibility for this criterion through comparable evidence. He argued that this 
criterion does not readily apply to his occupation as an animation director "due to the unique nature 
of his profession and the structure of his work within the animation industry." As part of his claim, 
the Petitioner contended that his "work as an animation director is inherently project-based. Like 
many other directors, while he has a registered company he primarily operates as a self-employed 
professional, collaborating on individual productions rather than being employed by a singular 
organization or establishment." The Petitioner indicated that the animation industry often relies on 
highly skilled directors who contribute to specific projects independently and as a result, "the criterion 
of serving a leading role for organizations or establishments is not easily applicable to his line of 
work." Instead, "his contributions should be assessed based on the prominence and success of the 
projects he has directed, which more accurately reflect his achievements and influence in his field" 
and the comparable evidence "should focus on his leadership and critical contributions to specific 
projects or productions with distinguished reputations." The Petitioner also asserted that the 
regulations and policy relating to the 0-1 nonimmigrant classification "offer a more fitting framework 
for evaluating [his] achievements." 
In denying the petition, SCOPS found, in part, that the Petitioner did not meet his burden ofexplaining 
why the regulatory requirements for any of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) are not readily 
applicable to his occupation and how the submitted evidence is comparable to the required objective

Upon review, the Petitioner has not sufficiently demonstrated why he cannot offer evidence that meets

leadership and critical contributions to specific projects or productions with distinguished reputations,

the record lacks evidence to establish that other animation directors could not meet this criterion by 
showing they performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a 
distinguished reputation. As such, the Petitioner does not establish that he satisfies this criterion. 
C. Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 4 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility for this criterion based on the income he received from 2020 to 2024 
as the animation director on multiple projects. He indicated that rather than looking at the 
compensation amounts listed in each of the contracts, a more accurate representation of his salary is 
the average compensation he received in 2023 and 2024, a total of CNY ¥670,787 (Chinese Yuan). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of seven contracts from 2020 to 2024, a bank statement, and 
comparative salary data for animation executive directors and senior level animation directors. In 
denying the petition, SCOPS found, in part, that the record did not contain detailed supporting 
documentation that reflected the Petitioner's actual earnings for any given period of time as an

unable to properly compare his earnings data with others performing similar work during the same 
time period. The Petitioner must present evidence of objective earnings data showing that they have 
commanded a "high salary" or "significantly high remuneration" in comparison with those performing 
similar work during the same time period. See Matter o_fPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 
1994) (considering professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers). He has not done 
so here and thus does not establish that he satisfies this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Although the Petitioner claims he meets three criteria, because his arguments fail on any single 
criterion discussed above he cannot numerically meet the required number of criteria and it is 
unnecessary for us to make a decision on these additional grounds. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the 
initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve these issues 
and need not further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also 
Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at* 10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the 
regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks 
an extraordinary ability visa"). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 ( 1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
