Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, the owner of an international travel company, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(I )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS found that the Petitioner met the 
category's initially required evidentiary criteria. But, in a final merits determination, SCOPS 
concluded that he did not demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. On appeal, the 
Petitioner contends that SCOPS committed procedural and legal errors. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he did not satisfy the requisite number of initial evidentiary criteria and that, 
even if he did, he would not have demonstrated his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. We will 
therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (implementing a two-step extraordinary 
ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
The record indicates that the Petitioner first served as a tour guide at age 10, working for tips near his 
home in Turkey. Now an adult, he has worked foll-time in the travel and tourism field for more than 
the past ten years. While studying business management at a Spanish university in 2012, he started a 
tourism company that he operated for about seven years. He then worked as a coordinator of one 
travel company and managing partner of another before founding his current tourism business in 
Turkey in 2022. 
Now in the United States, the Petitioner stated his intent to expand his business, which he claims 
already has two offices in this country. He stated: 
By residing in the United States, I will have the opportunity to create sustainable 
tourism platforms that empower communities, promote cross-cultural dialogue, and 
support economic recovery in diverse regions. My commitment is to use tourism not 
only as a business venture but as a transforrnative force for connection, innovation, and 
prosperity. 
B. The Alternative Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
SCOPS found that the Petitioner satisfied four criteria, submitting evidence of his: 
• Membership in associations in his field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for services in the field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (v), (vi), (ix).

The record supports SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner submitted evidence of his authorship of a

l. Membership in Associations 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for 
which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
If an association has multiple membership levels, evidence must show that, to obtain the petitioner's 
level, recognized national or international experts judged the petitioner as having attained outstanding 
achievements in their field. 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 2. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of 2025 certificates from a U.S.-based procurement/tourist 
management association designating him a "fellow chartered tourism manager" and a member of an 
association chapter. Copies of online association information states that, to qualify as a fellow, a 
candidate must demonstrate, among other things, "[a] track record of significant achievements in 
supply chain or tourism management, such as: 1) Developing innovative solutions; 2) Contributing to 
academic or professional research; [or] 3) Leading impactful initiatives or organizations." The online 
materials also state that fellow applications are reviewed by the association's "Fellowship 
Committee." 
As SCOPS' request for additional evidence (RFE) stated, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that, to 
obtain a fellow membership in the association, recognized national or international experts in their 
fields judged his achievements. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). The online materials state that the 
association's fellowship committee reviews applications for fellows. But the record neither specifies 
who serves on the fellowship committee nor demonstrates that the committee members are recognized 
experts in their fields. 
The Petitioner's RFE response noted that the online association materials state: 
Among the Fellows are: Entrepreneurs who have revolutionized their fields[;] 
Innovators who have introduced groundbreaking ideas and practices[;] Researchers and 
Academics who have contributed to the body of knowledge through thought leadership 
and scholarly work[; and] Business Leaders and Industry Professionals who have 
demonstrated outstanding achievements in their careers.

presentation of a single piece of evidence in a specific evidentiaiy categmy may be sufficient." 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B).

But the record does not specifically indicate that fellows in these specific roles, or even in general, 
serve on the fellowship committee. The record does not identify the committee members or 
demonstrate their recognized expertise in their fields. See Nelson v. USCIS, No. CV 21-10334-LTS, 
2022 WL 20583561, at *5 (D. Mass. June 15, 2022) (stating that 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) "demands 
membership in an organization that requires 'outstanding achievement' as judged by experts, and not 
merely membership in selective organizations in the field"). 
The Petitioner also submitted copies of online information about membership on a Turkish foreign 
economic relations board and a list of the board's business councils and their membership fees. He 
claims to be a member of the board and one of its business councils. But the information submitted 
does not state his membership on either. 
Also, the online information identifies board and business council members as companies, not 
individuals. The information states: "Companies wishing to become members of the Business 
Councils must first become members of the [board] and then apply for membership in one or more 
Business Councils of their choice." The materials do not indicate that board or business council 
members can include individuals. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) (requiring "documentation of the 
alien's membership in associations in the field") ( emphasis added). Further, the record does not 
demonstrate that recognized national or international experts judged board or council members. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 2 ("The petitioner must show that membership 
in the association requires outstanding achievements in the field for which classification is sought, as 
judged by recognized national or international experts.") (emphasis added). 
The Petitioner did not demonstrate his membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding 
achievements as judged by recognized national or international experts. We will therefore withdraw 
SCOPS' contrary finding. 
2. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, the Petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of [his] original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the 
field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The Petitioner points largely to evidence of his purported development and implementation of an "AI 
[ artificial intelligence ]-powered tourism system" that he describes as "a paradigm-shifting innovation. 
By driving a 50% increase in regional tourism and being adopted by international industry leaders, it 
has redefined how the tourism sector integrates artificial intelligence." 
But, as SCOPS' RFE stated, the Petitioner has not provided details about the AI system or 
demonstrated its major significance to his field. The record also does not support its purported 
adoption by "international industry leaders." The Petitioner submitted copies of media articles about 
himself and his work that mention an AI system. But, in the articles, only the Petitioner comments on 
it. His opinion alone is insufficient to show the system's importance to the field. See Goncharov v. 
Allen, No. 3:21-CV-1372-B, 2022 WL 17327304, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 29, 2022) (citations omitted) 
("Courts have routinely affirmed agency decisions that held [8 C.F.R.] § 204.5(h)(3)(v) 'requires 
substantial influence beyond one's employers, clients, or customers."') The Petitioner also submitted 
4 
support letters from six people. But none of the letters mentions the AI system, let alone explains its 
purported major significance to the field. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 
568850, at *9 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) ("[T]he agency may reject supporting letters that lack 'specific 
evidence' about how the plaintiff's contribution was a major contribution.") 
The Petitioner further cited his campaign to promote travel to lesser-visited regions of Turkey, his 
"empathy-based" customer service model, and his focus on environmentally sustainable tourism as 
examples of other original contributions he has made to his field. But the record also does not explain 
how these contributions have major significance to the industry. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 
3d 168, 187-88 (D.N.J. 2021) ( citations omitted) (stating that: a contribution's impact "should extend 
beyond the petitioner's locality or a handful of participants in the field;" "'[c]onclusory statements"' 
about the value of the contribution are insufficient; and the AAO may reasonably ask for 
'corroborating' evidence"). 
The Petitioner did not submit evidence that he has made original contributions of major significance 
in his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
3. Commandment of a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration for Services 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence that the[y] ha[ve] 
commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in 
the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted: proof that he solely owns his company; a letter from an accountant stating 
that the business generated revenues of $820,153.69 in 2023 and $1,195,882.10 in 2024; and copies 
ofbank statements in those years showing payment transfers to him from his company. The Petitioner 
claimed that "[e]very dollar of that revenue has been disbursed to [him]." He submitted evidence that 
the average monthly salary of a Turkish chief executive officer (CEO) is $5,250, with a maximum of 
up to $9,200. The Petitioner claimed that he received $9,665 a month. He therefore argued that his 
compensation is high relative to others in his field. 
The Petitioner, however, has not reliably demonstrated his compensation amount. First, he has not 
explained how he received all his company's revenues. Most companies have operating expenses that 
the businesses must deduct from their revenues before realizing any profits. The Petitioner has not 
explained how his company operates without expenses. 
Also, the bank statements indicate that payments to the Petitioner from his company totaled 
$224,445.09 in 2023 and $92,021.53 in 2024. 3 These figures do not match the annual revenue amounts 
that he claimed to receive in the corresponding years. Further, he has not explained how he calculated 
his monthly salary of $9,665. For example, ifhe received all his company's revenues of $1,195,882.10 
in 2024 as he claims, he would have received almost $100,000 a month ($1,195,882.10/12). In 
contrast, if he received $92,021.53 from his company in 2024 as the bank statements indicate, his 
monthly salary would have been only about $7,668 ($92,021.53/12). 
3 We calculated payments shown in euros and Turkish lira based on exchange rates listed on December 16, 2025. See Xe, 
www.xe.com/en-us/. 
5 
Because of the unexplained discrepancies in the Petitioner's compensation level, he has not 
demonstrated his commandment of a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for his 
services relative to others in his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
The Petitioner met only one of the four evidentiary requirements that SCOPS found he did. We will 
therefore determine whether he satisfies any of the additional criteria he claims to have met. 
4. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards 
This criterion requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of a "World's Leading Entrepreneur" award from a 2024 travel 
conference and a "Best Tourism Company" award in 2025. 
The record indicates that the Petitioner's business - not the Petitioner, himself - won the "Best 
Tourism Company" award. The plain language of this regulatory criterion requires "the alien's 
receipt" of awards. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) (emphasis added); see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 1 ("The description of this type of evidence in the regulation indicates 
that the focus should be on the person's receipt of the awards or prizes, as opposed to the employer's 
receipt of the awards or prizes.") The Petitioner's RFE response argued that "[h]is presence and 
acknowledgement at the award ceremony confirm that this was not merely a company-level 
recognition, but rather a reflection ofhis individual entrepreneurial leadership and contributions." But, 
based on the criterion's plain language and USCIS policy, the "Best Tourism Company" award does 
not meet this evidentiary requirement because the award was not in the Petitioner's name. Also, a 
copy of an online article about the award ceremony reports that, contrary to the Petitioner's argument, 
he "could not attend the ceremony because he was abroad." 
Unlike the "Best Tourism Company" honor, the "World's Leading Entrepreneur" award is in the 
Petitioner's name. But, as SCOPS' RFE stated, he has not demonstrated that this award was 
"nationally or internationally recognized." He submitted copies of online materials and an online 
article stating that 42,465 tourism workers voted for the travel conference awards in 40 categories over 
five days in 2024 and that representatives of 250 companies attended the awards' ceremony. But the 
record does not demonstrate that the online article has a national or international readership or 
otherwise indicate that people outside the travel conference recognize the award. See Krasniqi, 558 F. 
Supp. 3d at 182 ( citation omitted) (requiring evidence meeting this criterion to "show 'how a larger 
audience viewed [the] awards'"). 
For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner has not submitted documentation of his receipt of nationally 
or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor. We will 
therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary requirement. 
5. Published Material About the Petitioner 
This criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought." 
6 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the material, and 
any necessary translation." Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of published materials in professional publications about him and his 
work in his field. He provided printouts of online information about the publications in Turkey, 
Argentina, and Brazil that sufficiently identify their intended audiences as professionals in the travel 
and tourism industry. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 3 (stating that the intended 
audience is a relevant factor for determining a publication's professional nature). 
The Petitioner met the evidentiary requirement at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). We will therefore 
withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
6. Display of Work at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The Petitioner's RFE response included evidence of his participation in travel and tourism fairs, 
arguing that the materials are "comparable evidence" under this criterion. As previously noted, a 
petitioner may submit comparable evidence to meet an evidentiary criterion if the requirement does 
not "readily apply" to their occupation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)( 4). "When evaluating such comparable 
evidence, officers must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's 
occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that 
regulation." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), "Comparable evidence." 
We agree with the Petitioner that evidence of the display of work at artistic exhibitions or showcases 
does not readily apply to his occupation in this matter. He claims that, through "live demonstrations" 
and "product showcases" at travel fairs, he displayed AI-based tourism applications that he developed. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of photographs of himself and his companies' booths at the fairs, and 
general information about the events. But none of these materials show or state his display or 
demonstration of AI applications at the fairs. Thus, the Petitioner has not established display of his 
claimed work at the fairs. His evidence therefore does not truly compare to display of work at artistic 
exhibitions or showcases. 
7. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first considers whether a petitioner performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, 
establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is (or was) a leader within 
an organization, establishment, division, or department. Id. In contrast, a critical role means "that the 
person has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities." Id. 
7 
users next considers whether an organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. The word "distinguished" ordinarily means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, supra. Relevant considerations include not only an organization's relative 
size and longevity but also other factors, such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage of it. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his leadership role in founding and expanding his current travel 
business. The record shows that he clearly leads the company. 
But the Petitioner has not established the claimed distinguished reputation of his company. He 
submitted evidence that he and the business have received awards and have been the subject of media 
articles. But, founded in 2022, the company has had only a short existence. The business has not 
demonstrated growth or excellence over a long period. Also, with reported 2024 revenues of about 
$1.1 million, the company is relatively small, and the record lacks evidence that it employs anyone 
except the Petitioner. Further, the evidence does not indicate that the company has a large or 
prestigious customer base. Looking at these factors together, the evidence does not demonstrate the 
company's distinguished reputation. 4 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that he performed in leading or critical roles for 
organizations with distinguished reputations. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this 
evidentiary criterion. 
The Petitioner has only met two of the ten initial evidentiary criteria: published materials about him 
and his work in professional publications; and authorship of a scholarly article in his field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (vi). We therefore need not make a final merits determination. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) (requiring a petitioner to meet at least three alternative evidentiary criteria); 
see also INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). Nevertheless, 
even ifthe Petitioner met the requisite three initial evidentiary criteria, he would not have demonstrated 
his claimed extraordinary ability in his field in a final merits determination. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. users must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id.

Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8.

On appeal, the Petitioner contends that, before denying the petition in a final merits determination, 
SCOPS should have issued a second RFE or a notice of intent to deny [NOID] the petition. He notes 
that SCOPS' RFE notified him of deficiencies regarding the evidentiary requirements but not 
regarding the final merits determination. He states: "USCIS denied the petition based on final merits 
grounds that were never raised in the RFE, thereby issuing an adverse decision on a basis for which 
the Petitioner had no prior notice or opportunity to respond." 
USCIS, however, generally need not issue even one RFE. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(8)(iii) (allowing 
the Agency to deny a benefit request without issuing an RFE or NOID). USCIS must notify a 
petitioner of derogatory information of which they are unaware before basing a petition's denial on 
that information. 8 C.F .R. § 103 .2(b )(16)(i). But the Petitioner does not allege - nor does the record 
indicate-that SCOPS based the petition's denial on derogatory information ofwhich he was unaware. 
Also, the record does not support the Petitioner's contention that SCOPS deprived him of an 
opportunity to address the final merits determination. His RFE response includes a "Final Merits 
Determination" section, reviewing his evidence and how it purportedly "demonstrates his 
extraordinary ability in the travel agency and tourism field." 
The Petitioner also notes that, in its final merits determination, SCOPS "readjudicated" evidence. For 
example, SCOPS found that the only submitted article that the Petitioner wrote qualified as "a 
scholarly article[] in the field" under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). But SCOPS' decision states: 'The 
petitioner claims the article ... is scholarly, however, the evidence provided does not establish this 
claim." 
This decision's de novo appellate review of both the evidentiary criteria and the final merits 
determination eliminates such inconsistent findings and other errors in SCOPS' decision. 
The Petitioner argues that evidence of his membership in associations, high salary, authorship of the 
scholarly article, and original contributions demonstrate sustained acclaim and recognition in his field. 
He states that the associations to which he belongs "impose rigorous gatekeeping standards requiring 
demonstrated achievements of distinction vetted by recognized experts." As previously discussed, 
however, the Petitioner's evidence does not identify the purported experts or establish their recognition 
in their fields. Thus, evidence of his membership in associations in the field reflect only a limited 
amount of acclaim and recognition of him. 
The Petitioner contends that his "earnings substantially exceed the national maxima and are 
commensurate with top executive benchmarks. This is not anecdotal evidence - it is hard, quantifiable 
proof that the market recognizes his expertise as indispensable." As previously indicated, however, 
the Petitioner has not reliably demonstrated his compensation amount. He claimed to receive all his 
company's revenues. But evidence of the business's payments to him and his estimated monthly 
salary indicate far lesser compensation. Thus, the record does not support his contention. 
The Petitioner claims that the scholarly article he authored about integrating AI and tourism advances 
academic knowledge in his field. He states: "His work did not merely report on practice; it translated 
real-world innovations into a theoretical framework, thereby enriching academic discourse and 
influencing the next generation ofresearch and practice." But, as previously discussed, the Petitioner's 
evidence did not demonstrate that any others in the field have cited his article. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d 
9 
at 1121 ("[O]ther authors' citations (or a lack thereof) might be relevant to the final merits 
determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or her field of endeavor."); see also 
Donskoy v. USCIS, No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *7 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 11, 2021) 
(affirming a finding that a petitioner's scholarly articles did not demonstrate extraordinary ability 
where "USCIS had no evidence before it that the submitted publications garnered [him] any acclaim 
or recognition, or were even cited by other scholars"). The record therefore does not explain how the 
article purportedly enriched academic discourse and influenced future researchers and practitioners. 
The Petitioner also argues that his integration ofAI into his business demonstrates his influence within 
the tourism industry. "This is not incremental improvement," he states, "it is systematic 
transformation, producing measurable, large-scale results and setting a new benchmark for global best 
practice." The record, however, does not detail the Petitioner's AI system and lacks independent 
evidence that others in the field recognize or have adopted his claimed AT advancements. See Hamal 
v. US. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 
2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 
1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (upholding our decision that a petitioner did not establish that his 
work at a theater and cultural organization "has been recognized by the field as being significantly 
important or viewed as unusually influential.") Thus, the Petitioner's AI evidence does not establish 
his purported extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner contends that the letters on his behalf: 
are unrebutted and highly probative, confirming [his] field-defining impact in business 
and tourism management. These letters come from recognized leaders in the industry 
and consistently attest to his innovative contributions, his role in shaping international 
tourism practices, and his influence on the direction of the field. 
But the letters indicate that all their authors personally know the Petitioner and have collaborated with 
him and his tourism company. The letters are not independent and objective enough to place him 
among the small percentage at his field's very top. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("It is 
generally expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international 
acclaim would have received recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the circle of their 
personal and professional acquaintances.") 
The Petitioner also argues that other evidence demonstrates his receipt of acclaim and recognition in 
his field. For example, he states that the published materials about him reflect "extensive coverage in 
leading mainstream and professional outlets across multiple jurisdictions." As previously indicated, 
he documented published materials in professional publications about himself and his work in his 
field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). But these articles are insufficient to demonstrate his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. Moreover, the other online mainstream media articles about the 
Petitioner and his work are questionable and do not reflect a high level of acclaim or recognition. 
These writings include two articles published verbatim in multiple publications. The articles' 
replication, their lack of stated authorship, and their primary inclusion of summaries and quotations of 
the Petitioner's statements suggest that he or his company wrote them and distributed them to 
publications for promotional purposes. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 3 
("Marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the person's products or promoting the 
10 
person's services are not generally considered to be published material about the person (this includes 
seemingly objective content about the person in major print publications that the person or the person's 
employer paid for)." Counsel stated that the Petitioner neither wrote nor sponsored the two articles. 
But counsel's unsubstantiated assertions are not evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 T&N Dec. 49, 
51 (BIA 1998) ("[S]tatements in a brief: motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are 
not entitled to any evidentiary weight"). 
In sum, the Petitioner has demonstrated his receipt of some acclaim and recognition in the travel and 
tourism field. But a totality of the evidence would not establish that he has sustained national or 
international acclaim or his claimed place among the small percentage at his field's very top. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). Congress indicated that 
this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very 
top of their field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner has demonstrated his receipt of some acclaim and 
recognition in his field. But, considering the evidence as a whole, he would not have established his 
claimed place among the small percentage of his field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 
383, 386 (5th Cir. 2022) ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary ability classifications, we cannot say 
that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but 
merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even 
athletes performing in their sport's premier league do not automatically meet the extraordinary ability 
standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not satisfy the requisite number of initial evidentiary criteria. Even if he had, he
