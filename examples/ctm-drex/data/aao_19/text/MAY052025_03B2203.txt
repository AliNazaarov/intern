Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics through sustained national or international 
acclaim, and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. Section 203(b )( I)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner is the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, nor did he meet at least 
three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We 
review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics," which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Through their work, substantially benefit the country prospectively. 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise 
commensurate with individuals in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction ofat least three often lesser evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 Then, if a petitioner meets the initial evidence requirements, USCIS must 
conduct a final merits determination to assess whether the record-as a whole-shows a petitioner's 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th 
Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, iffulfilling 
the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner did not indicate or establish that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed to meet four of the ten evidentiary criteria: 
• (i) receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field; 
• (v) original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major 
significance to the field; 
• (vii) display of work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases; and 
• (viii) leading or critical role for organizations that have a distinguished reputation. 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not satisfy any of the claimed criteria. Moreover, it 
determined that the Petitioner did not establish he was coming to the United States to continue working 
in his field or that his entry would substantially benefit the United States. On appeal, the Petitioner 
continues to assert satisfaction of the initial four criteria 2, and claims SCOPS erred in evaluating the 
evidence, imposing novel substantive and evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth in the 
regulations. 
Upon de novo review, we agree that the Petitioner has not established eligibility under any of the 
criteria claimed.

A petitioner must support assertions with relevant, probative, and credible evidence. See Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. The Petitioner cannot rely on conclusory statements to satisfy their 
burden. See e.g., 1756, Inc. v. US. Atty Gen., 745 F. Supp. 9, 15 (D.D.C. 1990) (holding that an 
agency need not credit conclusory assertions in immigration benefits adjudications). Here, without

Evidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
To satisfy this criterion, a petitioner must first establish that the displayed material constitutes their 
work product, and that the work was displayed at an artistic exhibition or showcase. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8). The modifier of "artistic" in the regulatory language means 
that to satisfy this criterion, a petitioner must establish that the exhibitions or showcases which 
displayed his work were artistic in nature. Id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he satisfies this criterion based on the evidence of Instagram 
postings and articles showing his company's products and work. Asserting that his work "has been 
made available to a broad audience through these channels" and that "[ s ]ocial media platforms are 
widely recognized as contemporary showcases that allow artists and professionals to reach large 
audiences," he contends that his social media posts display his work "in a manner consistent with an 
exhibition." We disagree. 
First, the Petitioner has not shown how photographs and promotional materials of his product posted 
and shared on his company's social media accounts constitute a display of his work, rather than self-­
published, promotional material. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1122 (finding that self­
publishing a textbook, lecturing at a community college, and presenting at conferences were not 
displays at artistic exhibitions or showcases consistent with the relevant regulatory language). 
Moreover, accepting the Petitioner's interpretation that any display ofhis work that is made available 
to the public would satisfy this criterion would render the requirement that the display occur at an 
"artistic exhibition or showcase" meaningless. While artists may use social media forums like 
4 
Instagram to promote their art, the Petitioner has not sufficiently shown that this constitutes an artistic 
showcase as contemplated by the regulation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B). 
Similarly, while we acknowledge the submission of articles discussing the use of his company's 
product, and additional screenshots of videos depicting individuals using his product, we are not 
persuaded by the Petitioner's contention on appeal that, because "the article in question presented [his] 
work to a professional audience, which aligns with the definition of a 'showcase,"' he has satisfied 
this criterion. The Petitioner has not explained why these articles and postings should be considered 
artistic showcases. As stated, it is the Petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for each criterion. 
Without more, we cannot conclude that the Petitioner satisfies this criterion. 
Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
In order to meet this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that he not 
only played a leading or critical 
role, but also that the organization or establishment for which he played that role is recognized as 
having a distinguished reputation. Organizations or establishments that enjoy a distinguished 
reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, B.l (citing to the definition of distinguished, Merriam-Webster, 
https ://www.merriamwebster.com/ dictionary /distinguished). 
The record contains various pieces of evidence establishing the Petitioner's professional background 
and the operations of his company, including his resume, various business registration documents, as 
well as social media postings discussing his company's product. Upon review, SCOPS concluded that 
the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the record did not contain evidence establishing 
that his company held a distinguished reputation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner disagrees with SCOPS' conclusion and asserts that it did not properly review 
the evidence, which he claims shows "he has been the recipient of nationally and internationally 
recognized prizes and awards for excellence in the field of her [sic] endeavor." First, regarding the 
Petitioner's claim that he satisfies this criterion based on receipt ofawards, we have already considered 
and dismissed those assertions under the first criterion. Moreover, the Petitioner has not addressed 
SCOPS' conclusion that the evidence does not establish that his company has garnered a distinguished 
reputation. 
Nonetheless, upon de novo review, we agree with SCOPS that the record does not establish the 
distinguished reputation of the Petitioner's company. The business records and sale documents in the 
record establish the company is operational, but do not indicate the company has a distinguished 
reputation. And, while we acknowledge that the record contains evidence corroborating some 
individual interest in the Petitioner's product, and its bridal makeup services, he has not explained how 
this evidence sufficiently demonstrates that the company is marked by eminence, distinction, or 
excellence. 
Accordingly, we cannot conclude that the Petitioner meets this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement of a 
"major, international recognized award," or evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). As a result, we do not need to provide the type of final merits determination 
discussed in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record 
in the aggregate and conclude that it does not demonstrate that the Petitioner has established the 
acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
oftheir respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing toward that goal. See Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. at 954 ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the 
statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability designation 
is "extremely restrictive by design,"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very 
small percentage of prospective immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), 
No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 
(upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian 
theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals 
fail to win this designation")). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is otherwise consistent with a "career of acclaimed 
work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also 
section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that they are one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1 )(A) and 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
