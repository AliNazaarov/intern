Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an alien ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition) concluding the record 
did not establish that the Beneficiary had received a major, internationally recognized award, nor did 
the Petitioner demonstrate that it demonstrated eligibility in a final merits determination. The matter 
is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to 
demonstrate eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of 
the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We 
review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 20 I 0) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Beneficiary cofounded and serves as the chief technical officer of a company that uses 
autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) agents to detect real-time buyer intent signals online. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed it met eight of the regulatory 
criteria and SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied six of those eight. On appeal, the Petitioner 
continues to maintain that it meets the evidentiary criteria relating to performing in a leading or critical 
role, and we agree that he has satisfied that additional criterion. But as it had already met a least three 
criteria, satisfying an additional criterion in step one of this adjudication does not effectively impact 
the case; we still move on to whether the Petitioner will prevail in a final merits determination. We 
also note the Petitioner claimed eligibility under comparable evidence under the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)( 4) before SCOPS but it does not advance that same claim on appeal. It has therefore waived 
it. Matter ofA-H-D-, 29 I&N Dec. 642, 643 n. l (BIA 2026) ( citing Matter ofP-B-B-, 28 T&N Dec. 
43, 44 n. l (BIA 2020)). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether it has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, the Beneficiary's sustained national or 
international acclaim and that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, 
and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a 
final merits determination, we analyze the Beneficiary's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine ifhis successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability 
in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20); also see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (stating that in a final merits determination USCIS officers 
should then evaluate the evidence together when considering the petition in its entirety to determine if 
the petitioner has established by a preponderance of the evidence the required high level of expertise

of the immigrant classification). In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not documented 
the Beneficiary's eligibility. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 810 (E.D. Pa. 2025); 
Agrawalv. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. 
v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. 
Bankers Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of 
the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 
2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
SCOPS discussed each topical area relating to the criteria and a common thread running through their 
analysis was the Petitioner's failure to demonstrate the Beneficiary's sustained national or international 
acclaim as nearly all his achievements were from 2024 and 2025. But the Petitioner only mentions the 
concept of sustained acclaim in passing in the appeal brief and it does not explain how SCOPS made an 
incorrect determination on that issue. It merely states: "USCIS correctly observes that this technology 
startup's funding was obtained in 2024-2025, an uncontested fact that the Denial wrongfully raises to 
allege the beneficiary does not have 'sustained national or international acclaim."' 
That shortcoming waives the sustained acclaim issue on appeal. Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 693,693 
n.l (BIA 2023) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 I&N Dec. 657, 658 n.2 (BIA 2012) and finding issues that 
are not meaningfully challenged in the appeal are waived); Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 
(BIA 2021) (finding when a filing party mentions an issue without developing an argument, the issue is 
deemed waived). An issue within a brief that provides conclusory assertions but lacks adequate factual 
details or legal arguments is deemed forfeited. Olea-Serefina v. Garland, 34 F.4th 856, 867 (9th Cir. 
2022) (citing John-Charles v. California, 646 F.3d 1243, 1247 n.4 (9th Cir. 2011)). 
And the Petitioner cannot revive it by filing a motion on this decision as a filing party cannot cure their 
failure to preserve an argument in a previous stage of the same proceedings through a post-adjudicative 
motion. Philipp v. Stiftung Preussischer Kulturbesitz, 77 F.4th 707, 709-10 (D.C. Cir. 2023); United 
States v. Campbell, 26 F.4th 860, 875-76 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 143 S. Ct. 95,214 L. Ed. 2d 19 (2022) 
( finding that unless it would result in a miscarriage ofjustice, in most cases an issue abandoned on appeal 
should still be dismissed in any subsequent motion without addressing the merits). 
Although that shortcoming is fatal to this analysis, we summarize the remaining factors. A review of the 
record reveals that the Beneficiary has documented venture funding ofapproximately $1.3 million for his 
startup, participation inl lmemberships such as Forbes Councils, coverage in several media 
and trade outlets, instances of peer-review and judging activity, authorship of scholarly and professional 
3 
publications, a leading role as an executive in the startup, and a substantial equity stake in that company. 
This material shows recognition that is largely confined to a single venture-backed enterprise and related 
startup ecosystems and the totality of the evidence does not establish that the Beneficiary has achieved 
the level of sustained national or international acclaim contemplated by the statute and regulations, nor 
does it illustrate he has risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor. 
The awards and memberships presented were tied primarily to startup funding and accelerator 
participation, the media references are limited in number and scope, and the judging and publication 
history, while indicative ofprofessional competence, do not demonstrate that his influence or reputation 
extends broadly across the field beyond this one company. Accordingly, the record, as a whole, falls 
short of showing that the Beneficiary is among the small percentage at the top of his field as required for 
classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
And finally, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS summarily disregarded the Beneficiary's 0-1 
nonimmigrant visa approval. We disagree as they devoted a paragraph to the topic and informed the 
Petitioner that the agency is not legally bound to approve an EB-1 immigrant petition based on the 
Beneficiary's prior 0-1 status, as each classification has distinct statutory requirements and is adjudicated 
independently. 
An approved 0-1 petition is relevant to but not determinative of the outcome of an extraordinary ability 
immigrant petition. Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. 
Tex. June 17, 2025) (citing Cuckicv. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. 
Mar. 21, 2023) and Lapteva v. USCIS, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *l, *6 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 
2025). Also see, e.g., Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US v. US 
Dept. ofJustice, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22 (D.D.C. 1999); Fedin Brothers Co. Ltd. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103 
(E.D.N.Y. 1989) (generally finding that many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior 
nonimmigrant petitions). 
In line with USCIS policy, because the Beneficiary has previously held 0-1 status we elect to explain 
why he is not qualified for this immigrant classification that has different eligibility requirements. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(3). First, the records ofproceeding for the approved 
0-1 petitions are not before us and we cannot compare the records to determine whether the approved 
petitions involved different facts or were approved in error. The regulation repeatedly reflects that an 
eligibility determination on a benefit request will be based on information contained in the record of 
proceeding. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(10), (11), (14), (16)(i)-(ii). Further, each case must be decided on 
its own facts with regard to the sufficiency of the evidence presented. See, e.g., Matter ofH-C-R-C-, 
28 T&N Dec. 809,812 (BIA 2024). 
Under USCIS policy, the Beneficiary's receipt of 0-1 nonimmigrant classification is relevant and can 
be considered an indicator that he is eligible for EB-1 classification, but it is not determinative. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(3). However, as discussed above and in SCOPS' 
denial decision, the evidentiary shortcomings in the record do not support a grant of this petition. It 
is also unclear how the approval of a "roughly analogous" but much less stringent nonimmigrant visa 
should lead to the conclusion that the lesser approval should significantly impact this much more 
austere immigrant visa. As such, we have considered the Beneficiary's grant of 0-1 nonimmigrant 
4 
visa classification, but the evidence remains insufficient to meet his burden to establish his eligibility 
for EB-1 classification. 
In summary, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top oftheir respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCIS has long 
held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the 
"extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954-55 (Assoc. Comm'r 
1994). Considering the full measure ofthe Petitioner's ability and achievements, the level ofhis national 
or international acclaim and the extent to which his achievements have been recognized in the field are 
not indicative of a record of sustained acclaim. Also, he has not submitted extensive documentation 
exhibiting he has attained a level of expertise placing him among that small percentage that has risen to 
the very top ofthe field of endeavor. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a 
petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not 
met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
