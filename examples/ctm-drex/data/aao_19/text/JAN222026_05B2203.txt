Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an executive and entrepreneur in the field of digital marketing and computer vision, 
seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant 
visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements for this classification through evidence of 
either a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or by meeting at least three 
of the ten evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)( 1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

6. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration in Relation to Others in the 
Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner meets this criterion, but after review we will withdraw that conclusion. 
This criterion allows petitioners to establish either that they have a "high salary" or "significantly high 
remuneration" in comparison to others in their field. As evidence of his compensation, the Petitioner 
submitted his IRS Form W-2s for the years 2022, 2023, and 2024, reflecting wages of approximately 
$284,500, $151,300, and $128,800, respectively. Submitted pages from the website 
www.payscale.com indicate that the top 10% of CEOs inl lFlorida earned an annual salary of 
approximately $333,000. But such compensation surveys should be geographically-appropriate in 
order to provide an accurate comparison with those in the Petitioner's field who are similarly situated, 
and the Petitioner lives inl lNew York. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
Further, even if we were to accept this figure as an accurate basis for comparison, it would not show 
that the Petitioner's salary can be considered high relative to others in his field, particularly in 2023 
and 2024 when his salary fell below the median salary shown. 
As for total remuneration, the Petitioner referenced the same source in stating that when considering 
"bonuses, profit-sharing, and equity" in addition to salary, the top 10% of CEOs earn $410,000 
annually. When describing his equity compensation, the Petitioner based his figure for valuation of

B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F.Supp.3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, 
evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we 
decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
C. 0-1 Classification 
The record reflects that the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification reserved for nonimmigrants 
of extraordinary ability. However, the prior approval does not preclude USCIS from denying an 
immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a different standard - statute, regulations, and 
case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. 
June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 
21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). 
Many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., 
Sunl[ft Int'!, Inc. v. Mayorkas, No. 20-CV-08869-JCS, 2021 WL 3111627, at *11 (N.D. Cal. July 22, 
2021); Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. 
US. Dep 't ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 (D.D.C. 1999), ajf'd sub nom. IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep 't 
ofJust., INS, No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 1999); Fedin Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 
F. Supp. 1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), aff' d, 905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority

over SCOPS, the office adjudicating nonimmigrant visa petitions, is comparable to the relationship

III. CONCLUSION 
While we disagree with parts of SCOPS decision as discussed above, we agree with its ultimate 
conclusion that the Petitioner does not meet at least three of the evidentiary criteria under 
8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3), and therefore does not meet the initial evidence requirements for this 
classification. He is not eligible as an alien of extraordinary ability, and the petition remains denied.
