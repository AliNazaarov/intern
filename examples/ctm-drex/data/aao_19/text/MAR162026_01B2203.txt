Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a lawyer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner: I) did not satisfy at 
least three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria, 2) did not establish that he seeks to enter the 
United States to continue work in his area, and 3) did not demonstrate that his entry would substantially 
benefit prospectively the United States. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not meet any of the four claimed criteria. On appeal, the 
Petitioner challenges SCOPS' unfavorable determination on those same four categories of evidence. 1

at F.2(8)(1).

B. Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes major, 
significant contributions in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner argues that he provided his doctoral dissertation, diploma, and scholarly publications 
and "this evidence demonstrates that the Petitioner developed an original theoretical model analyzing 
legal consciousness in a post-Soviet, transitional legal systej, integrting legal theory, sociology, and 
public governance in a manner not previously addressed in legal scholarship." Although his 
dissertation and authored material may indicate original contributions, the Petitioner did not reference 
any evidence showing they represent majorly significant contributions in the field, as required by this 
regulatory criterion. See Guida, 2021 WL 568850, at *9 (upholding to reasonably ask for 
corroborating evidence); see also Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 
2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019); Soni v. United States, No. CV 11-2431, 2016 WL 4154137, 
at *5 (D.N.J. Aug. 2, 2016) (finding that dearth of supporting evidence is an appropriate reason to 
discount conclusory statements). 
Moreover, the Petitioner did not establish how this material has somehow impacted, influenced, or 
affected the field at a level consistent with major significance. See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134 
("[T]he regulatory requirement that the petitioner demonstrate the 'major significance' ofany original

experts in the field explaining the nature and significance ofthe person's contribution may also provide

when the record includes documentation corroborating the claimed significance. See generally 6

contribution and its significance in the field and should set forth the basis of the writer's knowledge

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the scholarly articles (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi)) 
and roles (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)) criteria, we need not reach these grounds because the Petitioner 
cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). See 
Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 2023), aff'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Trump, No. 23-
2029, 2025 WL 3491324 (1st Cir. Aug. 12, 2025) (declining to consider a criterion when the plaintiff 
cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we decline to reach and hereby reserve these issues. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter, as well as a review ofSCOPS 's unfavorable decision regarding the Petitioner's eligibility under 
section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) and (iii) of the Act. See Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25 (1976). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
