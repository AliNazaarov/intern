Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an artificial intelligence (Al) researcher, CEO, and head, seeks classification as an alien 
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( I )(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not 
establish that he satisfied at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria. We dismissed a subsequent 
appeal. The matter is now before us on motion to reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(l)(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner contests the correctness of our prior decision. In support of the motion, the 
Petitioner asserts our appeal dismissal improperly diminished the significance of the record letters, 
publications, citations, and high salary evidence. The Petitioner also contends the evidence was not 
considered in its totality with the proper standard ofreview. 
On appeal, we concurred with SCOPS that the Petitioner had demonstrated he met two of the criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vi), respectively, relating to participation as a judge of the work of 
others in the field and authorship of scholarly articles in the field. We also concurred with SCOPS 
that the Petitioner had not met the additional two criteria that he claimed to meet at 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(3)(v) and (ix), respectively, relating to original contributions of major significance in the 
field and commanding a high salary. On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner continues to maintain he 
meets the four evidentiary criteria he claimed previously.

In our prior decision, we considered letters of support from individuals in the IT field that "provided 
details about some of the Petitioner's projects and described the problems that the Petitioner's work is 
intended to address." Overall, we determined the letters did not establish the major significance ofthe 
Petitioner's work in the field. Specifically, we described and considered the contents of the letters of 
support but found their authors either did not specifically explain the major significance of the 
Petitioner's work in the field, "provide specific, corroborated explanations of how the Petitioner's 
contributions are of major significance," provide detail, or establish how widely the Petitioner's work 
is used. We also explained why the citation data provided by the Petitioner, while it related to the 
volume of citations and diversity of citing authors, did not sufficiently demonstrate the significance of 
the Petitioner's published work. 
On motion, the Petitioner asserts that his publication record reflects 312 citations at the time of filing 
and submitted statistics placed him in the top percentiles of cited researchers publishing work in the 
field of computer science. The Petitioner also contends that his work has been cited "in several high­
impact journals, demonstrating their academic and industrial significance." The Petitioner has not 
established the publication of articles in highly ranked or popular journals inevitably demonstrates the 
field considers the research and work to be an original contribution of major significance. Rather, a 
publication that bears a high ranking or impact factor reflects the publication's overall citation rate; it 
does not show an author's influence or the impact of their research on the field or that every article 
published in a highly ranked journal automatically indicates a contribution of major significance. See 
Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou, No. 1 :23eV256, 2024 WL 4008312, at *7 (M.D.N.e. Aug. 30, 2024), 
report and recommendation adopted sub nom. Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou Dir. of United States 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. l:23-eV-256, 2024 WL 4267307 (M.D.N.e. Sept. 23, 2024) 
(upholding journal rankings did not show an author's influence or impact).

Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high 
remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
In our prior decision, we concurred with SCOPS that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion. We 
found the Petitioner submitted comparative salaries information for "occupational titles" that did not 
"fully capture[d] the nature of his work with his company, such that the salary survey figures relate to 
individuals who perform similar work to the Petitioner." In addition, we found the comparative 
salaries provided by the Petitioner did not allow for a reliable comparison as they indicated only base 
salaries and bonuses while the majority of the Petitioner's 2023-2024 income was identified as 
overtime, housing allowance, coupon, and child benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner asserts our dismissal decision erroneously "intimated" that this criterion 
requires demonstration of a high salary, as compared to others who command high salaries. Rather, 
in our previous decision, we noted that the USCIS Policy Manual provides that evidence in support of 
this criterion can include comparative geographical and position-appropriate evidence. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1). We further noted that, relevant caselaw suggests that "in 
relation to others in the field" should be interpreted as allowing comparisons to those employed in the 
same occupation at a similar level. The Petitioner contends the evidence establishes the Petitioner's 
salary is high even when compared to other salaries exceeding the average in his field, claiming his 
equals 30 times the highest annual salary when including his benefit, bonuses and profit-sharing. The 
Petitioner states that even in comparing base salaries alone, his income is multiples beyond the highest 
annual salaries provided on motion for CEOs, AI researchers, or data scientists, or even CEO and 
managing directors combined. As such, the Petitioner claims the submitted average annual salaries 
are suitable comparisons for his own work as both CEO and head of the Research and Development 
Department. However, the Petitioner relies upon CEO wages figures that are newly submitted on 
motion. As stated, we will not consider new facts or evidence on a motion to reconsider. Previously, 
the Petitioner submitted information on annual salaries for managing directors, AI developers, and 
data scientists. Since 2012, the Petitioner has held the collective roles of CEO, AI researcher, and 
head ofthe Research and Development Department for~------------~a software 
company inl IThese unique roles occupied by the Petitioner are not reflected in the comparative 
wage data, as submitted prior to the instant motion. Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
error in our prior determination and does not meet this criterion. 
On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not established that our previous decision was based on an 
incorrect application of law or policy at the time we issued our decision. Therefore, the motion will 
be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4).
