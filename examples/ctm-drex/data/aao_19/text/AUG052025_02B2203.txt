Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a computational biology researcher, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish the Petitioner has a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major internationally-recognized award) or meets at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Specifically, while SCOPS correctly found that the 
Petitioner has participated as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization and has authored scholarly articles in the field in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media, it also found that he did not establish he has made original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in his field. As the 
Petitioner did not establish at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria, SCOPS did not conduct a final 
merits determination to determine whether the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen 
to the top of his field of endeavor and has sustained acclaim. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
We adopt and affirm SCOPS' decision. See Matter ofBurbano, 20 l&N Dec. 872, 874 (BIA 1994); 
see also Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (noting that the practice of adopting and 
affirming the decision below "is not only common practice, but universally accepted"). In correctly 
finding that the Petitioner did not establish that he has made original scientific, scholarly, artistic, 
athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in his field of computational biology, 
SCOPS listed the evidence provided by the Petitioner and thoroughly and accurately discussed the 
Petitioner's arguments and evidence. We therefore disagree with the Petitioner's assertion on appeal 
that he provided sufficient evidence of original scientific contributions of major significance in his 
field. The Petitioner provides similar arguments as before and points to areas in SCOPS' decision in 
which he claims it erred. Specifically, he claims that SCOPS was dismissive of his citation count, 
citation statistics from Clarivate Analytics, evidence from OpenAlex, funding by the National 
Institutes of Health (NIH), and letters of support. 1 
First, SCOPS referenced the Petitioner's Google Scholar profile and number of citations at the time 
of filing the petition and at the time he responded to a request for evidence (RFE). SCOPS 
acknowledged the Petitioner's claim that the increase in his total citations during this time period 
demonstrated the significance of his contributions. The Petitioner again refers to this increase in 
citation count on appeal. However, SCOPS correctly found the Petitioner must be eligible at the time 
of filing and therefore the citation increase at the time of the RFE was not probative. Additionally, 
SCOPS pointed out that the Petitioner did not demonstrate how his citation count compared to other 
similar, highly cited articles that his field established as having major significance. The Petitioner 
does not address this issue on appeal. Rather, he asserts that SCOPS imposed a threshold number for 
citations, but a review of SCOPS' decision does not include a citation number requirement. 
Second, SCOPS noted that the Petitioner's field is research driven and by nature research adds to the 
general knowledge in one's field, and publications can demonstrate the originality ofwork. However, 
SCOPS correctly stated that the Petitioner must establish his contributions have had major significance 
in the broader field. SCOPS mentioned that the significance of the Petitioner's research has yet to be 
realized, determined, measured, or established; and he has not established that his work has impacted 
the field at a significant level, such that it has been widely adopted and implemented throughout the 
field, remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or the field has been changed as a result. The 
Petitioner has not provided this type of evidence on appeal or otherwise established that the record 
shows this. 
Third, SCOPS discussed information from Clarivate Analytics regarding articles published from 2013 
to 2023 and their percentile rankings based on number of citations received. SCOPS noted that the 
Petitioner's field is computational biology, but he inappropriately included printouts for the broader 
fields of biology and biochemistry. The Petitioner refers to information from Clarivate Analytics 
showing that computational biology is a subfield of biology and biochemistry. While that may be the 
case, we would still assess the level of significance of his work relative to his particular field of 
computational biology. SCOPS discussed the evidentiary deficiencies of a document from A-S-. 2 The 
document was purported to be an email from a Clarivate Analytics representative, but it did not include 
any identification ofwho it was emailed to, when it was emailed, or that A-S- was a Clarivate Analytics 
representative. It also included a faulty website link in its text. The Petitioner includes evidence on 
appeal that A-S- worked for Clarivate Analytics, but did not otherwise address the issues mentioned 
by SCOPS. 
Fourth, SCOPS reviewed information about OpenAlex, and an OpenAlex profile with the Petitioner's 
citation count, publication count, and percentile rankings for both. SCOPS pointed out that the

Fifth, SCOPS addressed multiple letters of support from professors and found that while they 
expressed admiration for his original research and his influence on a few researchers, they did not 
provide specific detailed information explaining the unusual influence or high impact his work has 
had on the broader field. The Petitioner disagrees and points to a letter in the record mentioning his 
"research is esteemed for its provision of critical insight into molecular level changes in crop plants 
under drought and salinity stresses, and its revelation of associated DNA polymorphism, DNA 
methylation, and transcriptional variations being used for high-yield crops susceptible to said 
environmental conditions." The information from this letter does not detail who the research is 
esteemed by or otherwise establish the unusual influence or high impact his work has had on the 
broader field. The Petitioner also quotes from three other letters which SCOPS addressed in detail. 
Again, these letters express admiration for his original research and his influence on a few researchers, 
but they do not provide sufficient information explaining the unusual influence or high impact his 
work has had on the broader field. 
Finally, SCOPS discussed evidence related to funding from the NIH and how it failed to establish the 
major significance of the Petitioner's work. The Petitioner asserts that the NIH only funds new and 
breakthrough projects with impacts on human health. This general assertion does not address the 
specifics of the Petitioner's work and whether it is of major significance. 
Although the Petitioner has established that he participated as a judge ofthe work ofothers in the same 
or an allied field of specialization and has authored scholarly articles in the field in professional or 
major trade publications or other major media, he has not established that he has made original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in his 
field. Therefore, he is not eligible for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
