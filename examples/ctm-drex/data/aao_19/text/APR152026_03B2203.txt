Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a researcher and lecturer in the field of artificial intelligence (Al), seeks classification 
as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that while the Petitioner met the 
initial evidence requirements for the classification by meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the record did not establish that she had sustained national or international 
acclaim in her field and was one of the small percentage at the very top of her field. The matter is 
now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)( 1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met three of the evidentiary 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to her authorship of scholarly articles, her original 
contributions of major significance, and her participation as a judge of the work of others. On appeal, 
the Petitioner asserts that in addition to meeting the initial evidence requirements for this classification, 
the record establishes that she has sustained national or international acclaim in the field of AI and is 
one of the very small percentage of those at the top of her field. Per the following discussion, we 
disagree with SCOPS' conclusion regarding the significance of the Petitioner's original contributions 
to her field, and thus whether she meets the initial evidence requirements for this classification. 
In order to satisfy the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must establish that not only 
have they made original contributions to their field of expertise, but that those contributions have been 
of major significance in the field. For example, a Petitioner may show that the contributions have 
been widely implemented throughout the field, have remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or 
have otherwise risen to a level ofmajor significance. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134-
35 (D.D.C. 2013).

to support this assertion, including partial copies of papers she co-authored in which these

submitted information from Clarivate Analytics which appeared to show that the number of citations 
to these and three other papers co-authored by her were in the top 10% of papers in the field of physics,

However, after analysis of the evidence presented in support of this assertion, we conclude that these 
figures alone are insufficient evidence of the major significance of her published research in the field 
of AI. We first note that the Clarivate Analytics data indicates that it was compiled at some point in 
2024, whereas the Petitioner filed her petition in April 2025 and responded to SCOPS' NOID in 
October 2025. Thus the data does not reflect citations to published works after 2024, while the 
evidence of the citations to the Petitioner's work shows citations well into 2025. Further, the table 
shows that the number of average citations to papers published in a particular field varies widely 
among different fields. The Petitioner has not shown that the number of citations to papers published 
in the broader field of physics as of2024 is an accurate basis for comparison to the number ofcitations 
to papers published in the more specific areas of particle physics or quantum physics as oflate 2025. 
In addition, the Petitioner asserted in her NOID response that based upon a paper published by experts 
in bibliometrics, papers with citation rates in the top 10% in a field can be considered to be "highly 
cited or excellent," and that this is an indicator that the published work is recognized as significant in 
the field. But the notes explaining the data from Clarivate Analytics state that "citation frequency is 
highly skewed with many infrequently cited papers and relatively few highly cited papers," and defines 
"Highly Cited Papers" as those which are placed in the top 1 % of all papers in a given field by number 
of citations. The highly skewed nature of citations to scientific papers can be seen in the 2024 data 
for papers published in the field of physics in 2019, where papers in the top 50% were cited 8 times, 
the top 10% were cited 36 times, and the top 1 % were cited 133 times. Given the relatively small 
difference in the raw figures between the top 50% papers and the top 10% papers when compared to 
the much larger gap between the top 10% papers and the top 1%, this evidence does not support the 
Petitioner's assertion that the number of citations to her published papers reflects recognition by her 
peers that her original contributions are of major significance in the field.

B. Final Merits Determination 
As stated in the foregoing discussion, the Petitioner has not established that her original contributions 
have been of major significance in her field, and she therefore does not meet at least three of the 
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). While she is not eligible for classification as an alien 
ofextraordinary ability because she has not met the initial evidence requirements for this classification, 
we will nevertheless address her assertions regarding the final merits determination. 
In a final merits determination, we examine and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine 
whether the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is one of the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in 
the field through extensive documentation. Here, the Petitioner has not offered sufficient evidence 
that she meets that standard. 
In her appeal brief: the Petitioner begins by highlighting portions of the USCIS Policy Manual which 
describe types of evidence which may aid in determining a petitioner's eligibility. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(8)(2), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. In particular, the Petitioner stresses that 
publication of articles in particularly high-ranked journals, an overall rate of citation which is high 
relative to others in the field, and being named as a researcher on a competitively-funded U.S. 
government grant shows that she has sustained national or international acclaim and is one ofthe small 
percentage at the top of her field. 
Initially, we note that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence showing that she has been named as 
an investigator or researcher on a grant from the U.S. government or any other funding source. As

The Petitioner also asserts on appeal that SCOPS did not give proper consideration to the evidence of 
her peer review activities, claiming that it treated her peer review as "an insignificant obligation." 
While SCOPS characterized peer review as a "routine activity," it did not suggest that peer review was 
either insignificant or an obligation. In addition, the evidence from Elsevier that the Petitioner relies 
upon to assert that peer review "is a great responsibility that is only given to the most capable, 
experienced, and accomplished scholars" makes no such statement or suggestion. Rather, it verifies 
the importance of peer review in general "to uphold the quality and validity of individual articles and 
the journals that publish them." 
A reference letter from the Editor-in-Chief of Journal ofSubatomic Particles and Cosmology, one of 
the journals for which the Petitioner has reviewed manuscripts, applauds her performance as a 
reviewer, noting that her comments often assist authors in improving their papers. He also states that 
his journal only selects reviewers "with international reputation and academic authority in their 
respective research fields." This and other reference letters in the record confirm that peer reviewers 
must have expertise in the areas of research presented in the manuscripts they review, and that peer 
review itself remains essential to preserve the integrity of scientific journals and research. However, 
neither the Editor-In-Chief nor the others who wrote regarding the Petitioner's peer review activity 
provided details regarding the process of selecting peer reviewers, relying instead upon general 
statements that are not supported with documentary evidence. Reference letters which do not include 
specifics terms but offer general statements about a petitioner's accomplishments or eligibility are not 
generally persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3), www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. 
The record as a whole, including the evidence of the Petitioner's published research, the reaction to 
that research by others in the field, and her participation as a peer review for multiple scientific 
journals, does not establish the Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. Although her AI methods 
have been adopted by others in her field, the record does not show that this has brought her sustained 
acclaim at the national or international level which might reflect her status as one of the few at the top 
of the field of AI. Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. 
Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is 
such an elite level of accomplishment); Onaghise v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 
2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding that extraordinary ability visas are known 
as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are "reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). In 
this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of her work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the 
field. Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
