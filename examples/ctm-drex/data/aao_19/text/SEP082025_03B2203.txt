Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, who describes his profession as a senior distinguished engineer, researcher and 
developer in the field of biomedical textiles and materials, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 
8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidence requirements for this classification by demonstrating 
his receipt of a major, internationally recognized award or by meeting at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability 
if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner may demonstrate 
international recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award). Absent such an achievement, the petitioner must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation demonstrating that they meet at least three ofthe ten criteria listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F .3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner provided evidence to establish his eligibility under four of the ten regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (v), (viii), and (ix). SCOPS concluded he met the criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), (viii), (ix). We will not disturb these findings. On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that 
he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) (ii), and (v). 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Memberships in Associations Requiring Outstanding Achievements 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for 
which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in The I Ias an Associate 
Chartered Member. To support his assertion that this was a qualifying association, the Petitioner 
submitted informational materials including bylaws outlining the requirements for members under his 
category of "associateship." To hold an associateship, members must qualify under one of four 
possible routes: pass the prescribed ATI examinations, hold a degree or other qualification accredited 
for ATI, hold an appropriate degree or equivalent qualification in a relevant discipline, with broad 
general knowledge of textiles in related areas, i.e. upstream and downstream from specialism, 
demonstrate specialist textile knowledge comparable to degree level, with broad general knowledge 
oftextiles in related areas, i.e. upstream and downstream from specialism. Associateship also requires 
a period of professional experience which is also divided into four categories including two, five or 
ten years of relevant experience after graduation. Among other requirements, applicants must also 
complete an application form, pay a fee and provide references to support their application. They may 
also be required to attend an interview. 
SCOPS found insufficient evidence that the association required outstanding achievements or used 
recognized national or international experts to determine which individuals qualified for membership. 
Specifically, SCOPS noted that requirements that included employment or activity in a given field, 
minimum education, experience, or achievement, recommendations by colleagues or current 
members, or payment of dues do not satisfy this criterion since these requirements do not constitute 
outstanding achievements. SCOPS considered the letter from the Senior Membership Coordinator, 
E-T- 1 of The I Iwho attested to additional qualifications of consideration by the 
selection committee. However, E-T-' s assertions were not supported by the evidence in the record, 
and consequently E-T-' s letter was given little weight. Where an opinion is not in accord with other 
information or is in any way questionable, U. S. Citizenship and Immigration Services is not required 
to accept that evidence or may give less weight to it. Matter ofCaron International, 19 I&N Dec. 791 
(Comm. 1988); Matter of Sea, Inc., 19 I&N Dec. 817 (Comm. 1988). For example, although 
E-T- claimed that the association required evidence ofoutstanding and impactful personal contribution 
to the textile industry, this "qualification" was not reflected in the informational materials the 
Petitioner submitted. Under USCIS policy, the level of membership afforded to the person must show 
that in order to obtain that level of membership, recognized national or international experts judged 
the person as having attained outstanding achievements in the field for which classification is sought. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 2. Therefore, SCOPS concluded that the evidence did 
not establish that the Beneficiary was a member of an association in the field for which classification 
is sought, which required outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS abused its discretion by unilaterally implementing novel 
evidentiary requirements and applying a standard not supported by the plain language of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(i)(3)(i). The Petitioner, however, has misstated the section of the regulation upon which they 
propose to seek qualification of the Beneficiary. We note that the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(i)(3)(i) pertains to outstanding professors and researchers, while the Petitioner previously 
indicated the Beneficiary was seeking benefits under Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act which makes 
visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability. The Petitioner otherwise disagrees with 
SCOPS's assessment that the bylaws for the association do not indicate "the exact criteria utilized by 
the selection committee to select a chartered member." They assert that SCOPS placed an 
unreasonable burden on the Petitioner because many membership organizations may choose to keep 
their internal decision-making for different levels of membership private. The Petitioner asserts that 
there are multiple layers of membership, and "[ w ]hile the bylaws describe the general criteria for 
consideration as a higher-level membership as a Chartered Member, this does not mean that it includes 
all internal considerations that are formally applied by the reviewing membership committee." The 
Petitioner points to the letter from E-T- which states that the Chartered Member designation is a 
prestigious professional qualification within the textile industry, the selection committee is comprised 
of highly accomplished and well-established Chartered Members from well-recognized industrial, 
academic and research organizations, Chartered Members represent the top 5-10% of professionals in

outstanding achievements from their members as judged by recognized national or international

2. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
In order to satisfy the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), USCIS determines whether the person 
has made original contributions in the field. 2 USCIS then determines whether the original 
contributions are of major significance to the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 5. Examples ofrelevant evidence include, but are not limited to: published materials about 
the significance of the person's original work; testimonials, letters, and affidavits about the persons 
original work; documentation that the person's original work was cited at a level indicative of major 
significance in the field; and patents or licenses deriving from the person's work or evidence of 
commercial use of the person's work. 3 
To meet this criterion, the Petitioner submitted a variety of evidence, including: a scholarly article, 
letters of recommendation and support; conference materials; a Google Scholar profile; and 
information regarding his patents. SCOPS noted that the patents and the scholarly article established 
how the Beneficiary has been credited with original scientific and scholarly contribution in the field 
of biomedical textiles and materials. But SCOPS observed that a patent was not necessarily evidence 
2 See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. 
3 Id. 
of whether the greater field considered the Beneficiary's contribution to be of major significance. 
Moreover, although the issuance of a patent did verify the originality of a device or process, the 
significance of an invention is not evaluated during the patent application process. SCOPS cautioned 
that the significance ofthe innovation must be determined by USCIS on a case-by-case basis especially 
where the United States Patent and Trademark Office, according to its statistics, has approved over 
three hundred thousand patents per year since 2013. Thus, the mere possession of a patent does not 
speak to the invention's significance in the field; and the publication of scholarly work does not 
automatically denote the significance of the work in the greater field. 
SCOPS acknowledged the reference letters from M-K-, J-L-, R-M-, A-J- and Y-K- the Petitioner 
initially provided in support of the major significance of the Beneficiary's contributions. SCOPS 
noted that most of the references shared professional experience with the Beneficiary, or they were 
employed by the Petitioner, leading SCOPS to "question their ability to remain independent and 
objective." We note that SCOPS may consider the relationship between a beneficiary and a witness 
when evaluating the significance of a witness' statements. Id. SCOPS therefore determined that there 
were credibility concerns due to the shared relationships the Petitioner had with some ofthe references, 
when considering their ability to remain objective and independent about the Beneficiary's work. In 
reviewing the letters, SCOPS determined that the authors opined that the Beneficiary's work was novel 
but that they failed to illustrate how any one ofthe patents or the scholarly article are considered to be 
of major significance in the field of biomedical textiles and materials. Similarly, being credited with 
a new procedure or device, while furthering the field of study, does not automatically mean the 
contribution itself is considered to be of major significance. Moreover, the authors largely discussed 
the Beneficiary's work for the Petitioner and the value ofthat work; but contributions ofvalue to one's 
own employer are not automatically considered to be of major significance in the greater field. 
Turning again to the authors, SCOPS determined that their statements were not corroborated by the 
documentary evidence in the record, because the evidence consisted of various conference materials 
and a copy of the Beneficiary's Google Scholar profile page. Regarding the Google Scholar profile 
page, SCOPS reasoned that it remained unclear how these citations were considered to be markers of 
major significance because the record failed to support how the citation counts associated with the 
Beneficiary's work were considered to be "significant attention or commercialization." As for the 
conference participation, SCOPS determined that such opportunities were pursued by most individuals 
across all professional fields and the Beneficiary's participation did little to establish how any one of 
his contributions were considered to be of major significance. Thus, the record failed to corroborate 
the claims of the initial reference letter authors regarding the significance of the Beneficiary's 
contributions. 
After SCOPS issued a request for evidence, the Petitioner submitted additional reference letters from 
J-J- and D-B-. SCOPS acknowledged that both authors suggested that the Beneficiary's contributions 
addressed challenges in the field and lead to various biomedical device improvements (i.e.l II l but the authors failed to sufficiently illustrate how any one of the Beneficiary's 
contributions were considered to be of major significance in the field. Evidence that the person 
developed a patented technology that has attracted significant attention or commercialization may 
establish the significance of the person's original contribution to the field. Id. However, neither J-J­
nor D-B- offered probative details supporting how any one of the patented products had "attracted 
significant attention or commercialization." Similarly, with regards to scholarly articles, neither 
author offered probative details supporting how the Beneficiary's article enjoyed a high citation rate 
relative to others nor how the article was surrounded by "widespread commentary on its importance." 
Published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others working 
in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' work in that field, may 
be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of endeavor. Id.

On appeal, the Petitioner submits documents that they previously submitted including evidence of the 
patents filed with the United States Patent and Trade Office. In addition, for the first time on appeal, 
the Petitioner submits webpage printouts dated July 2025 from Justia showing the inventions, patents, 
and patent applications listed under the Beneficiary's name. We will not consider this evidence for 
the first time on appeal because, the Petitioner was put on notice and given a reasonable opportunity

On appeal, the Petitioner argues that the Beneficiary meets this criterion because of his patents, his 
"role as a presenter and lecturer at top international conferences in the field," evidence of his strong 
patent citation index and the "highly detailed and credible letters from five (5) prominent independent 
experts in the field of endeavor." The Petitioner asserts that SCOPS overlooked the significant and 
widespread impact of the Beneficiary's original scientific and scholarly contributions to the treatment 
of cardiovascular disease - the leading cause of death in the U.S. and globally." They aver that the 
Beneficiary is responsible for developing novel biomedical textile technologies used in commercially 
available artificial heart valves, such as the ~------------' And they assert that the 
Beneficiary's patented designs have been of major significance to the medical device industry and 
have revolutionized the treatment of heart valve repair and replacement through the implementation 
of biomedical textiles and materials. Upon de novo review, we will dismiss this appeal. 
We acknowledge the patents that are credited to the Beneficiary. However, although evidence that an 
individual's work has been patented may potentially demonstrate the work's originality, it will not 
necessarily establish that the work is of major significance in the field, without more. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), supra. In relevant part, evidence that may establish major significance to 
the field may include, for example, evidence that the individual developed a patented technology that 
has attracted significant attention or commercialization. See id. We note that because of his 
employment, the Beneficiary's inventions belong to the Petitioner, and the record does not establish 
that any other individual or entity has licensed the Beneficiary's inventions from the Petitioner. The 
right to use these patents by others could indicate the extent to which the patents may have had 
significance in the field of life sciences. However, the Beneficiary's ownership of the patent has 
prevented non-patent holders, licensees, clients, and customers from utilizing the invention. 
Therefore, without such evidence, the record does not establish how the Beneficiary's patents are 
evidence that his original contributions have attracted significant attention or commercialization that 
demonstrates major significance in the field of life sciences, as contemplated by 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of an individual's 
contribution may provide context for evaluating whether an original contribution is of major 
significance to the field, particularly when the record includes documentation corroborating the 
claimed significance. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). Regarding the reference 
letters, we determine that although they praise the Beneficiary's work, they are general in nature and 
lack supporting evidence that may corroborate the author's observations and opinions. For example, 
J-L- wrote, "[The Beneficiary's] revolutionary designs have been incorporated into [the Petitioner's] 
transcatheter aortic valve replacement product pipeline and have resulted in the publication of several 
US and international patents." And according to A-J-, "[The Beneficiary] developed a radially 
collapsible and stretchable valve outer coverinf for thel I. . . Overall, his work on 
this life-saving technology has made 1 a much more viable option for patients even in the 
presence of complications." However, while the Beneficiary has clearly made contributions to his 
employer, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that these contributions have been adopted or 
recognized broadly as advancing the field, though they remain a "viable option." Thus, we determined 
that the letters of recommendation and support do not support this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that the Beneficiary's 120 "forward patent citations" shows that he 
has made significant original contributions to the field of endeavor. The Petitioner asserts that a 
forward patent citation is a "strong indicator of patent value and the influence and relevance of the 
original patent on subsequent innovations within the same field." However, a forward patent citation 
is not conclusive evidence of technological influence or commercial relevance. For example, a 
forward patent citation may result from acknowledging a prior patent, thereby avoiding infringement, 
rather than a true manifestation of innovation. Here, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the 
Beneficiary's forward patent citations were only an indication of significant original contributions. 
The Petitioner points to the Beneficiary's participation as a lecturer and presenter at major conferences 
and events. They assert that the Beneficiary was invited to present his expert insights and original

B. Final Merits Decision 
The Petitioner has shown that the Beneficiary met at least two initial evidentiary criteria, falling short 
of the three initial evidentiary criteria required. Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of 
extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to show his or her achievements have been 
recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not 
satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude 
that the Beneficiary is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits under 
the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 
1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
