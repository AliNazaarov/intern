Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a designer of wedding dresses, seeks classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). This EB-1 classification 
makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their 
field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

3. Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). A leading role should be apparent by its position in the overall 
organizational hierarchy and through the role's matching duties. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 8. A critical role means an alien contributed in a significantly important 
way to the activities of the organization, establishment, division, or department. Id. Additionally, the 
Petitioner must establish that the organization, establishment, division, or department has a distinguished 
reputation.

4. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) requires evidence of the Petitioner's commercial success, 
"as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk or video sales." This criterion 
focuses on volume of sales and box office receipts as a measure of the person's commercial success 
in the performing arts. The evidence must show that the volume of sales and box office receipts reflect 
the person's commercial success relative to others involved in similar pursuits in the performing arts. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 10. 
USCIS found that the Petitioner did not submit evidence to meet the plain language of this criterion, 
pointing out that the submitted evidence did not show "commercial successes in the performing arts, 
as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." Although USCIS 
did not provide analysis ofthe submitted evidence, we agree that the record is not sufficient to establish 
this criterion. In support of this criterion, the Petitioner submitted several client contracts, a portfolio 
of couture designs, and client reviews and testimonials. While the record includes evidence that he 
has some commercial success by being a lead designer at his own bridal couture shop, these contracts 
and testimonials do not demonstrate the Petitioner's commercial success in the performing arts, as the 
plain language of the regulation requires. See Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. Ctr., United States 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 4:22-CV-00973-SDJ-AGD, 2024 WL 1299065, at *5 (E.D. Tex. 
Mar. 12, 2024), report and recommendation adopted, No. 4:22-CV-00973-SDJ-AGD, 2024 WL 
1289452 (E.D. Tex. Mar. 26, 2024), ajf'd, No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 
2025) (upholding that the plaintiff who works in the field of 3D Printed construction did not work in 
the field of performing arts). 
Also, the plain language of the regulation does not allow for submission of evidence other than box 
office receipts, video sales, or sales of other recorded media. See Sarin v. United States, No. 2:22-
CV-07498-SVW-JS, 2023 WL 5667531, at *11 (C.D. Cal. July 27, 2023) (holding "as shown by" in 
the regulatory language indicates that the subsequent list is not a list of examples but is rather an 
exclusive list). Here, the Petitioner has not submitted such receipts and sales attributable to work in 
the performing arts. 
We note that the Petitioner misidentified this criterion as criterion "ix" in the initial filing and may 
have confused this criterion with another criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) ( commanding a high 
salary in the field). It is unclear whether he intended to raise claims on both criteria (ix) and (x). 
However, in his response to the RFE, the Petitioner did not attempt to clarify his intention or submit 
6 
additional evidence to support that he earned a high salary or renumeration in the field; therefore, we 
deem that the Petitioner waives his claim under the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). Under 
8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l3)(i), failure to respond to specific RFE elements constitutes abandonment or 
waiver of those claims. See also Matter ofR-S-H-, 23 I&N Dec. 629, 638 (BIA 2003) ( citing Matter 
o_fGarcia-Reyes, 19 I&N Dec. 830, 832 (BIA 1988) in holding that when a filing party fails to respond 
to a specific eligibility requirement in the record, they abandoned or waived that issue on appeal); 
Indrawati v. US. Atty Gen., 779 F.3d 1284, 1297 (11th Cir. 2015) (issues not properly developed in 
the record are not preserved for appeal). 
Accordingly, we affirm USCIS' conclusion that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As discussed above, the Petitioner has not met four out of the six criteria that he initially claimed. 
Therefore, we need not discuss the remaining two criteria or conduct a final merits determination under 
Kazarian as it would not change the outcome of the decision. 4 See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 
25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on 
issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The evidence shows that the Petitioner is an experienced tailor and designer of wedding dresses and 
evening gowns who operates both his own wedding dress boutique and a sewing school for students. 
The Petitioner has successfully rebuilt his wedding bridal shop in the United States, and the community 
members praise his business and skills as a tailor and designer. But the Petitioner has not provided 
sufficient evidence that he has earned sustained national or international acclaim as a fashion designer 
or reached a small percentage at the very top of the field, as the Petitioner contends on appeal. 
The record as a whole does not establish the Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought.5 The 
Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 6 Here, the 
Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
