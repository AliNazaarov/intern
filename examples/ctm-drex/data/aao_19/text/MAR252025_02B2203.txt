Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a marketing and communications specialist, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "exceptional 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(I )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition. The Director concluded that the 
Petitioner did not meet any of the requested category's ten evidentiary requirements - three less than 
need for a final merits determination. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Director 
mischaracterized her specific field and misapplied law and policy. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that, although the Director mischaracterized the Petitioner's specific field, she has 
not met the minimum amount of evidentiary criteria. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country. 
Section 203(b )(I )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(I)( A )(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, USCIS must make a final merits determination. To merit approval, the record- as a whole 
- must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized 
achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
The record shows that the Petitioner, a Venezuelan native and citizen, earned a bachelor's degree in 
social communication at a university in her home country. She worked in a variety of jobs in 
Venezuela, including as a media and communications manager at a business union and as a media 
director at a communications company. 
In the United States, the Petitioner served as a producer and public relations (PR) professional at a 
U.S. radio station. Since 2018, she has worked for a U.S. entertainment company as "Producer, 
Advertising, PR & Artist Relations," managing world tours, producing shows, and overseeing content 
creation.

production, advertising, and public relations."

2. Membership in Associations 
To meet this criterion, a petitioner must submit "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted documentation of her memberships in two Venezuelan journalist 
associations. The Director found that journalism "is not business as [she] claims on the petition." The 
Petitioner argues that her memberships in the journalist associations relate to her work in the 
entertainment industry because both activities involve "public relations and media management." 
Contrary to the Director's decision, we find that journalism is a business and that the Petitioner's 
memberships in journalist associations sufficiently relate to her public relations and media work in the 
entertainment industry. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that the journalist associations require outstanding 
achievements oftheir members or judging ofmembers by recognized national or international experts. 
A letter from the president of one association states that it accepted the Petitioner as a member in 
recognition of her achievements over more than 30 years in the field. But the letter does not describe 
her achievements as outstanding or specify that membership in the association requires outstanding 
achievements. See, e.g., Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06-5105, 2007 WL 9229758, *5 (C.D. Cal. July 6, 
2007), ajf'd, 317 Fed. Appx. 680 (9th Cir. 2009) (holding that a petitioner's membership in multiple 
Jiu-Jitsu organizations did not satisfy this criterion because he provided no evidence that any of the 
organizations required outstanding achievements of their members). The letter also does not indicate 
that national or international experts judge the association's members. Counsel asserts that "these 
associations require outstanding achievements of their members and membership is judged by 
recognized experts in the field." But counsel's assertions do not constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter 
ofS-M-, 22 T&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("[S]tatements in a brief, motion, or Notice of Appeal are 
not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight.") 
The Petitioner has membership in associations in her field. But she has not demonstrated that the 
associations require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or 
international experts in their fields. We will therefore affirm the Director's adverse finding regarding 
this evidentiary requirement. 
3. Published Material About the Petitioner 
This criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought." 
5 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Evidence must include "the title, date, and author of the material, and any 
necessary translation." Id. 
When adjudicating this requirement, users first determines whether the published material relates to 
a petitioner and their specific work in their field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Criterion 3. The Agency will accept published material covering a broader topic if the material 
includes "a substantial discussion of the person's work in the field and mentions the person in 
connection to the work." Id. 
users next determines whether a publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 
3. Relevant factors include, for professional and major trade publications, the intended audience and, 
for major trade publications and other major media, a publication's relative circulation, readership, or 
viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of several published articles. The Director found that the articles did 
not discuss the Petitioner's work, "rather just mentioned [her]." The Director also found that the 
articles identified her work as journalism, "which is not in the field ofendeavor." The Director farther 
found that some articles lack their authors' names. The Petitioner contends that "multiple articles 
highlight her role as a producer" of successful shows, focusing on her business organizational skills, 
strategic communications, and media management. 
As the Petitioner argues, some of the articles substantially discuss her work in her field, including her 
creation and production of stage shows and COVrD-19 awareness campaigns on television and digital 
platforms. Also, as previously discussed, we find journalism to constitute a business and to closely 
relate to the skills the Petitioner uses in her work. Thus, contrary to the Director's decision, we find 
that she submitted published materials relating to her work in her specific business field. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that any of the publications in which the articles 
appeared constitute professional or major trade publications, or other major media. The record lacks 
sufficient evidence regarding the publications' relative circulations or their intended audiences. 
Several articles in the same digital publication identify it as "the largest digital newscast in America." 
But the record lacks independent evidence supporting the claim. See Mussarova v. Garland, 
562 F.Supp.3d 837, 847 (C.D. Cal. 2022) (finding online articles insufficient to meet this criterion 
where the petitioner omitted evidence that they appeared in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media). 
The Petitioner has not established that the published material about her and her work appeared in 
professional or major trade publications, or other major media. We will therefore affirm the Director's 
adverse finding regarding this evidentiary requirement. 
4. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
6 
USCIS first determines whether a pet1t10ner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is (or 
was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division, or department. Id. In contrast, a critical 
role means that a petitioner "contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of 
the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization 
or establishment." Id. 
USCIS next determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. The word "distinguished" means "marked by eminence, 
distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Merriam-Webster Online Dictionmy, 
www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant factors include not only an organization's relative size and 
longevity but also other information such as the scale ofits customer base or relevant media coverage. 
Id. 
The Petitioner submitted letters from former and current employers and U.S. business sponsors of a 
show she produced. The Director found the letters insufficient to meet the evidentiary criterion. The 
Director concluded that the letters did not establish the Petitioner's performance in leading or critical 
roles. 
We agree that letters from two of the Petitioner's former Venezuelan employers and the U.S. business 
sponsors lack enough information to determine whether her roles in those organizations were leading 
or critical. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8 (requiring supporting letters to "contain 
detailed and probative information that specifically addresses how the person's role for the 
organization, establishment, division, or department was leading or critical"). But the letter from her 
current employer, combined with other evidence ofrecord, demonstrates the Petitioner's critical role 
for the entertainment company. The record shows that she organizes and manages large events, 
secures sponsorships for shows, and negotiates contracts. These duties are critical to the entertainment 
company's profitability and success. We therefore find that the Petitioner has submitted evidence of 
her performance in a critical role. 
As the Director found, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that her current employer ( or her 
former employers and U.S. business sponsors) have distinguished reputations. The employers and 
sponsors describe themselves as leading businesses. But the record lacks sufficient independent 
objective evidence to support their claims. See Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, 
*8 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) ("Successful businesses can be expected to have satisfied customers and 
the positive esteem of peers. But it takes more to have a 'distinguished reputation."') 
The Petitioner's evidence demonstrates her performance in a critical role for her current employer. 
But the documentation does not demonstrate that the employer has a distinguished reputation. We 
will therefore affirm the Director's adverse finding regarding this evidentiary criteria.

5. The Remaining Criteria 
The Petitioner has not met four ofthe six evidentiary criteria she claims to have satisfied. She therefore 
cannot meet the minimum amount of three evidentiary requirements. Thus, we need not reach and 
hereby reserve consideration ofher arguments regarding her claimed participation as a judge ofothers' 
work in her field or her purported commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration 
in relation to others in the field. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 ( 1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate 
decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not met the minimum amount of evidentiary requirements for the requested 
immigrant visa category. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
