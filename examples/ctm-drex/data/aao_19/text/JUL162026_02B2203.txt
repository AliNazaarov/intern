Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a managing director in the financial services risk management field, seeks classification 
as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria, as required, after conducting a final merits determination, he did not show his 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: (i) 
the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry into the United States 
will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied seven of the ten criteria pertaining to receipt of lesser 
recognized awards or prizes, membership, published materials, judging, authorship, leading or critical 
role, and salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(iv), (vi), (viii), and (ix). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) determined that the Petitioner met six ofhis seven claimed criteria 
for receipt of lesser recognized awards or prizes, published materials, judging, authorship, leading or 
critical role, and salary. However, they found that the Petitioner did not meet the criterion of 
membership. Because they found the Petitioner met at least three ofthe ten evidentiary criteria though, 
they conducted a final merits determination. In doing so, SCOPS concluded that the record did not 
demonstrate the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim and that his achievements have 
been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating he is one of that small percentage who has 
risen to the very top of the field. Therefore, they denied the petition. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he satisfies the criterion of membership and that the evidence 
in the aggregate establishes his eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. We need not 
determine whether the Petitioner satisfies this additional evidentiary criterion because the appeal

based on the documentation presented to SCOPS in the context ofthe final merits determination below. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, their sustained national or international acclaim, that they are among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that their achievements have been recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze an 
individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his successes are 
sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS misapplied the standards for making a final merits 
determination, did not apply the correct preponderance ofthe evidence standard, and issued a decision 
that lacked a reasoned explanation for its conclusions. The Petitioner contends, among other things, 
that his lead roles inside institutions "verified as industry leaders" and evidence that his work informed 
national policy and market-level outcomes "squarely supports the finding that he is among a 'small 
percentage at the top' of his field." 
Upon review of the record, however, we agree with SCOPS' conclusion that the Petitioner has not 
established his eligibility. While the evidence may demonstrate the Petitioner's noteworthy 
accomplishments, it does not establish that he is an individual of extraordinary ability who has 
sustained national or international acclaim, and demonstrated that he is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. 
Regarding the Petitioner's service as a judge of the work of others, the Petitioner points to his peer 
review work, including for the Journal of the Association oflnternational Graduate Students (JAIGS) 
and the Risk Theory Society, as well as for the Editorial Board of Risk Manager Magazine. While he 
argues that we should treat such roles as "strong indicators of recognition by peers who are 'national 
or international experts," as noted in SCOPS' decision, participation in the peer review process, even 
when it involves reputable journals or conferences, does not automatically result in a finding that the 
petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim at the very top of their field. Here, the 
Petitioner has not shown the context of his judging activities, for example, how his peer review or 
judging experiences compare to those of others at the very top of his field, nor is there evidence that 
his involvement as a judge resulted in widespread attention from the field. See Hamal v. United States 
Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (finding the 
petitioner did not provide needed context for his judging experience and the response to his work). 
The record also does not show that the journals that invited the Petitioner to serve as a peer reviewer 
reserve those invitations for those who are at the top of the field or otherwise enjoy standing in the 
field consistent with sustained national or international acclaim. This context is relevant, as reviewing 
manuscripts for journals that select peer review based on subject matter expertise would not provide

As it pertains to his written work, the Petitioner states that the record includes his scholarly and 
professional publications, such as his articles published in the International Journal of Research in 
Business and Social Science, as well as his industry related articles published in NICA's Credit 
Manager magazine. He also claims that his "expert contributions" were incorporated into a national 
white paper on financial inclusion. Of note, however, the record reflects that these articles were 
published in 2025. Publication in one year does not indicate sustained acclaim over the course of the 
Petitioner's almost 15-year-long career. Moreover, the Petitioner submitted little evidence that his 
articles have been cited by others in his field or have otherwise impacted his field in a manner 
commensurate with sustained national or international acclaim as a managing director at the very top 
of his field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) 
(upholding the Petitioner did not show the significance of authorships reflecting that he is among the 
small percentage at the very top of the field). 
Likewise, the Petitioner claims that the documents showing his press and profile coverage from outlets 
such as Forbes Switzerland, Guardian Nigeria, The Credit Manager Magazine, Punch Newspaper, CIO 
Views Magazine, and The Nation Newspaper establish his reputation and public recognition within 
the profession. He claims that these documents show his achievements have been widely recognized 
in the field and amplifies his "top-level profile in his field." But again, the record reflects that these 
articles were published in 2020, 2024, and 2025. While these articles may show that the Petitioner 
garnered some limited media attention over those three years, the Petitioner did not establish that such 
a level of fairly minimal reporting is consistent with a level of success among that small percentage 
who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim, or has a career of acclaimed work. See Hamal v. US. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-

eligibility for the benefit sought. The Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements are 
reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress.
