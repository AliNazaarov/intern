Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a professor of applied linguistics who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition). The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The 
Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Section 291 of the Act; Matter oJChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the 
questions in this matter de novo. Matter of Christo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). 
Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner earned various language-related degrees and has served as an assistant professor or a 
professor for approximately two decades. Her field of endeavor or expertise is in applied linguistics. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met six of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied five ofthe criteria relating to: published material,judging, 
original contributions, the authorship of scholarly articles, and performing in a leading or critical role 
but that she had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes or awards. SCOPS also made a 
favorable determination in a final merits determination, but they concluded that she did not adequately 
establish that she sought to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability 
or expertise under section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains her eligibility. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude that she has not 
satisfied at least three of the regulatory criteria meaning she is not eligible for this immigrant 
classification. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner claimed a grant awarded to the rectorate ofthec::]University for a project she oversaw 
when she was thel Iat the university. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did 
not meet the requirements of this criterion. 
This grant was issued in response to a prospective proposal describing an endeavor the university 
intended to undertake and it funded a future project. The grant did not recognize a distinguished past 
achievement. It was awarded based on meeting technical, programmatic, or institutional requirements 
2 
rather than comparative peer evaluation of "excellence" across the entire discipline. Further, 
university program grants are typically limited to institutional endeavors (e.g., campus-specific 
research or departmental initiatives) and they lack the broader national or international reach the 
regulation's plain language demands. In summary, the Petitioner did not receive the grant, it did not 
constitute prizes or awards for excellence, and the record does not include documentary evidence of 
its national or international recognition in the field of endeavor. 
As a result, we agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner did not submit evidence that meets the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. 
2. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, the 
Petitioner must establish the circulation statistics are high relative to other similar forms of media. 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided multiple articles and SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the 
requirements of this criterion without offering any reasoning. We do not agree with that conclusion 
and we will withdraw SCOPS' favorable grant of this criterion. 
First, the published material the Petitioner provided is not about her and related to her work. Instead, 
it is about different conferences, and the content briefly mentioned her. "The published material must 
be specifically about the applicant and focus on [their] personal work in the relevant field, not merely 
relate to the applicant's work, mention [their] name, or include [their] photo." Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, 
No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *4 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025) (citing Gadhave v. 
Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023)); Joseph v. 
USCIS, No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001 *3 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (affirming USCIS' determination 
that articles do not meet this criterion even when they quote and mention a petitioner, but are not 
primarily about them). 
Second, two of the six articles are identical and simply appear in different publications; also termed 
syndication or content syndication. USCIS evaluates media coverage based on editorial independence 
and objective third-party recognition and this type of replication across publications warrants 
diminished evidentiary value. This syndication creates the appearance it was distributed through 
automated networks or paid distribution services rather than independent editorial selection, or that it 
is self-promotional materials, which USCIS policy does not allow. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
3 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (noting that marketing materials created for 
the purpose of selling the person's products or promoting the person's services are not generally 
considered to be published material about the person which includes seemingly objective content about 
the person in major print publications that the person or the person's employer paid for). And finally, 
although the Petitioner asserts that the relevant publications constitute major media, she has not 
submitted evidence substantiating that characterization, especially with respect to the nature of the 
section of the publication in which her materials appear. See Aravamuthan, 2025 WL 1708126, at *4 
(finding a petitioner's submission of promotional material did not meet this criterion). 
As the Petitioner has not met this criterion's plain language requirements, we are withdrawing SCOPS' 
conclusion to the contrary. 
3. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect 
and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or 
to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. Publications 
and presentations are not sufficient under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they were of 
"major significance." Kazarian v. USCIS, 580 F.3d 1030, 1036 (9th Cir. 2009) aff'd in part 596 F.3d 
1115. 
The Petitioner claimed the following qualified her under this criterion: her published research, her 
founding ofan academic writing center at a university in her home country, her founding ofa scholarly 
journal, the development of an online course to help teachers improve how they teach foreign 
languages to young children, and her selection to teach specialized professional development and 
academic training programs for foreign language teachers in her home country. Without providing 
any analysis, SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the requirements of this criterion. While we 
agree the Petitioner has experienced notable achievements, we do not agree with SCOPS' conclusion 
that they are of major significance in the field. 
We reiterate that the Petitioner claims her area of extraordinary ability is applied linguistics. 
Published research. We do not question the originality of her work, leaving only whether it was of 
major significance in the field. On that issue, the Petitioner claimed that her Google Scholar Profile 
shows an extensive citation record, h-index, and consistent scholarly referencing of her work 
worldwide. While the Petitioner provided her Google Scholar profile containing the claimed elements, 
she did not offer evidence in which to compare any ofthese factors. Even so, it is not enough to satisfy 
this criterion's requirements for the Petitioner to compare her entire publication citation totals to a 
4 
group of researchers. Comparing overall citation rates of individuals is more appropriate to the final 
merits determination, where it can serve as a metric of acclaim and renown within the field. 
A more apt metric under this criterion would be comparing citations to one of the Petitioner's 
individual published articles (as opposed to all of her work) with other researchers' single published 
papers in her specialized field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (providing that "published research that has provoked 
widespread commentary on its importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it 
has been highly cited relative to others' work in that field, may be probative of the significance of the 
person's contributions to the field of endeavor"). We note agency policy here does not reference the 
entire body of work of other researchers compared with a petitioner's body of work. Rather, USCIS 
policy focuses on whether a petitioner's individual published article is highly cited relative to other 
researcher's individual work in the field. And her ResearchGate profile, Academia.edu profile, and 
AD Scientific Index data suffers from the same issue as not being a relevant factor under this criterion 
to demonstrate the major significance of any of her individual contributions to the broader field. 
Founding an academic writing center. The Petitioner established a support unit designed to help 
graduate students and faculty members improve the language and clarity of their English academic 
papers. She claims this center has been used as a prototype for similar initiatives at other Turkish 
institutions. For this contribution to meet the requirements, the Petitioner must demonstrate the 
creation and existence of this unit had a significant impact in the broader field. The evidence she 
presented relating to this claim consisted of her appointment as the director of the center, and other 
materials from the center or university itself. But absent from the record is probative evidence that 
demonstrated what affect her efforts here had in the field. As a result, while notable, the creation of 
this center is not an achievement that will meet this criterion's requirements. 
Founding a scholarly journal. Next, she notes that she created a scholarly journal that generally 
publishes on a biannual schedule and is recognized as a Q4-ranked Web of Science journal. The 
Petitioner notes it has produced 15 volumes and dozens of issues over more than a decade. For its 
impact in the field, she states that by "conceiving this journal, building its editorial policies and 
international board, and developing it to the point of Web-of-Science indexing, I effectively added a 
durable, open-access, globally indexed journal to the language-education literature, an infrastructure 
contribution that continues to support the work of many other scholars beyond my own publications." 
This speaks to the originality of founding the journal, but falls short of explaining how a journal that 
is ranked in the bottom 25 percent of its specific subject category based on citation impact in Web of 
Science amounts to a contribution in the field of major significance. 
Online course and teaching specialized programs. The Petitioner's final claims under this criterion 
also suffer a similar deficiency as her above claims, as there is little to no probative evidence that 
illustrates how her efforts have resulted in a contribution of major significance. Regarding the 
development of an online course to help teachers improve how they teach foreign languages to young 
children, the Petitioner indicated that the "web portal hosts the modules and classroom-tested activities 
as open-access resources and in the is explicitly presented in the project's final documentation as a 
model for in-service program developers and policy makers across Europe. In practice, this means 
that the program's digital materials and framework continue to be used and adapted by 
5 
teacher-education programs." However, the Petitioner did not offer evidence that this has had any 
significant impact in the field of applied linguistics. 
And for her selection to teach specialized professional development and academic training programs 
for foreign language teachers in her home country, she stated that she "helped equip emerging 
academics from universities across Turkiye to integrate EU frameworks into their own 
language-teacher education programs, strengthening institutional capacity beyond my home 
university." Although she claims this "specifically supports competitive, peer-reviewed scientific 
training activities, so participation as an instructor reflects recognition of my expertise at the national 
level," she offers no evidence that might demonstrate what impact her efforts effected in the field of 
applied linguistics. 
Because the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that her contributions have had a sufficiently 
appreciable impact in the applied linguistics field, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination 
under this regulatory criterion. 
4. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
qualifying role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization 
or establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. 
For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. In determining whether the organization, 
establishment, department, or division has a distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, 
longevity, scale of its customer base, and relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1 ). Organizations, establishments, divisions, or departments that enjoy a 
distinguished reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Id. The Petitioner 
must submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024).

III. CONCLUSION 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final 
merits determination in this matter. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)). We also reserve the issue of 
whether the Petitioner established she is coming to the United States to continue work in the area of 
expertise under section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(5). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
