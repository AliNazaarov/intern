Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software engineering manager, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability in the sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the record 
establishes that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required, 
the totality ofthe evidence did not show, in a final merits determination, that the Petitioner has received 
sustained national or international acclaim and that he is among the small percentage at the very top 
of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate recognition 
of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If the petitioner does not submit this evidence, then they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). If the evidentiary standards do not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, 
they may submit "comparable evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

(x). The Petitioner initially claimed to have met ten criteria:

Following review of the initial evidence, as well as the Petitioner's response to a request for evidence

who has risen to the very top of the field. 
Because SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner has established three ofthe initial criteria, on appeal we 
will review the totality of the evidence in the context of a final merits determination. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS made multiple errors of law and fact in denying his 
eligibility under all ten of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The Petitioner also 
asserts that SCOPS applied an incorrect "comparative standard [to] 'leading figures in the field,' as 
though it were an independent evidentiary requirement." 
As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, that he is one of that small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(l) (stating 
the definition of sustain is "to support or maintain, especially over a long period of time ... To persist 
in making (an effort) over a long period of time"). In a final merits determination, we analyze an 
individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his successes are 
sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-
20. See also generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2) (instructing that USCIS officers 
should consider the petition in its entirety to determine eligibility according to the standard: sustained 
national or international acclaim and the achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise, 
indicating that the person is one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor). In the totality, we will also consider evidence generally related to the other categories that 
the Petitioner claimed, but that SCOPS did not grant. In this matter, we determine the Petitioner has 
not shown his eligibility.

The Petitioner asserts that, despite accepting four criteria as established, SCOPS then improperly 
imposed a "comparative standard" as an independent evidentiary requirement in the final merits 
determination. For example, the Petitioner objects to SCOPS stating, in its final merits determination, 
that although the evidence shows that the Petitioner has judged the work of others, he did not 
demonstrate how his judging experience "compares to others in the field, or how the quality or quantity 
of the reviews conducted stands out from his peers." The Petitioner asserts that this is in error because 
the regulatory criteria does not include this additional requirement of "proof that the petitioner's 
judging activity must somehow exceed or surpass the judging activities of other invited experts." The 
Petitioner claims that SCOPS made the same error as it relates to authorship of scholarly articles and 
having a leading or critical role in an organization with a distinguished reputation. 
The Petitioner's claim relies on a mischaracterization ofKazarian and USCIS policy. In determining 
whether a petitioner has established extraordinary ability, we use a two-step analysis to evaluate the 
evidence. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See also generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B). As described in the USCIS Policy Manual, the evidence evaluated in the first step 
analysis is also reviewed in the second step, where the officer must determine whether the person is 
one of that small percentage of the who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and that the 
person has sustained national or international acclaim. Id. Objectively meeting the regulatory criteria 
in the first step does not establish that the person in fact meets the requirements for classification as a 
person with extraordinary ability. Id. In a final merits determination, "In all cases, the petitioner must 
provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence and considerations to demonstrate that the 
evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary ability in the totality of 
the circumstances." 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2). 
Therefore, SCOPS' analysis, in a final merits determination, as to whether the evidence (including 
evidence that demonstrates initial regulatory criteria) shows that the Petitioner meets the requirements 
for classification as a person with extraordinary ability, is a proper application of this framework. In 
fact, the USCIS Policy Manual provides the following example that relates to judging the work of 
others: 
Participating in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization alone, regardless of the circumstances, should satisfy the regulatory 
criteria in the first step of the analysis. However, the second step requires the officer 
to evaluate the person's participation to determine whether it was indicative of the 
person being one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor and enjoying sustained national or international acclaim. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
This analysis is not, as the Petitioner asserts, applying "an abstract comparative benchmark 
disconnected from the actual record." Rather, this analysis reflects the proper analysis in which the 
officer, at step one, determines only if the evidence objectively meets the regulatory criteria and, at 
step two, considers whether the evidence in its totality demonstrates sustained national or international 
acclaim.

The Petitioner has more than ten years of professional experience in software engineering and 
development, including management and leadership with global companies. In addition, his academic 
career in teaching undergraduate and graduate software and engineering courses allows him to guide 
and mentor future technology professionals. However, in considering the totality of the evidence, the 
Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 6739. The Petitioner has not shown 
that he has risen to that small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). The commentary for Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of 
extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive 
documentation than that required for lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704. For the 
reasons discussed below, we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that he enjoys a career 
that meets this very high standard. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386-87 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating 
that the narrow definition reflects the stringency of the extraordinary ability standard).

The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in the IEEE, a professional association of 
engineers dedicated to advancing science and technology, as well as background information about 
the organization, including, its bylaws, and the qualifications needed to be a senior member. The 
evidence shows that senior membership requires at least five years of "significant performance" in the 
field, as indicated by achievements or contributions such as publishing books or papers, developing 
courses, or acting as a team lead or engineer in charge. However, the Petitioner has not explained how 
these qualifications are reflective of acclaim consistent with this highly restrictive classification. See, 
e.g., Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 820 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (upholding that the petitioner's IEEE 
membership did not show extraordinary ability because the eligibility requirements were not stringent 
enough and IEEE senior membership did not reflect sustained national or international acclaim). 
Further, although his membership recognizes his professional experience in the field of engineering, 
the Petitioner has not demonstrated how membership in a single association reflects that he is among 
that small percentage that has risen to the top of his field. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has presented his work in various forms, including a podcast,

The Petitioner's judging experience primarily involves evaluating the work of students in graduate 
and postgraduate thesis papers, rather than established, recognized professionals. The Petitioner did 
not establish that his participation as a judge places him among the small percentage at the very top of 
his field. The record does not explain how his experience in judging graduate candidates would result 
in one who is at the very top ofthe field. The Petitioner did not demonstrate that his judging experience 
contributes to a finding that he has a career of acclaimed work in the field or is indicative of the 
required sustained national or international acclaim. The record does not show that he garnered wide 
attention from the field based on his judging of students. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has authored scholarly articles and displayed his work as a 
lecturer and speaker at technical conferences. However, he did not demonstrate the significance of 
these publications or how these works show that he is at the very top of the field. See Bhanu v. 
Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the Petitioner did 
not show the significance of authorships to reflect that he is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field). He did not demonstrate, for instance, that his work brought wide praise from critics, 
drew notable crowds at conferences, or raised conference attendance, or was responsible for the 
success of the events. The Petitioner does not place his work in context in his field to show that it 
reflects a career ofacclaimed work and sustained national or international acclaim, or that he is among 
that small percentage who has risen to the top of the field. 
The Petitioner's academic research profile lists four publications co-authored by the Petitioner, with 
1,892 "reads" and one citation. The viewing statistics for the Petitioner's thesis reflect 213 "visits" 
and 149 "downloads" from January 2021 to July 2025. Although citations demonstrate that the field 
has taken some interest in the Petitioner's work, they are not an automatic indicator that the Petitioner's 
work has been recognized in the field. Here, the number of citations to the Petitioner's work is not 
reflective, and the Petitioner has not demonstrated, that his work has been ofmajor significance in the 
field or that his publications have garnered him sustained national or international acclaim.

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(8)(2) (providing 
that some evidence weighs more favorably on its own, while other evidence is more persuasive when 
viewed with other evidence). Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary 
ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visas are intended to be available to only an 
exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has
