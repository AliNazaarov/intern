The Petitioner, a technology company, 1 seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an alien of extraordinary 
ability in business. 2 This 0-1 nonimmigrant visa classification is available to aliens with extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
Section 101(a)(15)(O)(i), 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(O)(i). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy 
the initial evidentiary requirements of submitting evidence of the Beneficiary's receipt of a major, 
internationally recognized award, or at least three of the eight possible forms of documentation listed 
under 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B)(l)-(8). See also 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(A). The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter ofChristo's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 101(a)(l5)(O)(i) of the Act establishes 0-1 classification for an alien of extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics that has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim, whose achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation, and who seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability. U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) regulations define "extraordinary ability in the 
field of science, education, business, or athletics" as "a level of expertise indicating that the person is one 
of the small percentage who have arisen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 
214.2( o )(3)(ii).

In addition, DHS regulations set forth initial evidentiary criteria for establishing an alien's sustained 
national or international acclaim and recognition for achievements in the field of endeavor. 
A petitioner may submit evidence either of the alien's receipt of "a major, internationally recognized 
award, such as a Nobel Prize," or at least three of the eight listed categories of documents. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(A)-(B). If the petitioner demonstrates that a particular criterion does not readily 
apply to the alien's occupation, it may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(C). 
The petitioner's submission of documents satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria does not, in and of 
itself: establish the alien's eligibility for 0-1 classification. See 59 Fed. Reg. 41818 (Aug. 15, 1994) 
('The evidence submitted by the petitioner is not the standard for the classification, but merely the 
mechanism to establish whether the standard has been met."). Accordingly, where a petitioner 
provides qualifying evidence satisfying the initial evidentiary requirements, we will determine 
whether the totality of the record and the quality of the evidence show sustained national or 
international acclaim such that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Section 10l(a)(15)(O)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii), (iii). 3 
II. ANALYSIS 
Pages 5 and 6 of the petition indicate that the petitioning entity seeks to employ the Beneficiary as its 
"Vice President of Strategy and Business Development" for two years, compensating him with an 
annual wage of $100,000. The record includes an employment offer letter, providing that the 
Beneficiary "will be responsible directly and indirectly for bringing revenue into the company from 
all business lines ... and via all sales channels ...." The employment offer also lists the Beneficiary's 
other forms of compensation, such as commission, stock grant, and bonus. 
The Petitioner does not claim or establish that the Beneficiary has received a major, internationally 
recognized award. See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(A). As such, the Petitioner must satisfy the initial 
evidentiary requirements by submitting documents showing that the Beneficiary meets at least three 
of the eight regulatory criteria listed under 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B)(l)-(8). SCOPS determined 
that the Beneficiary met none of the eight regulatory criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that 
he meets the following three criteria. 4

B. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B)(5) requires the Petitioner to submit "[e]vidence of the 
alien's original scientific, scholarly, or business-related contributions of major significance in the 
field." USCIS Policy Manual explains that "[e]vidence that the beneficiary's work was fonded, 
patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily 
establish, on its own, that the work is ofmajor significance to the field." See generally 2 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at M.4(C)(2). Evidence that the beneficiary's work "has provoked widespread 
commentary on its importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been 
highly cited relative to other works in that field, may be probative of the significance of the 
beneficiary's contributions to the field of endeavor." See generally id. USCIS Policy Manual farther 
provides that "evidence that the beneficiary developed a patented technology that has attracted 
significant attention or commercialization may establish the significance of the beneficiary's original 
contribution to the field." See generally id. In short, the Petitioner must demonstrate that the 
Beneficiary's contributions are both original and of major significance in the field of business.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the initial evidentiary requirements of submitting at least three of the 
eight forms ofdocumentation listed under 8 C.F .R. § 214.2( o )(3)(iii)(B)( 1)-( 8). Because the Petitioner 
has not met the initial evidentiary requirements, we need not provide, and therefore reserve, a totality 
determination to consider whether the Beneficiary has sustained national or international acclaim and 
is one ofthe small percentage who has arisen to the very top ofthe field. See Section 101(a)(15)(O)(i) 
of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii) and (iii); see also INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) 
(per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that 
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). Consequently, the Petitioner has not established the 
Beneficiary's eligibility for the 0-1 visa classification as an alien of extraordinary ability in business. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
