Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a management analyst, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers 
(petition), concluding the Petitioner had not established she had a major, internationally recognized 
award, or that she met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by apreponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de nova. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability if: they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United 
States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit 
the United States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).1 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the smal I percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing atwo-step review where the documentation 
is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claims extraordinary ability as a management analyst and indicated she is the owner of 
acompany in Brazil. She has not indicated or established that she has received amajor, internationally 
recognized award under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), she must therefore satisfy at least three of the alternate 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner asserted that she met the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v), (viii), and (ix). 
The Director determined the Petitioner did not meet any of the criteria, and we agree. 
1. Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards or Prizes

whether the prizes or awards were granted for excellence in the field, we may consider factors such 
as: the criteria used to grant the awards or prizes; the national or international significance of the 
awards or prizes in the field; the number of awardees or prize recipients; and limitations on 
competitors. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1) (providing, as guidance, 
relevant considerations in determining whether prizes or awards are for excellence in the field of 
endeavor) .

2. Membership in Associations 
In order to satisfy the plain language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), petitioners must demonstrate 
(i) membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought; and (ii) the association 
requires outstanding achievements of their members as judged by recognized national or international 
experts in their disciplines or fields. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) 
(providing, as guidance, relevant considerations in determining the criterion). In the record below, the 
Petitioner submitted her Brazilian "work and social security card" (card) and referenced her work in 
Brazil as fulfilling this criterion. The Director determined that the Petitioner's evidence did not 
demonstrate membership requiring outstanding achievements and her card demonstrating compulsory 
registration with a governing authority did not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that having the card is a government-endorsed acknowledgement of 
a professional's ability to perform complex functions within their field. However, her assertion was 
not corroborated by the record and does not address how she satisfies this criterion. For example, she 
does not explain how her having a card issued by the government of Brazil demonstrates her 
"membership in an association in the field" of management analysts. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) (providing, as guidance, examples of associations that may meet the 
requirements as including certain professional associations or fellowships with certain organizations 
or institutions). Nor does the Petitioner establish that the criteria to obtain the card required her to 
demonstrate outstanding achievement as judged by recognized experts in the field. Here the record 
includes a copy of the relevant law requiring individuals to obtain the card "for the exercise of any 
job" and states that in order to have a profession entered on the card, the individual needs to provide, 
in relevant part, proof of qualification for the profession. This evidence does not demonstrate that a 
prerequisite to obtaining a card requires outstanding achievement as judged by recognized experts. 
Rather, demonstrating that membership is based on a level of education, and/or a requirement for 
employment in the occupation may be concluded as not meeting the requirements of this criterion. Id. 
(providing, as guidance, relevant factors weighing against concluding membership in the association 
was based on outstanding achievements in the field). As a result, the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
she satisfies this criterion.

3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
In order to meet 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must establish that she not only made original 
contributions but that they have been of major significance in the field. Relevant supporting evidence 
may include, for example, published materials, testimonials, letters, and affidavits about the 
significance ofthe person's original work; documentation that the person's original work was cited at 
a level indicative of major significance in the field; patents or licenses deriving from the person's work 
or evidence ofcommercial use ofthe person's work. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(1) (providing, as guidance, relevant considerations and examples of evidence that may 
establish this criterion). In support of this criterion the Petitioner submitted her academic record, and 
letters of recommendation. The Director concluded that the evidence submitted did not demonstrate 
her contributions as original and of major significance to the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that she provided original methodologies to the management analyst 
field by, for example, playing a critical role in implementing quality management systems for one 
employer, introducing novel approaches to optimize operations to an organization she consulted for, 
developing business related solutions for the city of I IBrazil, and employing innovative 
strategies to ensure her company's continued growth. However, she does not explain how her 
contributions were original. Rather, she references her recommendation letters, claiming they 
demonstrate the originality of her contributions, which have been recognized in the industry. 
However, the letters were authored by former colleagues who do not sufficiently detail their 
knowledge and expertise in the field and generally speak to her skills and contributions to her 
employers, but do not specifically identify any original contributions by the Petitioner or explain how 
those contributions are of major significance in the field. As a result, the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
she satisfies this criterion. 
4. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires a petitioner first demonstrate that she has 
performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, establishment, or a division or department 
of an organization or establishment. A supporting role may be considered critical if the petitioner's 
performance in the role is (or was) important. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(1) (providing, as guidance, examples of leading or critical roles and considerations for 
evaluating the evidence). To establish she played a critical role, the Petitioner must establish that she 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. Id. 
Letters from persons with personal knowledge of the significance of the petitioner's leading or critical 
role can be particularly helpful as long as the letters contain detailed and probative information that 
specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization, establishment, division, or 
department was leading or critical. Id. The Petitioner submitted recommendation letters in support of 
this criterion. The Director discussed the letters, noting they specified her roles as supportive, and 
determined that, while they commented on her assisting her employers in achieving a certification, 
obtaining a trophy, and reducing costs on a project, they did not establish her roles were critical. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the recommendation letters evidence her leading or critical role 
by, for example, demonstrating her leading projects, evaluating operational processes, identifying 
4 
inefficiencies, and implementing managing practices. However, the letters do not provide details on 
these tasks. Even the letter that credited her for helping the company win an award was vague on her 
contributions, only claiming she "chaired the process." A separate letter by one of the examiners for 
the award noted that the Petitioner, who was an administrative coordinator for the company, drafted 
the report summarizing management processes. However, this author did not describe what role the 
report played in the company obtaining the award. As a result, the submitted letters lack detailed 
probative information demonstrating the Petitioner's contributions were of significant importance to 
an organization or establishment's activities to demonstrate she played a leading or critical role. 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) then requires the Petitioner establish that the organization 
or establishment, or the department or division for which the person holds or held a leading or critical 
role, has adistinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). Here again, the Petitioner referenced 
her recommendation letters. However, the Director determined the Petitioner's evidence did not 
demonstrate how the organizations she worked for were considered distinguished. On appeal, the 
Petitioner merely restates that the organizations she worked for were distinguished without evidence 
to corroborate her assertions. The Petitioner has therefore not satisfied this criterion. 
5. Commanded Significantly High Remuneration 
In order to meet 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix), petitioners must demonstrate that their salary or 
remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. The Petitioner 
submitted a declaration dated August 2022 by her accountant stating she "has income from 
withdrawals and rents totaling R$ 15,000.00 (fifteen thousand reals) per month." She also provided 
copies of a salary report from[ ~or a business administrator, demonstrating a monthly salary 
range for this occupation as R$ 2,549.64 to 10,519.75, depending on the size of the company and years 
of experience. The Director determined that the Petitioner did not establish that her salary is high 
compared to individuals with a similar work history and years of experience. 
On appeal, the Petitioner re-asserts that she has provided industry specific salary data and her salary 
is well above professionals in her field. The Petitioner does not, however, explain why she provided 
salary information for business administrators instead of management analysts. Moreover, the 
Petitioner's resume evidences that she has been the owner of a company since 2017. Her personal 
salary data from 2022 is representative of her role as owner of a company. The record does not contain 
information regarding her company, and what it does, or her role, other than owner, but she lists her 
duties as: being responsible for product creation and development; creation and monitoring of social 
networks; direct and consigned sales; and fashion events and brand fairs. The Petitioner does not 
claim the duties she performs as owner is that of a management analyst. For all these reasons, the 
Petitioner has not shown that she meets this criterion. 
B. Summary of Evidentiary Criteria and Reserved Issue 
The Petitioner has not established that she has received a major, internationally recognized award 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), or that she satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we need not provide the type of final merits determination 
referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
5 
ultimate decision); see also Matter of L-A-C-, 26 l&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to 
reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing toward the top. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2) (explaining "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor"). USCIS has long held that even athletes performing 
at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of 
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the record as awhole does not demonstrate 
a level of recognition that indicates the required sustained national or international acclaim or 
demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not 
otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor. 
Ill. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
