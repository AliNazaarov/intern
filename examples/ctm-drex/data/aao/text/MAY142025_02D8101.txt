Form 1-129, Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker (Extraordinary Ability- 0) 
The Petitioner, a tattoo studio, seeks to classify the Beneficiary as a tattoo artist of extraordinary 
ability. To do so, the Petitioner pursues 0-1 nonimmigrant classification, available to individuals who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 101(a)(l5)(O)(i), 8 U.S.C. § 110l(a)(15)(O)(i). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish 
the Beneficiary's satisfaction of the initial evidentiary criteria applicable to individuals of 
extraordinary ability in the arts: nomination for or receipt of a significant national or international 
award, or at least three of six possible forms of documentation. The matter is now before us on appeal. 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
As relevant here, section 10l(a)(l5)(O)(i) ofthe Act establishes 0-1 classification for an individual who 
has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics, which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim, whose achievements have been recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation, and who seeks to enter the United States to continue work 
in the area of extraordinary ability. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) regulations define 
"extraordinary ability in the field of arts" as "distinction," and "distinction" as "a high level of 
achievement in the field of arts evidenced by a degree of skill and recognition substantially above that 
ordinarily encountered to the extent that a person described as prominent is renowned, leading, or well­
known in the field of arts." See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii). Next, DHS regulations set forth alternative 
initial evidentiary criteria for establishing a beneficiary's sustained acclaim and the recognition of 
achievements. A petitioner may submit evidence either of nomination for or receipt of "significant 
national or international awards or prizes" such as "an Academy Award, an Emmy, a Grammy, or a 
Director's Guild Award," or at least three of six listed categories of documents. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(A)-(B). A petitioner may submit comparable evidence if the criteria do not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation. See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C). 
The submission of documents satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria does not, in and of itself: 
establish eligibility for 0-1 classification. See 59 Fed. Reg. 41818, 41820 (Aug. 15, 1994) ("The 
evidence submitted by the petitioner is not the standard for the classification, but merely the 
mechanism to establish whether the standard has been met."). Accordingly, where a petitioner 
provides qualifying evidence satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria, we will determine whether the 
totality ofthe record and the quality ofthe evidence shows extraordinary ability in the arts. See section 
10l(a)(l5)(o)(i) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii), (iv). 
II. ANALYSIS 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not demonstrate the Beneficiary's nomination for, or receipt of, 
significant national or international awards or prizes under 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(A). In addition, 
SCOPS concluded the Petitioner established the Beneficiary's eligibility for only two of the five 
claimed alternative criteria, in which at least three must be fulfilled. On appeal, the Petitioner contends 
the Beneficiary received significant national or international awards or prizes and satisfies three 
additional categories of evidence.

D. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(6) 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(6) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has either 
commanded a high salary or will command a high salary or other substantial remuneration for services 
in relation to others in the field, as evidenced by contracts or other reliable evidence." To demonstrate 
that the petitioner meets this criterion, the petitioner should provide appropriate evidence establishing 
that the beneficiary's past or future compensation is or will be high relative to others working in the 
field. 13 
Regarding the Beneficiary's past compensation, the Petitioner provided an "Income Certification," 
including monthly invoices, for the Beneficiary's income from February 2023 to January 2024 for "his 
Principal Business Activity Personal Services Related to Tattoo Art and Landscaping Tattooing" in 
I !Argentina. As comparable evidence, the Petitioner submitted screenshots from 
worldsalaries.com for tattoo artist salaries in Argentina. 
In the denial, SCOPS stated: 
You provided a "declaration" from an Argentinean CPA who indicates that the 
beneficiary, in the past, has earned $13,098,692 in Argentinean currency. However, 
this salary equates to approximately $14,526 per year in US dollars. At an annual

The record includes a printout from World Salaries, which indicates only 25% oftattoo 
artists in Argentina earn more than 294,300 ARS. However, while it is noted that the 
record included a "declaration" from an Argentinean CPA, which states the beneficiary 
has earned $13,098,692 in Argentinean currency, the record does not demonstrate how 
the beneficiary has earned a significant salary amount as compared to the field as a 
whole, not just in one regional area. While the documentation establishes the 
beneficiary has earned more than the average tattoo artist in Argentina, the record does 
not establish how this relates to the field as a whole, not just one geographic area. 
Although SCOPS converted the Beneficiary's Argentinean salary into United States currency and 
determined that it was "not a significant salary amount," policy guidance states that USCIS should 
evaluate persons working outside ofthe United States based on wage statistics or comparable evidence 
relevant to the applicable work location, rather than by simply converting the salary to U.S. dollars 
and then viewing whether that salary would be considered high in the United States. 14 Thus, the 
appropriate comparison is to liken the Beneficiary's salary with the salary of other tattoo artists in 
Argentina rather than comparing the Beneficiary's foreign salary to tattoo artists in the United States. 15 
Here, the Petitioner offered evidence reflecting that the Beneficiary commanded a high salary in 
relation to other tattoo artists in Argentina. According to the submitted World Salaries evidence, the 
highest average annual salary for tattoo artists in Argentina is 325,800 ARS, in which the Beneficiary 
earned 40 times that amount. Because the Petitioner demonstrated the past aspect, we need not 
determine whether the Beneficiary qualifies for the prospective part of this criterion. 16 
Accordingly, the Petitioner established the Beneficiary satisfies this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Because the Petitioner has demonstrated that the Beneficiary fulfills at least three categories of 
evidence, we remand this proceeding so that SCOPS can review the totality of the record and the 
quality of the evidence to determine whether the Petitioner has shown the Beneficiary's extraordinary 
ability in the arts - sustained national or international acclaim, received a high level of achievement, 
and recognized as being prominent, renowned, leading, or well-known in the field of arts. See section
