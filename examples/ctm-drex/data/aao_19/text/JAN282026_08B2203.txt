Appeal of Service Center Operations Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a law and policy specialist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that he met at 
least three of the ten regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now 
before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a law and policy specialist in the field of technology law and artificial intelligence 
(AI) ethics in Central Asia. In his statement, the Petitioner explains that he plans to continue running 
his business, which conducts research, surveys, and generates reports, "to provide valuable insights 
and assistance to the U.S. business community, state institutions, and individual scholars interest in 
Central Asia and China regions and sectors of New Innovation Technologies in these regions." He 
also states that he plans to "establish strategic partnerships and collaborations with organizations and 
institutions within the United States to drive interdisciplinary research initiatives." 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the 
initial evidentiary criteria and we agree. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts he meets the evidentiary 
criteria relating to awards, membership, published materials, original contributions, leading or critical 
role, and salary. 1

nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards criterion. However, as the Petitioner did not

A. Memberships 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based on his participation in arbitration panels and 
fellowships, as well as his senior membership with the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers 
(IEEE), and life membership with thel IHigh Court Bar Association. Regarding the arbitration 
panels, the Petitioner asserts that they are "field-relevant legal associations and that membership is 
selective, evaluative, and based on demonstrated achievement." However, the Petitioner has not 
established that being included on a list of arbitrators is a type of membership in an association. See 
Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *4 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025) 
(upholding plaintiff did not show a group of individuals hired or invited to serve as advisors to a school 
is considered an association in the field ofendeavor). Nevertheless, we have reviewed the documentation 
about the various arbitration panels and observe that they do not show that any of these panels require 
outstanding achievements from their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields. For instance, to be considered for the World Intellectual Property 
Organization (WIPO) List of Neutrals, the WIPO Center Neutrals Committee considers the following: 
legal or technical qualifications, professional experience, professional experience in dispute resolution, 
publications, and professional memberships. Moreover, the Russian Arbitration Center only requires its 
arbitrators to be lawyers, and the Brunei Darussalam Arbitration Centre requires its applicants to 
demonstrate "an appropriate level of expertise and experience in arbitration and be a good standing [] 
character." While the Petitioner contends on appeal that these "institutions appoint members based on 
demonstratable subject-matter expertise, cross-border experience, and a high degree of trust in the 
individual's legal and ethical competence," he does not explain how these standards represent outstanding 
achievements. Moreover, the Petitioner claims that the "nominees are subject to scrutiny from 
institutional boards and selection committees comprising of international experts," but he does not show 
that these "institutional boards" or "selection committees" are comprised of recognized national or 
international experts in the field. The Petitioner submits no evidence to support these assertions.

For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner does not establish he fulfills this criterion. 
B. Published Materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l).

On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he submitted numerous pieces of evidence in support ofthis criterion 
and that SCOPS applied "overly rigid standards" and "completely overlooked properly documented and

eligibility requirements of the petition at the time of filing. 8 C.F.R. §§ 103.2(b)(l), (12). Therefore, we 
will not consider them here. 
Regarding the China Global Television Network (CGTN) article from 2018, while it included the title,

professional or major trade publication or other major media. The Petitioner submitted printouts from 
CGTN' s website which included background information about the publication, but he did not submit the 
publication's circulation data to demonstrate that it qualifies as a major media publication in the country 
of publication. 
Given the above, the Petitioner has not established that he meets this criterion.

E. Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner submitted documentation indicating that he is a "foreign highly qualified specialist," 
along with printouts from sites including Glassdoor.com and World Salaries for lecturers in 
Uzbekistan. However, the Petitioner did not offer comparable salary evidence pertaining to the "law 
and policy specialists." To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must show that he has received a high 
salary in relation to other law and policy specialists rather than lecturers. Both precedent and case law 
support this application of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( considering a professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); 
see also Skokos v. US. Dept. ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding 
salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); 
Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. III. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus 
other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. III. 1995) (comparing salary of 
NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). Because the Petitioner did not provide 
sufficient evidence demonstrating the salaries of other law and policy specialists, he did not establish 
that he commands a high salary in relation to others in his field. Therefore, the Petitioner has not 
established that he meets this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
