Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an occupational health and safety specialist, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields . Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Service Center Operations (SCOPS) 
denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three initial evidentiary 
criteria for this classification, he did not show as required bis sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrate that he is amongst the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if be or she bas extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(b)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner does not claim a one-time achievement, such as a major, internationally recognized 
award. So, he must provide evidence of meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria contained at 
8 C.F.R. § 203.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed eligibility for the classification based on the 
following criteria:

• authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other

A. "Documentation ofthe alien's membership in associations in the field for which class[fication is 
sought, which require outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national 
or international experts in their disciplines or fields. "8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or fields." 
USCIS determines if the association for which the person claims present or past membership requires 
that members have outstanding achievements in the field as judged by recognized experts in that field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The 
petitioner must show that membership in the association requires outstanding achievements in the field 
for which classification is sought, as judged by recognized national or international experts. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Relevant factors that may lead to a conclusion 
that the person's membership in the association(s) was not based on outstanding achievements in the 
field include, but are not limited to, instances where the person's membership was based solely on the 
following factors (by themselves or in the aggregate): a level of education or years of experience in a 
particular field; the payment of a fee or by subscribing to an association's publications; and a 
requirement, compulsory or otherwise, for employment in certain occupations, as commonly seen with 
union membership or guild affiliation for actors. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). 
In the initial petition, the Petitioner claimed that certifications he held that were issued by the 
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), National Examination Board in 
Occupational Safety and Health (NEBOSH) (UK), Sociedade Portuguesa de Seguranca e Higiene 
Occupacionas (SPOSHO) (Portugal), Board of Certified Safety Professionals (BSCP), Canada Safety 
Council (CSC), and National Association of Safety Professionals (NASP) satisfied his eligibility under 
the criterion. The Petitioner conceded that membership in CSC and NASP did not serve as "primary 
evidence" of the Petitioner's claim to eligibility under the criteria, but "provide[d] corroborative 
weight by demonstrating" participation, adherence, and development in his chosen profession. 
In its RFE, SCOPS listed the Petitioner's certifications except for those issued by the BCSP. SCOPS 
noted the record did not contain evidence demonstrating that certification or admission by the 
certifying bodies required outstanding achievement as judged by recognized national or international 
experts. 
In response to the RFE, the Petitioner did not provide any evidence demonstrating that certification or 
admission by OSHA, NEBOSH, SPOSHO, CSC, or NASP required outstanding achievement as 
judged by recognized national or international experts. Instead, the Petitioner focused on SCOPS 
apparent omission ofhis BCSP certifications. The Petitioner asserted that holding BCSP certifications 
"constitutes formal inclusion within a defined and selective professional group." He further stated 
that BCSP bylaws provide a process to establish minimum education, experience, and training 
requirements, as well as determine the qualification of applicants and verify their competency. 
According to the bylaws, certification reflects that "an individual has met a set of standards and 
4 
demonstrated competency by examination after completing an application and evaluation process." 
On appeal, the Petitioner reiterates his contentions. The Petitioner's assertions are not well taken. 
As stated above, the USCIS Policy Manual precludes "requirement[s], compulsory or otherwise, for 
employment in certain occupations" as evidence of outstanding achievements required for 
membership. The requirements as set forth in the BCSP bylaws are for, as the Petitioner stated, 
"inclusion within a defined and selective professional group." So, it is plain that BCSP certification, 
at either one or both of the levels the Petitioner holds, is not demonstrative of membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 
However, even if were to consider BCSP certification as something other than a professional 
certification, we would still conclude that it did not constitute a membership in associations in the field 
for which for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements oftheir members, 
as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. The evidence 
in the record does not demonstrate how educational, experiential, and training "prerequisites, 
examination-based proof of competence, ethical compliance, and ongoing recertification" constitute 
outstanding achievements. Moreover, the record does not identify the recognized national or 
international experts making "eligibility determinations" for certification by BCSP. BCSP's bylaws 
stated that a "Board has final authority for the purpose of examining and certifying candidates and 
credential holders." But the BCSP Board's members are unidentified. So, we cannot determine if 
they are recognized as national or international experts in their field. 
For all the reasons discussed above, we conclude that the Petitioner does not meet this criterion. 2

C. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
leading role may be demonstrated by evidence that establishes the individual is or was a leader within 
an organization or establishment, or a division or department thereof. An alien may fulfill a critical 
role in an organization or establishment, or a division or department thereof under this criterion. And 
a critical role may encompass a supporting role. But the evidence must establish that the alien's 
contributions through the critical or supporting role are of "significant importance to the outcome of 
the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization 
or establishment." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner avers that "the record demonstrates that [the Petitioner's] roles at I._________. 
.__________________.were both leading and critical" as described in the criterion. The 
Petitioner held the position of senior occupational safety engineer at I I He also claims that he 
served as a safety manager at I I In the United States, the Petitioner claims that he has 
continued to serve in leading and critical roles as I I site safety and health officer and safety 
specialist atl I But none of the Petitioner's roles support his claim under this criterion. 
The evidence the Petitioner submitted is insufficient for us to evaluate if the Petitioner occupied 
leading or critical roles at the entities he identified. The record does not contain sufficient evidence, 
such as a job description, an organizational line and block chart, or other similar evidence to establish 
the level at which his role was situated in the employer's hierarchy. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 
3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *7 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025) (upholding plaintiff did not 
provide sufficient evidence about his overall responsibilities and rank within the organizational 
hierarchy to establish his roles were either leading or critical). See also Chivukula v. Allen, No. 3:22-
9 
CV-0381-S, 2023 WL 4504601, at *6 (N.D. Tex. May 1, 2023) (finding USCIS did not add novel 
requirements by stating that the plaintiff did not include documentary evidence establishing whether 
his role was more leading or critical than other individuals at the same level) and Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 
558 F. Supp. 3d at 189 (finding it reasonable to request specific duties as evidence of a leading or 
critical role). 
The evidence the Petitioner submitted consisted of letters from individuals who had worked with him 
or occupied administrative roles at the employers. Letters from persons with personal knowledge of 
the significance of the person's leading or critical role can be particularly helpful when evaluating a 
petitioner's claim to the criterion, so long as the letters contain detailed and probative information that 
specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization, establishment, division, or 
department was leading or critical. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). But 
such letters lack probative value ifthey do not specifically address how the role was leading or critical. 
SeeAgrawalv. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *21 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (finding 
that testimonial letters are not particularly valuable unless the letters specifically address how

In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the person's salary or remuneration is 
high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). USCIS does not interpret the phrase "has commanded" to mean that the 
person must have already earned such salary or remuneration in order to meet the criterion. Rather, a 
credible contract or job offer showing prospective salary or remuneration may establish that the person 
has been able to command such compensation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). Evidence regarding whether a petitioner's compensation is high relative to what of others 
working in the field can include compensation surveys and organizational justifications so long as the 
description of the occupation matches, the validity of the survey is credible and reliable, and the 
location, currency, and salary rate being measured are comparable. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

The Petitioner did not meet the evidentiary standard .So, we are not required to make a final merits 
determination. But, as the Petitioner's appeal contends that he has sustained acclaim, will continue to 
work in the area of his claimed extraordinary ability, and will substantially benefit the United States 
of America prospectively as a permanent resident, we will conduct a final merits determination. Upon 
our de novo review of the record we conclude that, even if the Petitioner had established that he met 
the evidentiary standard we would nonetheless conclude he did not demonstrate eligibility for the 
classification because the evidence does not indicate he has sustained national or international acclaim 
and is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofhis field ofendeavor with achievements recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if his or her successes are sufficient to, by a preponderance of the evidence, 
demonstrate that he or she has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor through sustained national 
or international acclaim, that he or she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor, and that his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
The record as a whole does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's achievements are reflective of a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990). As stated above, the Petitioner claims to have served as a judge ofthe work ofothers. 
But an evaluation of the significance of his experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence 
indicates the required extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 
F. 3d at 1121-22.4 The Petitioner listed several occasions where he served in a judging capacity and 
so met the plain language of the criterion. However, the Petitioner did not establish that these judging 
opportunities contribute to a finding that he has a career of acclaimed work in the field or indicate his 
sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not show, for example, how his judging 
experience compares to others at the very top of the field. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner 
apart from others in his field, such as evidence that he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging 
recognized, acclaimed experts in his field, the Petitioner has not shown that his judging experience 
places him amongst that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Hamal v. United States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding the 
petitioner did not show that he served as a judge of renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists 
or the competitions were considered prestigious or attracted significant attention by the field). 
Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The record 
reflects the Petitioner presented evidence showing that he has published articles in scholarly 
publications. But the Petitioner did not show the significance ofhis authorships or how his publication 
record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Jafarov v. United 
States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 
5, 2024) (finding it reasonable for USCIS to focus on the fact that a publication record of six 
publications over seven years inconsistent with a career of acclaimed work and sustained national or

The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. USCIS has long held that even 
athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the statutory standards for 
classification as an individual of"extraordinary ability." Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954. Whilst 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification 
sought, we find the record insufficient to demonstrate that he has sustained national or international 
acclaim and is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 5
