Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a medical doctor who studies cerebral venous diseases, requests classification under 
the employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with 
"extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
two often initial evidentiary criteria - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that he satisfied two additional evidentiary requirements and demonstrated 
sufficient acclaim and recognition for classification as an alien with extraordinary ability. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he met at least three alternative evidentiary criteria but that the record does 
not ultimately establish his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. We will therefore dismiss the 
appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step review process to be 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
The record shows that Chinese medical universities awarded the Petitioner bachelor's and master of 
medicine degrees and a doctor ofscience degree in neurobiology. He completed a neurology residency 
at a Chinese hospital and continues to work for the facility as a doctor. Between 2014 and the petition's 
filing in April 2025, the Petitioner first-authored 22 research papers regarding cerebral cardiovascular 
disease and reviewed 14 articles by others in the field before their papers' publications. Also by the 
petition's filing, he and co-researchers received three approved patents for devices in the field, with 
three additional applications pending. 
The Petitioner proposes to continue working in his field in the United States. He stated that he seeks 
to enhance understanding, diagnosis, and research of the conditions of cerebral venous sinus 
thrombosis and other brain venous pathologies. He said that he would focus on improving early 
detection methods, understanding molecular mechanisms, and developing more effective therapeutical 
approaches. 
B. The Alternative Evidentiary Requirements 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten alternative evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner submitted evidence of: 
• Published materials about him and his work in his field; and 
• His authorship of scholarly articles in the field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (vi). 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that he also submitted evidence of his:

• Original contributions of major significance in the field.

To satisfy an evidentiary criterion, the Petitioner must objectively meet the parameters ofan applicable 
regulatory description. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B). "[N]either USCTS nor [the] 
AAO [ Administrative Appeals Office] may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 
(9th Cir. 20 l0). 
We will first review SCOPS' finding regarding the evidentiary requirement at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv). This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually 
or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which 
classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner submitted copies of e-mail messages indicating that, upon request from scientific 
journals, he reviewed scholarly articles by other neuroscience researchers before their papers' 
publications. Under USCTS policy: 
Examples ofjudging the work of others may include, but are not limited to: . . . Peer 
reviewing for a scholarly journal, as evidenced by a request from the journal to the 
person to do the review, accompanied by proof that the review was actually completed. 
6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)( l ), Criterion 4. 
SCOPS issued a request for additional evidence (RFE), finding that the record did not answer the 
following questions: "Why was [the Petitioner] selected as a judge? Was the opportunity to judge 
prestigious? Was the opportunity to judge given because ofhis extraordinary accomplishments? [And 
w ]hat kind of press coverage did the events he judged receive?" The Petitioner's RFE response 
included additional information about the rankings and peer review guidelines ofthe scientific journals 
that asked him to review articles. SCOPS, however, found the evidence insufficient, stating that it did 
not answer any of the RFE's four questions. 
As the Petitioner argues on appeal, SCOPS erred by requesting evidence that the criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) does not require. SCOPS' inquiries into the reasons for his selection as a peer 
reviewer and the nature of his judging opportunities sought evidence beyond the regulatory 
requirement. As previously indicated, "neither USCIS nor [the] AAO may unilaterally impose novel 
substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1121. The Petitioner's e-mail evidence showed that scholarly journals asked him to review 
the work of other researchers in his or an allied field and that he completed the reviews. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 4. Thus, contrary to SCOPS' finding, the Petitioner submitted 
evidence demonstrating that he judged others' work in his or an allied field. 
The Petitioner has met three alternative evidentiary criteria, the minimum number required for a final 
merits determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not determine whether he also

Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely

SCOPS did not make a final merits determination on the Petitioner's filing. We will therefore now do

C. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. USCIS must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id.

Further, a vice president of the hospital where the Petitioner works stated that the Petitioner's research 
on Parkinson's disease indicates that electroacupuncture therapy can continuously regulate neural 
function, significantly improving motor systems and promoting nerve repair. The vice president stated 
that the Petitioner "has deeply validated the therapeutic effects of electroacupuncture on motor defects 
in Parkinson's disease models, providing new treatment targets in this field." 
Despite these advancements, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated sufficient acclaim and 
recognition to place himself among the small percentage at his field's very top. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2) ( defining the term "extraordinary ability" as "a level of expertise indicating that the 
individual is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor"). 
All three experts who provided letters on his behalf are affiliated with the hospital where he works 
and/or the university where he obtained his doctoral degree. "It is generally expected that one whose 
accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have received 
recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the circle of their personal and professional 
acquaintances." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). The record lacks evidence that the Petitioner has 
received sufficient acclaim and recognition for his advancements beyond his workplace and school. 
Also, the Petitioner refers to the three patents on which he has worked and their corresponding 
technologies as his own. But the patent certificates each list at least six other inventors. The university 
vice president referred to the Petitioner as a "key" and "core" member of the vice president's research 
team. But the record does not establish the degree to which the Petitioner contributed to the patented 
technologies. Thus, the evidence of his technological contributions does not demonstrate his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner argues that independent Chinese news articles about him and his work demonstrate his 
receipt of national acclaim and recognition. The record contains copies of two 2024 articles about 
him: one in a health and wellness newspaper; and the other on a popular online news portal. The 
national publications respectively discuss his development of the severity and prognosis scales for 
treating cerebral venous thrombosis in China and a procedure for treating Parkinson's disease with 
dopaminergic neural progenitor cells derived from neural stem cells. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that the two news articles reflect sufficient acclaim and 
recognition to place him among the small percentage at his field's very top. Where a "few" articles 
discussed an electrical engineer's "accomplishments" in his field, a U.S. district court found that that 
evidence "is not consistent with national or international acclaim." Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 
l 19CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020). In contrast, the Acting 
Associate Commissioner ofthe former Immigration and Naturalization Service found that publications 
about a professional golfer supported his classification as an alien with extraordinary ability where 
"[ v ]irtually every major newspaper has covered the petitioner's progress in the world of golf." Matter 
ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,955 (Acting Assoc. Comm'r 1994). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he wrote a section about strokes in two reports on neurological 
diseases that the Chinese government used to formulate health policies. The hospital vice president 
5 
said that the reports also serve as "important references" for scholars on Chinese disease research. The 
Petitioner states that his "selection as an editorial committee member demonstrated that [his] original 
research findings have achieved significant recognition, which indicates the impact and high 
professional esteem for [his] original contributions." 
The record, however, lacks evidence explaining how editorial committee members were selected to 
work on the reports. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("[T]he statements made by the witnesses 
should be corroborated by documentary evidence in the record.") The hospital vice president stated 
that the Petitioner's "writing is clear, well-organized, detailed and illustrated, earning high praise from 
industry experts." But the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his selection to the editorial committee 
or his work on the reports generated sufficient acclaim or recognition to place him among the small 
percentage at his field's very top. 
The record also does not support the Petitioner's claim of his "prestigious membership in a 
professional association recognizing outstanding achievements." He submitted evidence of his senior 
membership in a Chinese stroke organization. He contends that the organization's senior members 
cannot constitute more than 0.5% of the group's total membership and that the organization's 
constitution reserves senior membership "for those who enjoy high academic prestige, and have 
achieved remarkable achievements in the development of discipline." 
The record, however, lacks sufficient corroborating evidence to support the Petitioner's contentions. 
He did not provide a copy of the organization's constitution or other documentary evidence to support 
his claims. He submitted evidence of his membership in numerous other stroke, neuroscience, and 
neurology organizations in China, Europe, and the United States and printouts of online information 
about some of the groups. But the record does not demonstrate that any of the organizations require 
outstanding achievements of their members. Thus, his association memberships do not sufficiently 
support his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
Finally, the Petitioner contends that his publication record is "commensurate with that of leading 
experts." As previously indicated, the record shows that, by the petition's filing, he first-authored 22 
scholarly research papers in his field, which generated more than 300 total citations. But the record 
does not demonstrate that his research's quantity or quality places him among the small percentage at 
his field's very top. For example, the record indicates that the author of each of his expert letters has 
written at least 90 published papers, substantially more than him. He also has not established that he 
has a high number of citations relative to others in his field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. CV 22-103, 
2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (affirming USCIS's finding that a petitioner "did 
not show the significance of his authorships or how his publications compare to others who are viewed 
to be at the very top of their field."); see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("[S]uch evidence 
may indicate a person's high overall standing for the purpose of demonstrating that the person is 
among the small percentage at the top of the field.") 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). The legislature indicated 
that this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the 
very top of their field of endeavor." Id. The Petitioner met the category's initial evidentiary 
requirements and demonstrated his receipt of acclaim and recognition in his field. But, considering 
6 
the evidence as a whole, he has not established his claimed place among the small percentage at his 
field's very top. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary ability 
classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [ a petitioner] 
was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (holding that 
even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not automatically meet the extraordinary 
ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner met the required number of alternative evidentiary requirements. But he did not 
demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
