Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a business legal consultant 1 seeking classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition and dismissed a subsequent motion, 
concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria, after 
a final merits analysis, she did not establish that she had sustained national or international acclaim 
and that she is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. We dismissed a 
subsequent appeal, concluding that upon de novo review, the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of 
ten evidentiary criteria, as previously determined by SCOPS, and that a final merits determination was 
therefore not warranted. Nevertheless, we affirmed SCOPS' decision which was based on a final 
merits determination, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish that she has sustained national or 
international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The 
matter is now before us on motion to reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
A motion to reconsider must be supported by pertinent precedent to establish that our prior decision 
was based on an incorrect application of law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the 
evidence in the record ofproceedings at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Because the 
scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these 
proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements 
and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit.

On motion, the Petitioner contests the correctness of our adverse findings concerning each individual 
criterion, but she does not address our final merits determination where we discussed the criteria within 
the broader context of extraordinary ability, explaining how the totality of the evidence falls short of 
establishing that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small 
percentage at the very top of her field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th 
Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, iffulfilling 
the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
To summarize, we determined that the record contains sufficient evidence demonstrating that the 
Petitioner judged the work of others in her field and authored scholarly articles. However, we found 
that the Petitioner did not establish that her experience judging students' final papers at two 
universities in Brazil garnered wide attention from the field such that she was recognized outside of 
those universities and impacted or influenced her field. And while we acknowledged that the 
Petitioner authored an article for learned persons in her field, we pointed out that her material was 
published in 2003, more than 20 years ago, and does not show that she has sustained national or 
international acclaim. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
We also discussed the Petitioner's contributions to a book, a legal brief she filed with the Supreme 
Federal Court in Brazil, and an article she had published in Revista Academica, an online magazine 
that allows writers to submit their own original articles for publication on the site. However, we 
observed that the Petitioner did not compare her authored works to those of others who are recognized 
as already being at the top in her field to provide context and we found that her legal brief was not 
shown to have been in professional or major trade publications or other major media. We also found 
that the article in Revista Academirn lacks the characteristics of a scholarly article because it does not 
reflect original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse; it lacks footnotes, endnotes, or 
bibliographies; and it does not demonstrate its pre-publication review by experts in the legal and 
business fields. 
Thus, even though we determined that the Petitioner satisfied the elements ofthe judging and scholarly 
articles criteria, we nevertheless concluded that neither her judging experience nor her publication 
record is consistent with having a "career of acclaimed work" as contemplated by Congress. See H.R. 
Rep. No. at 59 and 56 Fed. Reg. at 30704. Importantly, we noted that meeting these criteria does not 
automatically place one at the top of the field, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish that her 
judging experience and publication record demonstrate that she is among the small percentage at the 
very top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. §204.5(h)(2). 
Beyond the criteria that the Petitioner satisfied, we also considered six additional criteria - awards, 
membership, published material about the individual, original contributions, leading or critical role, 
and high salary - to determine whether the totality of the evidence demonstrates eligibility. We then 
concluded that the evidence neither fulfills the requirements of any of the six listed criteria nor 
contributes to an overall finding that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and 
is among the small percentage at the top of her field.

In support of the motion, the Petitioner addresses only our adverse findings concerning the individual 
criteria but declines to address our analysis in the final merits determination, arguing that it is 
impermissible to make an adverse determination "based solely on a subjective merits assessment 
disconnected from the text of the regulation." In support of her assertions regarding "the application 
of extra-regulatory standard at the Final Merits Determination stage," the Petitioner cites a district 
court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), deeming it "highly 
persuasive authority." We disagree with the Petitioner's reliance on this non-precedent decision. 
First, the regulations pertaining to motions to reconsider expressly require that the filing party state 
the reasons for reconsideration and cite "any pertinent precedent decisions" to establish that the 
decision that is the subject of the motion was legally insufficient based on its incorrect application of 
law ofUSCIS policy. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). The Petitioner has not complied with this requirement. 
Further, in contrast to the precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are 
not bound to follow a decision of a United States district court. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 
(BIA 1993). Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, 
unpublished opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att '.Y Gen. United 
States, 147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non­
precedential opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 
n.5 ( 5th Cir. 2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 ( considering the reasoning of district court 
decisions). Importantly, we note that the Mukherji decision was issued by a district court in Nebraska 
and is therefore outside of the Petitioner's jurisdiction, which is in Florida. For these reasons, we are 
not bound by the ruling of Mukherji v. Miller in the instant case and therefore our analysis of the 
instant visa petition under the current two-step review framework provided in Kazarian was not in 
error. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1115. 
As stated earlier, the Petitioner does not address the content of our final merits analysis and only 
contests our reliance on that analysis as a dispositive measure. As such, the Petitioner has failed to 
overcome the adverse findings in our final merits determination. 
On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not established that our previous decision was based on an 
incorrect application of law or policy at the time we issued our decision. Therefore, the motion will 
be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). 
ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
