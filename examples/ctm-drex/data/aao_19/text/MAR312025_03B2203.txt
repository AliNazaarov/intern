Appeal of Nebraska Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an engineer, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in the 
sciences. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes irnrnigrant visas available to aliens who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
The Director of the Nebraska Service Center denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not 
establish either that he received a major, internationally recognized award, or that he satisfied at least 
three of the 10 initial evidentiary criteria. The Petitioner subsequently submitted a combined motion 
to reopen and motion to reconsider, which the Director dismissed. The matter is now before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to irnrnigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner described the endeavor as a plan to operate a startup "heat power and heat pump systems 
firm" that will provide "thermal energy assessments, advanced heat pump designs, energy 
optimization techniques, and pioneering insights into sustainable heating innovations." The Petitioner 
submitted a business plan that indicates the company would operate in New York, with potential 
clients including construction companies, real estate developers, architectural firms, public works, 
office buildings, and similar entities. 
As noted above, the Director concluded the record does not establish the Petitioner received a one­
time achievement of a major, internationally recognized award. The Director further determined that 
the record does not satisfy, in the alternative, at least three of the 10 criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Specifically, the Director concluded that the record does not satisfy the criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(ii), (v)-(vi), and (viii), noting that the Petitioner did not assert eligibility 
under the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii)-(iv), (vii), (ix)-(x). On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts 
that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(ii), (v)-(vi), and (viii). The Petitioner 
does not assert on appeal-and the record does not support the conclusion-that the record satisfies 
the criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii)-(iv), (vii), (ix)-(x). The record does not satisfy at least three 
of the 10 criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) for the reasons discussed below. 
A. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards for Excellence 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 
The Director acknowledged that the record contains "evidence of academic achievements or 
credentials received by the [Petitioner] while pursuing an education." However, the Director noted, 
"Generally, academic honors are not considered to be nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor, because they are generally given to students or early 
career professionals in the field and inherently exclude established professionals who have already 
achieved excellence in the field." The Director concluded that the record does not establish how the 
academic achievements or credentials in the record qualify as nationally or internationally recognized

C. Authorship of Scholarly Articles in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 
The Director acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted various certificates; however, the Director 
noted that the Petitioner did not submit copies of scholarly articles the Petitioner may have authored, 
nor did he submit them on appeal. 1 Moreover, the Director noted that the certificates do not establish 
when any of the articles they reference may have been reviewed, accepted, and published. The 
Director also observed that the record does not establish that the Petitioner authored the articles he 
referenced, or that the publications qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major 
media. Based on those and similar issues, the Director determined that the record does not satisfy the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that he authored two articles published in Academic, thereby 
satisfying the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 2 However, the Petitioner has not established that 
he authored scholarly articles, nor has he established that Academic qualifies as a professional 
publication, major trade publication, or major media publication. The record contains a printed copy 
of a webpage stating that Academic "helps undergraduates, graduate students, doctoral students, 
teachers and researchers to submit their scientific works to the public" and that it "enabl[ es] authors 
to publish articles quickly and efficiently," without reference to a peer-review process. The record 
does not otherwise establish that the referenced articles were peer-reviewed prior to publication. See 
generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) (noting that scholarly articles are generally 
peer reviewed by other experts in the field of specialization). Therefore, the Petitioner has not 
established by a preponderance of the evidence that he authored scholarly articles. 
Additionally, the record does not establish that Academic qualifies as a professional or major trade 
publication or other major media. While the above-referenced webpage states that its intended 
audience is "the public," the record does not otherwise clarify its intended audience, circulation, or 
readership. See id. (noting that the relevant factors in evaluating whether a submitted publication is a 
professional publication or major media include the intended audience (for professional journals) and 
the circulation or readership relative to other media in the field (for major media)). 
Because the record does not establish that the Petitioner authored scholarly articles and that the 
publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication or other major media, it does not 
satisfy the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi).

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not established he received a one-time achievement or, in the alternative, evidence 
that meets at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we need not 
provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS 
v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25; see also Matter ofL-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. at 526 n.7. Nevertheless, we 
have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that he is one of the small percentage who 
has risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. See section 203(b )( 1 )(A) ofthe Act; see also 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
