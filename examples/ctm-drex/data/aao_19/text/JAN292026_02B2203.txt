Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software engineer, requests classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS found that the Petitioner met the 
requested category's initial evidentiary requirements. But, in a final merits determination, SCOPS 
concluded that he did not demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. On appeal, the 
Petitioner contends that he satisfied additional evidentiary criteria and demonstrated sufficient acclaim 
and recognition to establish his place among the small percentage at his field's very top. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that the record does not establish his claimed extraordinary ability in his 
field. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternative evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) then makes a final merits determination. To merit final approval, the 
record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and 
recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022); see generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner 
The record shows that a Ukrainian university issued the Petitioner bachelor's and master's degrees in 
computer science. He has worked as a software developer and engineer for more than 10 years. He 
developed an award-winning mobile application to help Ukrainian medical students study for their 
country's medical exams. He also has written technical guides and blogs on software development 
and has organized developer education initiatives. Now in the United States, the Petitioner works for 
a large financial services company, developing mobile applications. 
The Petitioner states that he wants to continue his software engineering activities in the United States. 
He stated: "Working with top U.S. tech businesses and research companies, I will continue [to] 
develop creative software solutions addressing important problems in sectors including healthcare, 
education, and corporate technology." He also said that "I want to keep working with the U.S. 
developer community to improve open-source projects" and to "encourage cooperation and knowledge 
exchange among American developers." 
B. The Alternative Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore had to meet at least three of the ten alternative evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner met four evidentiary requirements. He 
submitted evidence of: 
• Published materials about himself and his work in his field; 
• His participation as a judge of others' work in the field; 
• His authorship of scholarly articles in the field; and 
• His performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (iv), (vi). (viii). 
1 If an evidentiary criterion does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, they may submit "comparable evidence" 
to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4).

On appeal, the Petitioner claims that he also provided evidence of his: 
• Receipt of nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for his services in his field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v), (vii), (ix). 
The Petitioner has already satisfied the minimum number of evidentiary criteria. We therefore need 
not consider his appellate arguments that he met additional evidentiary requirements. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
We will next review SCOPS' final merits determination. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
To merit final approval as an alien with extraordinary ability, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field-that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to their field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that [they have] achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in 
their field of expertise." Id. users must review all evidence in the totality and consider "any 
potentially relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory 
criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner urges us to follow a U.S. district court decision finding that satisfaction of at 
least three initial evidentiary criteria establishes a presumption that an alien has extraordinary ability. 
See Buletini v. INS, 860 F. Supp. 1222, 1234 (E.D. Mich. 1994). Buletini held: 
Once it is established that the alien's evidence is sufficient to meet three of the criteria 
listed in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the alien must be deemed to have extraordinary ability 
unless [ the immigration service] sets forth specific and substantiated reasons for finding 
that the alien does not meet extraordinary ability. 
Id. 
U.S. district court decisions, however, do not bind users in other matters. See Trump v. CASA, Inc., 
606 U.S. 831, 858 n.17 (2025) (noting that "district court opinions lack precedential force even vis-a.­
vis other judges in the same judicial district"). Thus, we need not follow Buletini in this matter. 
Moreover, since Buletini's issuance more than 30 years ago, users has adopted a different policy to 
adjudicate extraordinary ability petitions. As previously indicated, users now uses a two-step 
analysis. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010). If a petitioner received a 
major international award or meets at least three alternative evidentiary criteria, the Agency then 
3 
determines whether the record- as a whole - establishes "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements sufficient to place the alien among the small percentage at their field's 
very top. Id.; see also Amin, 24 F.4th at 391 (finding USCIS' current adjudication method to be 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). We therefore decline to follow Buletini. 
The record establishes the Petitioner as a very talented software engineer, specializing in mobile 
applications. But the record lacks independent, objective evidence sufficient to place him among the 
small percentage at his field's very top. 
The Petitioner documented that, in 2021, he won a '~-----------~" contest 
sponsored, in part, by an online Ukrainian technology publication. The award stemmed from his 
mobile application for Ukrainian medical students. A panel of software developers and investors, as 
well as the publication's readers, judged the contest. The record shows that the award brought the 
Petitioner international acclaim and recognition. But he has not demonstrated that this single award, 
alone, reflects "sustained" acclaim or recognition commensurate with one of that small percentage at 
his field's very top. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in three international professional associations 
in his field. Two of the memberships, including a senior membership in the Institute of Electrical and 
Electronics Engineers (IEEE), required "significant performance" or "significant achievements" in his 
field for at least five-year periods. But he has not established that the memberships' requirements 
necessarily place him among the small percentage at his field's very top. See Hamal v. United States 
Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *3 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (finding that 
memberships demonstrated an alien's "professional stature" but "did not establish the exceptional 
level of distinction required for this extraordinary-ability visa."); see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. 
Supp. 3d 798, 820 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding that USCIS properly "explained that [the petitioner's] 
IEEE membership did not show extraordinary ability because the eligibility requirements were not 
stringent enough and that he lacked evidence that he became an IEEE senior member because of his 
sustained national or international acclaim"). 
The record contains copies of nine online published articles about the Petitioner and his work in his 
field. The record indicates that, not only readers in his home country of the Ukraine, but also some in 
other nations viewed the writings. But the articles, by themselves, do not place him at his field's very 
top. Seven of the nine articles were published in 2024, suggesting that they do not reflect "sustained" 
acclaim as the requested immigrant visa category requires. See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland 
Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal, 
supra) (affirming USCTS' finding that a petitioner did not show that "his press coverage is indicative 
of a level of success consistent with being" at his field's top). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he participated as a panel judge at two software development 
competitions. But the record does not demonstrate that the competitions held prestige or attracted 
significant attention in the field. See Joseph v. Dir. ofTexas Serv. Ctr., USCIS, No. 24-40249, 2025 
WL 458001, at *4 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (upholding USCTS' finding that a petitioner did not establish 
that his judging of others' work "set [him] ... apart from others in the field" or established "a career of 
acclaimed work"). 
4 
The Petitioner argues that he has made several original contributions to his field. He cites: his 
development ofthe mobile application for Ukrainian medical students and an online library facilitating 
multilingual applications; his online, 17-lecture course for building user interfaces in mobile 
applications; his authorship of a blog on software development; and his role as one of 11 experts at a 
central Asian "technopark" for startup companies. 2 But the record lacks sufficient evidence that these 
contributions have generated acclaim and recognition sufficient to establish his claimed extraordinary 
ability in the field. See Joseph, 2025 WL 45800 l, at *4 (finding that the petitioner's evidence of 
original contributions did not demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim"). 
As proof ofthe significance of his contributions, the Petitioner points to the six recommendation letters 
on his behalf. These letters provide detailed examples of specific software projects he has developed 
within time and cost constraints and praise his combination of technical skills, leadership, and 
innovative thinking. A former manager of the Petitioner described him as "one of the most talented 
and dedicated software engineers I have ever encountered." The former manager said: "He is not just 
a software engineer; he is a visionary who understands the bigger picture and works hard to create 
solutions that make a difference." 
But, despite the high praise, the recommendation letters do not demonstrate the Petitioner's place 
among the small percentage at his field's top. The record lacks sufficient independent expert testimony 
about his skills and abilities. All of his recommendation letters come from people who personally 
know him, including current and former co-workers and managers. "[I]t is generally expected that 
one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have 
received recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the circle of their personal and 
professional acquaintances." Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at 
*3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (quoting 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3)). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his first or sole authorship of five scholarly articles in his field. 
But the record lacks evidence of the rankings of the journals that published his work. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) (stating that publication in highly ranked journals may indicate 
prestige or acclaim). Also, the evidence indicates that the Petitioner's articles generated a total of two 
citations. He has not established that this total reflects extraordinary ability in his field. See Agrawal 
v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *23 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding USCIS' 
finding that "the evidence does not demonstrate that the research reported in [the petitioner's] articles 
has made an impact on the field sufficient to provoke widespread commentary throughout the [field] 
or major press describing her or her contributions"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) 
(stating that a relatively high rate of citations may indicate a petitioner's high overall standing in their 
field). Further, the Petitioner's scholarly articles do not demonstrate sustained acclaim, as they all 
were published in 2024. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (stating that the requested immigrant 
visa category requires "sustained national or international acclaim").

contribution to his field. But the record shows that he did not begin writing the weekly briefuntil 2025, after the petition's 
December 2024 filing. As the weekly brief would not establish his eligibility for the requested benefit at the time offiling,

The record contains evidence that the Petitioner presented educational information on mobile 
development testing to employees of a large Ukrainian information technology company, which 
uploaded a video of the presentation to its website. The Petitioner claims that his presentation helped 
to "enhance the understanding and implementation of testing processes across lO distinct projects 
within the organization." But the record lacks sufficient independent evidence of the presentation's 
purported influence on the company. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("Approval or denial of 
a petition is based on the type and quality of evidence submitted.") Also, the Petitioner has not 
explained how the presentation demonstrates his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that, during his tenure with his current employer, he has improved 
user interfaces in its mobile applications, implemented a tool that detects and eliminates redundant 
code, introduced "snapshot testing" to reduce systems' testing times, and designed enhanced security 
protocols. But the record lacks sufficient evidence that the Petitioner's role with his employer has 
garnered national or international acclaim or significant recognition beyond the organization. See 
Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) 
(finding that "the record does not indicate that [the petitioner's] work to develop [his employer's] 
organization has resulted in national or international acclaim"). 
The Petitioner also provided evidence that his current base annual salary of $172,300 exceeds the 
average annual salary of U.S. software engineers ($137,350), the mean annual wage of U.S. software 
engineers ($138,110), and the average annual salary of software engineers in his state ($135,040). But 
he has not demonstrated that his salary is so high that it indicates his claimed extraordinary ability in 
his field. See Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 820 (affirming USCIS' finding "that while [a petitioner's] 
high salary demonstrated recognition of his skillset, the record did not show that his earnings were so 
high as to indicate that he is one of a small percentage at the top of his field, or that his salary was 
reflective of sustained national acclaim"). 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability. Proposed Rule for 
Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). The legislature indicated 
that this immigrant visa category was meant "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the 
very top of their field of endeavor." Id. Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner has 
established that he is a very talented software engineer. But the record falls short of demonstrating his 
receipt of sustained national or international acclaim or identifying him as one ofthat small percentage 
at his field's very top. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary ability 
classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [ a petitioner] 
was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (holding that 
even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not automatically meet the extraordinary 
ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. We will therefore 
affirm the petition's denial.
