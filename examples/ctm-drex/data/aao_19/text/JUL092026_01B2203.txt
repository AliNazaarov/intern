Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a pharmaceutical researcher who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( 1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § ll 53(b)(1 )(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS, through the Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner 
demonstrated that he met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria, he did not meet the requisite 
standard in a final merits analysis. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. The 
Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
While we may not agree with every aspect in SCOPS' thorough final merits analysis, we do agree 
with their ultimate determination and we adopt and affirm its decision with the added comments below. 
See Matter ofJ-A-, 29 I&N Dec. 253, 254 (BIA 2025) (citing Matter ofBurbano, 20 I&N Dec. 872, 
874 (BIA 1994); see also Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (noting that the practice 
of adopting and affirming the decision below "is not only common practice, but universally 
accepted"); Garland v. Ming Dai, 593 U.S. 357, 370-71 (2021) (noting an administrative appellate 
body's act of adopting and affirming the below decision is legitimate, incorporating the lower 
decision's analysis and reasoning on the issues into the appellate determination). 
In our de nova review, we conclude that the Petitioner's case did not warrant a final merits 
determination because he claimed three criteria and only met two of them. Stated differently, we do 
not agree with the portion of SCOPS' decision that the Petitioner submitted evidence of his "original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the 
field" under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in his field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 
126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it 
has some meaning. Nielsen v. Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 (2019) (finding that every word and every 
provision is to be given effect and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to 
duplicate another provision or to have no consequence). Contributions ofmajor significance connotes 
that the Petitioner's work has significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions 
must have already been realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must 
submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. 
The Petitioner's claims under this criterion were based on his publication record, that his publications 
were among the most highly cited in their respective publication year, and that other researchers have 
relied on his findings in their own work. In addition, the Petitioner claims his work in the areas of 
colorectal cancer, brain tumors, and inflammation related diseases have resulted in adequate 
contributions to meet the requirements here. When SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the 
requirements of this criterion, it essentially reiterated his claims but did not provide any analysis 
detailing how his efforts have resulted in the requisite impact in the field. 
The Petitioner claimed that because he had published work "in some of the most discerning, 
high-impact journals in his field" that this amounted to "a rare accomplishment achieved by only the 
very best researchers in the field." But the act of publishing in these respected journals does not 
automatically result in the types of contributions this criterion requires. "Evidence that the person's 
work was funded, patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will 
not necessarily establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance to the field." See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. 
Simply publishing in prestigious journals might suggest his research is valuable, but that does not 
categorically mean that his valued research has significantly contributed to the field as a whole. 
Publications alone are not sufficient under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they were 
of major significance. Here, the Petitioner did not establish that publication in a popular or highly 
ranked journal alone demonstrates a contribution ofmajor significance in the field. Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *20 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding that it cannot be 
automatically presumed that all articles published by respected journals and organizations are 
considered majorly significant); Venkatnarayan, 2024 WL 4008312, at *7, report and 
recommendation adopted, 2024 WL 4267307 (upholding the high-ranking status of a journal did not 
show an author's influence or impact of the petitioner's research on the field, nor did it establish that 
every article published in a highly ranked journal automatically indicates a contribution of major 
significance). We therefore find this argument unpersuasive. 
He also claims that because other researchers have relied on his findings in their own work, that this 
demonstrates his contributions to the field have been of major significance. USCIS policy speaks to 
that issue indicating that "published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its 
importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative 
to others' work in that field, may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the 
2 
field of endeavor." Id. Of particular importance, we highlight the policy reflects one should both 
provoke widespread commentary and be highly cited. 
As supporting evidence, the Petitioner provided a report from Clarivate Analytics reflecting baseline 
citation rates between 2013 and 2023 for several research fields with the relevant field here being 
pharmacology and toxicology. First, we dispose of his assertions on appeal as he relied on data from 
several years after the petition filing date to place his work "in the top 0.02% of authors in his area of 
expertise in terms ofresearch productivity." A petitioner must establish eligibility at the time they file 
the visa petition. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l), (12). USCIS may not approve a visa petition if the Petitioner 
was not qualified at the priority date but expects to become eligible at a subsequent time. See Matter of 
Izummi, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 175-76 (Assoc. Cornrn'r 1998); Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N Dec. 45, 49 
(Reg'l Comm'r 1971). 
Additionally, while the Petitioner may have shown that some ofhis work is well cited in the field, even 
one paper that is highly cited, what he did not demonstrate is that his work has spurred widespread 
commentary from others in the field on how it is important. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l) (noting to satisfy this criterion, it requires more than showing a paper is highly cited). 
And after reviewing the articles the Petitioner provided that cite to his work, he has not shown that 
any one of the citing authors placed unusual reliance on his work within their paper. The example 
citations in the record cite the Petitioner's paper as one of dozens, and sometime of hundreds of other 
studies and do not single out his work as significantly notable among other research in the field. In 
the rare instance that another work cited to his publication more than one time, that occurred within 
the same paragraph and did not reflect a notable reliance on his findings to support their main 
hypothesis. 
Here, the Petitioner did not offer textual and contextual evidence that his cited work was a direct 
foundation or building block for the other researchers' work. These examples of peripheral mentions 
fall far short of the in-depth discussion offered as a representative example in USCIS policy, which 
expects evidence of published research that has provoked "widespread commentary" regarding its 
importance. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l); Venkatnarayan, 2024 WL 
4008312, at*7 (upholding USCIS' finding that there was no evidence distinguishing the alien's work 
from all the other works cited in the articles to show the major significance of his work). Nor has the 
Petitioner demonstrated the number ofcitations his published articles received reflect a level ofinterest 
in his work from relevant parties to meet this criterion's mandates. Bhanu v. Garland, No. CV 22-103, 
2023 WL 6119357, at *7-8 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023). 
Next, the Petitioner asserts that because his articles have received attention from others in the field 
internationally, that this aids him in demonstrating his contributions have been of major significance. 
While this fact does establish attention from more than one country, it does little to show the 
significance of his work unless a particular area of research was somehow limited to one country or 
region of the world. As that is not the case, we acknowledge this claim but note that it does little to 
help the Petitioner here as generating citations from international researchers is not an anomaly, but 
the natural result of a connected global scientific network.

He also claimed significant contributions to the field of pharmaceutical science through exploring 
biomarkers and therapeutic targets for colorectal cancer, designing and synthesizing peptides and 
therapeutic agents for brain tumors, and designing and synthesizing novel inhibitors for imaging and 
treating inflammation related diseases. While support letters describe his research efforts and findings 
relating to colorectal cancer, it is unclear how those conclusions have resulted in changes in the field 
that are of major significance. Simply performing research on improving the prognosis and treatment 
of colorectal cancer does not rise to the type of effects in the field that this criterion anticipates. 
As it relates to developing peptides, the Petitioner relied on six of his published papers and the fact 
that they appeared in journals with high impact factors to demonstrate his contributions to the field are 
of major significance. But publishing one's research findings, even that which is well-cited, does not 
necessarily reflect that work amounts to an impactful contribution in the field. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 
580 F.3d I 030, I 036 (9th Cir. 2009), aff'd in part, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 20 I 0) (finding publications 
alone are not sufficient to demonstrate major significance). And we have already addressed the claim 
relating to publishing in prestigious journals. Without a demonstration that such publication resulted 
in an significant improvement in the broader field, it is insufficient to meet this criterion's mandates. 
Agrawal, 2025 WL 2879217, at *20; Venkatnarayan, 2024 WL 4008312, at *7. 
And finally his claims relating to the identification of biomarkers, the Petitioner identified the letter 
from.__________,as supporting his claims. I I described the Petitioner's work, noting his 
findings were a new concept that will lead to much better drugs," but this is a result that may only 
occur in the future, and it is not a contribution to the field that has already been realized. That the 
Petitioner will provide a prospective benefit to the United States is a separate requirement under the 
Act. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(iii) of the Act. 
While the evidence shows promise in the Petitioner's work, it does not establish how his work already 
qualifies as a contribution ofmajor significance in the field, rather than prospective, potential impacts. 
The significant nature of his work has yet to be determined or measured. This regulatory criterion 
requires petitioners to show they have already made a significant impact in their field. A Petitioner 
must establish the elements for the approval ofthe petition at the time offiling. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(1), 
(12). A petition may not be approved if the Petitioner was not qualified at the priority date but expects 
to become eligible at a subsequent time. See Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N Dec. at 49. This does not 
establish that, as of the priority date, the Petitioner contributed to his field in a significant manner as 
the regulation requires. 
And as a final note, the Petitioner notes that as a researcher, he "contributed to the development of 
ROF-2" used "for amyloid detection and as a screening tool for amyloid inhibitors." First, it appears 
the ROF-2 concept was validated and further developed by other researchers in the field and the 
Petitioner is not named on the evidence he presented for this experimental chemical molecule. The 
Petitioner has not explained why the commercialization of this chemical molecule should be 
considered as a contribution of major significance to the field that is attributed to him. We further 
note the research paper the Petitioner submitted with his response to SCOPS' request for evidence 
relating to ROF-2 postdates the petition filing date and we have already explained that he may not rely 
on that type of evidence in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l), (12); Matter of Katigbak, 
14 I&N Dec. at 49.

Based on this analysis we are withdrawing SCOPS' favorable determination under this criterion. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's 
burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that 
burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
