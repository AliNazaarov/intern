Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a doctoral student in the field of mathematics, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 
1153(b )( 1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not 
establish that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirement for this immigrant visa classification 
through either a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or by meeting at 
least three of the evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

2. Published Material About the Petitioner Relating to His Work in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) seeks "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought." It also requires that such evidence includes "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." The published material should be about the alien and his 
work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other major media and 
major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to other media in the 
field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner submitted copies of more than 20 articles, all mentioning his overall best score in the 
2019 national mathematics competition held by I Idiscussed above. Beyond the criterion's 
requirements for date and author name, these articles are not about him and his work in the field. 
Firstly, the bulk of the content of these articles discuss the competition itself and include extensive 
comments from its organizers, and only mention the Petitioner in identifying him as a final year 
undergraduate atl Iand including a short quote from him. While we acknowledge that the 
alien and his work need not be the sole focus of the published material, the articles here do not include 
a substantial discussion of the Petitioner and his work. Id. 
Secondly, this criterion requires published materials about an alien and their work in the field for which 
class[fication is sought. Here, the Petitioner has identified his intention to continue to pursue research 
in "analytic number theory, [which] will expand into areas such as pseudo-randomness and spectral 
statistics, with practical applications in cryptography and quantum computing." He also describes his 
published work in mathematical modelling and the analytical study of number theory. The Petitioner 
has not established that the articles' discussion of his participation in a contest for undergraduate 
mathematics students constitutes published media about his work in the field for which classification 
is sought. 
In addition, SCOPS pointed out in their decision that the text of the articles was identical or nearly 
identical in all cases, despite authorship being attributed to the ___________ in 
some cases, to individual writers in others, and in many cases to the publication or to no individual or 
organization. While we agree with the Petitioner's statement on appeal that the record does not include 
evidence showing that he paid for the publication of this material, USCIS evaluates media coverage 
based on editorial independence and objective third-party recognition and this type of replication 
across publications warrants diminished evidentiary value. This syndication creates the appearance it 
was distributed through automated networks or paid distribution services rather than independent 
editorial selection, or that it is self-promotional materials which USCIS policy does not allow. See Id. 
(noting that marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the person's products or promoting 
the person's services are not generally considered to be published material about the person which 
includes seemingly objective content about the person in major print publications that the person or 
the person's employer paid for). 
Finally, the Petitioner also submitted visitation and viewership statistics for some of the publications 
in which the articles appeared from sources including SimilarWeb and HypeStat. These statistics 
include rankings which help to put the raw visitation figures in context, and combined with other

B. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the 
field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).
