Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a global vehicle manufacturer that seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Beneficiary had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did it 
demonstrate that the Beneficiary met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now 
before us on appeal under 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate 
eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Section 291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the 
questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). 
Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Beneficiary will serve as the Petitioner's global vice president overseeing physical product 
engineering responsible for the design and execution ofboth internal combustion and electric vehicles. 
His field of endeavor is in the area of automotive engineering and technical management and he 
occupies a chief executive position within that field. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must demonstrate he satisfies at least three of the alternate 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed the 
Beneficiary met six of the regulatory criteria: prizes or awards, membership, published material, 
original contributions, leading or critical role, and high salary or remuneration. SCOPS decided that 
the Petitioner did not satisfy any of those criterion's requirements. 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that the Beneficiary meets the same evidentiary criteria it 
previously claimed except for published material. The Petitioner's published material claims have 
been abandoned on appeal. Issues not challenged on appeal are deemed to be waived. Matter ofS­
M-H-, 29 I&N Dec. 412, 414 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter of P-B-B-, 28 I&N Dec. 43, 44 n.1 (BIA 
2020)). After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude that the petition should remain 
denied. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner provided two awards that SCOPS determined fell short of meeting the above 
requirements. On appeal, the Petitioner restates much of the same claims that SCOPS found 
inadequate. 
2 
Even though the Petitioner as an organization might have a global presence, they have not 
demonstrated that the recognition of the award permeates outside the company within the field itself 
at a national or international level. National and international recognition results, not from the entity 
that issued the prize or the award, but through the awareness of the accolade in the eyes of the field 
nationally or internationally. This recognition should be evident through specific means; for example 
but not limited to, national or international-level media coverage. A national or international level 
competition may issue lesser awards that merely receive local or regional recognition, which do not 
meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. Additionally, unsuppo1ted conclusory letters 
from those in the Petitioner's field are not sufficient evidence that a particular prize or award is 
nationally or internationally recognized. Sufficiently probative evidence that an award is nationally 
or internationally recognized is generally material from sources besides the issuing entity. Krasniqi 
v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021). 
The Petitioner asserts that a single mention of the award on a website (theautochannel.com) or in a 
publication (Modem Machine Shop) that also has a presence on the internet is adequate media 
attention at a national level to demonstrate the Beneficiary's award received the requisite level offield 
acknowledgment. 
The Petitioner, however, provides no evidence to establish the popularity of the website such that 
event coverage on these sites is indicative of the national or international recognition. The record 
lacks evidence that these sources are on line versions of media with a national reach. "The presence 
of something on the internet, a medium accessible to nearly anyone with a computer or smart device, 
does not establish that it is actually read or respected." Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 185. The 
Petitioner has not presented any evidence to establish that the content from these sources can be 
considered to receive national or international recognition. The Petitioner bears the burden to establish 
eligibility, and in this instance it failed to provide any evidence regarding the reputation of the 
websites. 
National or international accessibility by itself is not a realistic indicator of a given website's 
reputation. The plain language ofthe regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) specifically requires that 
the Petitioner's awards be nationally or internationally recognized in the field ofendeavor and it is the 
Petitioner's burden to establish that the evidence meets every element ofthis criterion. In this instance, 
there is no documentary evidence demonstrating that this award is recognized beyond the presenting 
organization and these websites and is therefore commensurate with a nationally or internationally 
recognized prize or award for excellence in the field. 
Ultimately, a combination of the regulation, agency policy, and SCOPS' notices informed the 
Petitioner of the type of material necessary to demonstrate eligibility here. But it was apparently 
unable to produce the requisite material, and the end result is a failure to demonstrate eligibility under 
this requirement.

2. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that the Beneficiary is a member ofan association in his field. Second, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate both of the following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally 
recognized experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the 
achievements are outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a 
condition of eligibility for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements 
to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH­
AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at *3--4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner claimed the Beneficiary was a qualifying member of two entities before SCOPS who 
decided neither was sufficient to meet this criterion's plain language requirements. On appeal, the 
Petitioner only advances the Beneficiary's membership under the Institution ofMechanical Engineers 
and has therefore abandoned its claims relating to the Engineering Council. Matter ofS-M-H-, 29 I&N 
Dec. at 414. 
On appeal, the Petitioner alleges SCOPS mistook the terminology of being an elected member of the 
Institution of Mechanical Engineers as an indication that this was not a qualifying membership in an 
association. Even ifwe presume the Beneficiary was a member ofthis association, the Petitioner must 
show the remaining requirements have been satisfied. It appears from the evidence the Petitioner 
provided that the Institution of Mechanical Engineers is an entity with varying levels ofmembership, 
each with their own requirements: (1) associate, (2) affiliate, (3) professional comprising three 
categories of chartered engineer, incorporated engineer, and engineering technician, (4) fellow, and 
(5) the highly prestigious honorary fellow. 
Agency policy discusses differing levels of membership within an association and provides: 
Associations may have multiple levels of membership. The level of membership 
afforded to the person must show that in order to obtain that level of membership, 
recognized national or international experts judged the person as having attained 
outstanding achievements in the field for which classification is sought. 
As a possible example, general membership in an international organization for 
engineering and technology professionals may not meet the requirements of the 
criterion. However, if that same organization at the fellow level requires, in part, that 
a nominee have accomplishments that have, for example, contributed importantly to 
the advancement or application of engineering, science, and technology, and that a 
council of experts and a committee of current fellows judges the nominations for 
fellows, that higher, fellow level may be qualifying. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manua1. Different 
membership levels that have distinct requirements to become a member at that level must meet all of 
4 
this criterion's requirements. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at 
*3-4. 
First, the Petitioner provided a certificate only reflecting the Beneficiary's membership in this 
association was as a chartered mechanical engineer appearing to place him within the professional 
level of membership. For the broader professional level, the association's website provides: 
"Professional registration is the definitive measure of a successful career in engineering, and is global 
recognition of your knowledge and experience." Under the chartered engineer categ01y, the website 
provides: "Chartered Engineers develop solutions to complex engineering problems using new or 
existing technologies, and through innovation, creativity and technical analysis. Their academic 
knowledge is at master's level or equivalent." It does not appear that this organization requires 
outstanding achievements for the level of membership the Beneficiary possesses. 
Compare those requirements to that of a fellow: "Fellowship is a mark of exceptional commitment, 
knowledge and experience in mechanical engineering, and is the senior class of membership for 
professionally registered engineers. It also recognises engineers' contributions to the profession and 
the Institution." Although the provided evidence for the fellow level ofmembership lacks sufficiently 
detailed information to show that it qualifies for this criterion's plain language requirements, its 
requirements are closer to the type ofmembership this criterion requires than the level the Beneficiary 
has attained. And even though the appeal brief mentions that the Beneficiary's membership meets 
standards of other governing entities, it remains that the Petitioner has not shown that the evidence 
meets all of this criterion's requirements. 
3. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salaiy or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner to submit 
evidence of a "high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others 
in the field." The Petitioner must present evidence of objective earnings data showing that the 
Beneficiary has earned a "high salary" or "significantly high remuneration" in comparison with those 
performing similar work during the same time period. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 955 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); 
see also Skokos v. DHS, 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for 
those performing lesser duties is not a proper comparison to others in the field); Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 
8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at *6 (upholding USCIS determination that a 
petitioner's comparative evidence must relate to their specific field and not a general characterization 
of an overly broad field); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) ( considering NHL 
enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N.D. Ill. 1995) 
(comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). USCIS policy 
instructs its officers to pay particular attention to a petitioner's field to make an appropriate comparison 
when it states: 
Broad descriptions that include multiple occupations or multiple industries may not 
provide an accurate comparison to others in the field. For example, "directors and 
producers" might be listed as a single category across industries, but evidence that a 
film director receives high remuneration based on a broad range of occupational data 
5 
that include disparate occupations such as film director and radio show producer, may 
not be sufficiently probative. 
Average or median salary information for those performing work in a related but distinct occupation 
with different responsibilities is not a proper basis for comparison. Kapoor v. USCIS, 763 F. Supp. 
3d 247, 258 (E.D.N.Y. 2025) (stating USCIS reasonably concluded that a petitioner's evidence 
comparing average and median salaries was insufficient to establish they commanded a high salary in 
relation to others in the field); see also Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 9 (D. Mass. 2023); 
Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 853 (C.D. Cal. 2022). 
Accompanying the initial filing, the Petitioner provided material it issued to the Beneficiary dated 
March 10, 2025, reflecting his annual base salary and other forms of remuneration, the U.S. 
Department of Labor's Occupational Outlook Handbook profile for Top Executives, and the 
Occupational Information Network wages for Chief Executives. SCOPS found that material 
inadequate to support its claims under this criterion and it issued a request for evidence (RFE). In 
response, the Petitioner submitted evidence of the Beneficiary's pay for previous years with itself as 
well as another vehicle manufacturer. This reflected both the Beneficiary's salary as well as his other 
forms ofremuneration that he has commanded. In the RFE response the Petitioner also offered salary 
surveys from ZipRecruiter, and Salary.com, and other sources that the Petitioner has abandoned on 
appeal as it does not argue those topics. Matter ofS-M-H-, 29 I&N Dec. at 414. SCOPS determined 
that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements ofthis criterion. 
Because the Beneficiary's field-and in particular his position-play such an important role in the 
analysis under this criterion, we reiterate that he occupies the vice president position in the field of 
automotive engineering and technical management. We note that because compensation and 
remuneration can widely vary based on the type ofwork a chief executive oversees, we do not consider 
a proper comparison to simply be other chief executives in the automotive industry because the type 
ofwork the Beneficiary manages is highly technical relating to engineering and crash safety. So any 
comparative data should demonstrate his salary is high when compared to other chief executive's in 
the automotive engineering and technical management field. 
The Petitioner has demonstrated the Beneficiary's salary and other forms of remuneration. What's 
left is to evaluate whether the evidence it provided in which to compare to the Beneficiary's 
compensation meets the standards under this criterion. We conclude that it does not. 
We agree with SCOPS that the initially submitted material was not adequate as it was not related to 
the Beneficiary's particular field, and average or median salaries are not a valid basis for comparison 
to establish the Beneficiary's compensation is high. Kapoor, 763 F. Supp. 3d at 258. For the evidence 
in the RFE response, the Petitioner begins its appellate arguments identifying the material from 
ZipRecruiter and Salary.com stating they are: 
[I]n direct alignment with the proffered role of [the position in the petition], an 
executive-level position that is critical in ensuring the efficient, high-quality, and 
cost-effective engineering of Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) and electric vehicles 
(EVs). This role is directly responsible for aligning the company's global hardware 
engineering with the overall company vision, strategy, and business objectives. 
6 
The ZipRecruiter material listed the position as vice president automotive with a nationwide average 
salary of $157,532. First, there's no indication within the record that this website's "vice president 
automotive" occupation has responsibilities sufficiently similar to the Beneficiary's position with the 
Petitioner. See, e.g., Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 955. Second, this material does not sufficiently 
support the Beneficiary's eligibility as it only presents average salary statistics and that is not an apt 
comparison to his salary. See, e.g., Kapoor, 763 F. Supp. 3d at 258. 
And finally, nationwide statistics are not a proper comparison as the Beneficiary will work in a 
particular location in the United States. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1) 
(reflecting that comparative wage or remuneration data should not only be in their field, but also in 
the geographic area); see also Berardo v. USCIS, No. 3:19-CV-01796-SB, 2020 WL 6161459, at *6 
(D. Or. Oct. 20, 2020) ( citing to agency policy to conclude that "geographic differences are relevant 
to a determination ofwhether a salary is high in relation to others in the field"). A position's salary in 
different locations in the United States could vary widely, which is the reason a petitioner's 
comparative salary or remuneration evidence should be in the same geographic area. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F .2(8)(1 ). As a result, the evidence from Zip Recruiter does not aid 
the Petitioner in demonstrating the Beneficiary's eligibility. 
And the Salary.com printout suffers from a similar shortcoming because the salary data it contains 
relates to Hampstead, New Hampshire and not the area where the Beneficiary will work. SCOPS cited 
the USCIS Policy Manual relating to the importance that comparative evidence be in a similar 
geographic region in both the RFE and the denial decision, but the Petitioner did not address that issue. 
Here, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets this criterion's requirements. 
4. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392, 415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision in a statute is to be 
given effect and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another 
provision or to have no consequence). Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. Contributions of major significance 
connotes that the Petitioner's work has significantly impacted the field. The Petitioner must submit 
evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided evidence of two vehicle safety patents associated with the Beneficiary that 
have been adopted by several vehicle manufacturers. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not 
meet the requirements of this criterion. We do not agree with, and we withdraw, SCOPS' adverse 
determination under this criterion.

B. Remaining Claims and Additional Notes 
We conclude that although the Petitioner's evidence satisfies the original contributions criterion, it 
does not meet the criteria regarding prizes or awards, membership, or a high salary. While it argues 
and submits evidence for one additional criterion on appeal relating to the Beneficiary performing in 
a leading or critical role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), it is unnecessary that we make a decision on 
this additional ground because it cannot numerically meet the required number of criteria. As the 
Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the remaining issue. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328,332 (2022) (citing INS 
v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); see also M-C-C-, 29 I&N 
Dec. 401, 405-06 (BIA 2026) ( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is 
otherwise ineligible). 
We also note the Petitioner filed a separate petition for the Beneficiaty under this same immigrant 
classification that USCIS approved. We did not review that record of proceeding to evaluate that 
decision's propriety, but we note that the agency adjudicates each case based on the material within 
the record. The regulation repeatedly reflects that an eligibility determination on a benefit request will 
be based on information contained in the record of proceeding. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(10), (11), (14), 
(16)(i}-(ii). Each case must be decided on its own facts with regard to the sufficiency of the evidence 
presented. See, e.g., Matter of H-C-R-C-, 28 I&N Dec. 809, 812 (BIA 2024). The record before us 
reflects that the proper decision here was a denial of this immigration benefit. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification on the Beneficiary's behalf, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that 
goal. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically 
meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. at 954-55. Here, the 
Petitioner has not shown the significance of the Beneficiary's work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career ofacclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A). Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Beneficiary has garnered 
national or international acclaim in the field, and he is one of the small percentage who has risen to 
the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an alien ofextraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
