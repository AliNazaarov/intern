Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an economist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner met at least three 
of the ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification and that he seeks to enter the United States 
to continue work in his area of extraordinary ability. SCOPS further determined that the Petitioner 
had willfully misrepresented a material fact by submitting an altered document. We dismissed the 
Petitioner's appeal but subsequently reopened the matter on service motion under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .5(a)(5). 1 Accordingly, the reopened matter is again before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3(a)(3). 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability, a petitioner must establish that they: have 
extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; seek to enter the United 
States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will prospectively 
substantially benefit the United States. Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of the 
Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim as well as extensive documentation that their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. See section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act. 
1 Following the issuance of our decision, we issued a notice of service motion pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(5)(ii) and 
provided the Petitioner the opportunity to provide additional evidence in support of the petition. His response has been 
incorporated into the record. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). A petitioner can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements 
for this immigrant visa classification through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). If such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively 
provide evidence that meets at least three of the ten listed criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published material about them in 
the media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types of evidence. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

Section 212(a)(6)(C) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § l 182(a)(6)(C), provides: 
Misrepresentation - (i) In general - Any alien who, by fraud or willfully misrepresenting 
a material fact, seeks to procure ( or has sought to procure or has procured) a visa, other 
documentation, or admission into the United States or other benefit provided under this 
Act is inadmissible. 
To establish willful misrepresentation, an officer must find: 1) the alien procured or sought to procure 
an immigration benefit through 2) the presentation of a false oral or written statement to an official of 
the United States Government 3) that was willfully made and 4) material to the alien's eligibility for 
the requested immigration benefit. See section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) ofthe Act, 8 U.S.C. § l 182(a)(6)(C)(i); 
see also Matter ofTexperts, Inc., 29 I&N Dec. 491,496 (AAO 2026) (collecting cases). 
A misrepresentation may be found to be willful if it is "deliberately made with knowledge of [its] 
falsity." Matter ofMensah, 28 I&N Dec. 288,293 (BIA 2021) (citing cases). For example, a signature 
on an official immigration form, by itself: establishes a strong, rebuttable presumption that the signor 
knows of and has assented to the false contents of a benefit request. Matter ofA.J Valdez and Z. 
Valdez, 27 I&N Dec. 496, 498-99 (BIA 2018). 
Further, information is "material" when it has a "natural tendency to affect the official decision" of an 
adjudicator or "tends to shut off a line of inquiry ... that would predictably have disclosed other 
[relevant] facts." Matter ofMensah, 28 I&N Dec. at 293-294 (quoting Kungys v. United States, 485 
U.S. 759, 771 (1988) and Matter ofD-R-, 27 I&N Dec. 105, 113 (BIA 2017) (cleaned up)). 
Here, the record shows that the Petitioner submitted a title page and excerpt from the ~I--~ 
publication that was not a true and correct version of that document in support of his immigrant visa 
petition, which amounts to making a false representation to a U.S. government official. 
Second, the Petitioner willfully made the false representations. See Matter ofMensah, 28 I&N Dec. at 
293-94 (Misrepresentations are willful if they are "deliberately made with knowledge of their falsity.")

2. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has established that he meets the evidentiary criteria relating to membership, published 
materials, and judging at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), and (iv). As the Petitioner submitted the 
requisite initial evidence, we will evaluate whether he has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the 
evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, that he is among the small percentage at the 
very top of his field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioners' accomplishments 
and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that 
they have extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.

evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 
at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989). We also note that it is the 
Petitioner's burden to submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates his claims. Statements made 
without supporting documentation are of limited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the 
Petitioner's burden of proof. Matter ofSoffici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998).

On appeal, the Petitioner's claim that his achievements and contributions have been recognized in his 
field at a level that establishes his extraordinary ability is based, in significant part, on reference letters. 
He emphasizes that these letters from experts in the field refer to him as "Georgia's most brilliant mind 
in economics," "the most distinguished economist in Georgia," "Georgia's top research economist," 
and "Georgia's most exceptional and influential economic researcher." We acknowledge that the 
reference letters in the record are universal in their praise for the Petitioner. 8 However, letters of 
support, although not without weight, cannot form the cornerstone of a successful claim for this 
classification and instead should be corroborated by documentary evidence. Further, letters that make 
general and expansive statements regarding the Petitioner and his accomplishments are generally not 
persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(3). 
For example, the letter from Z-T- states that the Petitioner is "Georgia's top research economist," and 
bases his conclusion on the fact that the Petitioner has "held critical positions in the public sector and 
private sector," made "unparallelled contributions," and has "has been equally successful in academia, 
authorizing [sic] a few dozen articles, studies and publications that have reshaped the landscape of 
Georgian economics." A letter from Z-J-, a Georgian political party leader and former member ofthe
I Ihighlights that the Petitioner has made a "consequential mark" on both the public 
and private sectors, worked with "countless international organizations" and established himself as 
"the most important expert" on a variety of topics in economics. However, as discussed above the 
record provides no definitive employment history for the Petitioner detailing his roles in the public 
and private sector and thus does not sufficiently document the Petitioner's "critical positions" and 
"consequential mark" he is claimed to have left. Further, the record does not corroborate his work 
with "countless international organizations" or his authorship ofdozens of academic articles, and does 
not show how he has been recognized for "unparalleled contributions" that have had a far-reaching 
influence or impact on his field. 
The letters in the record are not sufficient to establish that the Petitioner's work in his field has resulted 
in sustained national or international acclaim or extends beyond the circle of colleagues and contacts 
he encountered during his career. The letters, which discuss claimed achievements and work that are 
not adequately corroborated by other evidence in the record, do not demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
attracted a level of recognition placing him at the very top of his field. In addition, the letters do not 
contain sufficient information and explanation to show that he is viewed by the overall field, rather 
than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that he has consistently garnered recognition on 
a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor. Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's talents, they are not sufficiently 
corroborated by evidence reflecting a career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required 
sustained national or international acclaim. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 
WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding the plaintiff submitted letters only from people 
within his circle of professional acquaintances, and none of the letters point to evidence of extensive 
documentation of his accomplishments to show sustained national or international acclaim).

3. Continued Work in the Area of Extraordinary Ability 
Because the Petitioner is ineligible for the requested classification, we need not reach, and therefore 
reserve, discussion of whether he established that he seeks to enter the United States to continue work 
in the area of extraordinary ability, as required by section 203(b)(l)(A)(ii) of the Act. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability and the appeal is dismissed on this ground. Further, by submitting documents 
in support of the petition that were not true and accurate, the Petitioner has sought to procure an 
immigration benefit through willful misrepresentation of material facts. This finding of willful 
material misrepresentation shall be considered in any future proceeding where the Petitioner's 
admissibility is an issue. See section 212(a)(6)(C) of the Act.
