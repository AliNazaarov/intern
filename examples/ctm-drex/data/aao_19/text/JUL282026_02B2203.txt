Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a restauranteur, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner satisfied at 
least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 
203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in 
ce1iain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claims to have established six of these criteria: 
• (i), lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field; 
• (iii), published material about the individual in professional or major media; 
• (iv), participation as a judge of the work of others; 
• ( vii), display of work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases;

USCIS determined that the Petitioner established criterion (iii), relating to published material about

1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized A wards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." We 
first determine whether the individual was the recipient of prizes or awards, and then whether the 
award is nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence in the field 
of endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims to meet this criterion based upon an award conferred by I I 
alderman ofth~ Ion thel ICity Council. As evidence he rovided a plaque that states 
it is awarded to the Petitioner by Alderman! lof th City Council. The plaque 
states that it is "[ c ]elebrating the impact of Hispanic heritage food on._____, with the I I 
standing as one of the best sandwiches ever created by Hispanic hands." 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that an award conferred by the I ICity Council should be 
considered nationally or internationally recognized because I lis a major U.S. city and the City 
Council is the legislative body governing "one of the largest and most internationally known cities in 
the United States." The Petitioner asserts that recognition from the City Council is therefore 
"nationally and internationally relevant." 
A government agency's issuance of an award, however, does not alone establish a prize's national 
recognition. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) ("[A]s reflected in the 
regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not the awarding organization.") (emphasis in 
original). The Petitioner must demonstrate the level of recognition of the award, not of its 
issuer. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 136 ("Without evidence of how a larger audience 
viewed Plaintiff's awards, there was no way for the agency to evaluate whether those awards were 
recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion."). The Petitioner submitted evidence regarding the 
reputation ofl I. He also provided evidence of1 Ias a "culinary destination" 
and "food city." He did not, however, provide evidence of the reputation of this award itself: and he 
has not established that a plaque from an alderman of the City Council ofl lis necessarily a 
nationally or internationally recognized award. Without evidence as to whether the award is nationally 
or internationally recognized, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets this criterion. 
The record therefore does not establish that the Petitioner meets this criterion. 
2. Display at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to demonstrating that the displayed work 
was the person's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the work was displayed must 
be artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. While Merriam­
Webster's online dictionary definition of "exhibition" includes public showings other than those that 
are artistic in nature, the plain language of the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and explicitly 
requires that the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in nature. See id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he meets this criterion based upon the restaurant's 
participation in the 20241 !Festival and the 2025 I IFestival. As evidence of 
these events, the record contains a poster for the 202~ IFestival and a website article about 
thq !Festival. As USCIS stated in its decision, the record does not contain photographic 
evidence of the display of the Petitioner's work at these events. The Petitioner asserts that the 
preponderance of the evidence demonstrates, based on the Petitioner's restaurant being listed as a 
vendor, that it is more likely than not that the Petitioner's work was displayed at these events. 
We agree with USCIS that the record is not sufficient to demonstrate that the Petitioner's work was 
displayed at these events. The website information and festival poster appear to show that the 
restaurant was a participating vendor selling food at these events. The Petitioner has not established 
that a food vendor selling food at a festival constitutes the "display of work." Additionally, regarding 
the I IFestival, the festival poster provided is not sufficient evidence to demonstrate that 
this event was an artistic exhibition or showcase, rather than a promotional or business event. In this 
context, the word "artistic" ordinarily means "relating to or characteristic of art or artists." See id. 
Non-artistic exhibitions or showcases do not satisfy the criterion's requirements. See id. The record 
does not establish that the Petitioner displayed his work in the field at an artistic exhibition or showcase 
based upon the restaurant's participation in these events. 
The Petitioner has not established this criterion. 
3. Leading or Critical Role for Distinguished Organizations or Establishments 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations 
or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the person was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the person has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof, has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra.

4. High Salary or Other Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that the individual "has commanded" such 
salary or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay 
statements. Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the individual's occupation 
and location should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working 
in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra. 
USCIS found that the Petitioner did not provide sufficient documentation of his income and did not 
demonstrate that the provided comparison data was appropriate. On appeal, the Petitioner contends 
that the 2024 corporate tax filing with schedule K-1 and letter from his accountant fall within the type 
of evidence listed by the Policy Manual as relevant to demonstrating income. The Petitioner farther 
asserts that the absence of evidence of W-2 wages is not a deficiency but is indicative of his income 
as a shareholder. 
We agree with USCIS that the evidence does not sufficiently demonstrate the Petitioner's income. 
The Petitioner submitted a letter from his accountant that claims that he is projected to earn $56,000 
in earnings and that his "earnings along with his profits for 2025 are projected to be at or over 
$181,000.00." The accountant farther states that "[c]urrent projections have gross sales at 4.1 million 
based on current revenue and projections at a rate of 9.5% in growth." 
The record does not contain sufficient documentation to demonstrate these claims. Although the 
Petitioner does claim to earn at least some of his income through wages, he did not submit a copy of 
his W-2 or his personal income tax return. In addition to the letter from the accountant, the Petitioner 
provided a report of employee earnings statements fo~ lo~ I Inc., which shows that the 
Petitioner had earned $39,692.28 in earnings year-to-date as lof August 8f 2025. The Petitioner also 
provided the 2024 corporate tax filing with Schedule K-1 for on IInc. The tax filing 
shows that in 2024 the corporation had $3.8 million in sales and $145,600 in ordinary business income. 
Additionally, the Schedule K-1 shows that the Petitioner is a 50% shareholder of the corporation.

B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
We further reserve our opinion as to whether the Petitioner has established that his entry will 
substantially prospectively benefit the United States. See id.
