Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b )(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish his receipt of a major 
internationally recognized award, or, in the alternative, demonstrate that he meets at least three of the 
ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant 
to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability under section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act, 
a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would offer substantial benefits to the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If a petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he 
or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). If these standards do not readily apply to a petitioner's occupation, 
they may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner received a bachelor's degree in industrial engineering from a Venezuelan university 
in 
2009. His curriculum vitae indicates that since receiving his degree, he has worked as a procurement

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner has neither claimed nor documented that he has received a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must establish that he meets at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed eligibility under eight of the ten 
criteria, including: 
• Memberships in associations in his field that require outstanding achievements; 
• Published material about him and his work; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in his field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in his field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles; 
• Performance in leading or critical roles for organizations that have a distinguished reputation; 
• High salary or other significantly high remuneration for services in relation to others; and 
• Commercial successes in the performing arts. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)-(vi) and (viii)-(x). 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not demonstrate that he satisfied any of the eight claimed 
criteria. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that USCIS did not address the evidence he submitted with 
specificity or adequately explain why the evidence was insufficient to support his claim that he 
qualifies as an alien of extraordinary ability. He requests that the matter be remanded to USCIS for 
issuance of a new decision. 
2 
As a preliminary matter, we will address the Petitioner's contention that USCIS failed to properly 
review and analyze his evidence and applied a standard of proof that exceeds the preponderance of the 
evidence standard. Under the preponderance standard, we consider not only the quantity, but also the 
quality of the evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter ofChawathe, 
25 I&N Dec. at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989). 
When USCIS provides a reasoned consideration of a petition and has made adequate findings, it will 
not be required to specifically address each claim the petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it to 
address every piece of evidence the petitioner presents. Guaman-Loja v. Holder, 707 F.3d 119, 123 
(1st Cir. 2013) ( citing Martinez v. INS, 970 F.2d 973, 976 (1st Cir. 1992); see also Kazemzadeh v. US. 
Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 (11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 
1993). 
To satisfy the initial evidentiary requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), the Petitioner's evidence 
must objectively meet the parameters ofthe applicable regulatory descriptions. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B). Here, although USCIS did not address every piece of evidence the 
Petitioner presented under these criteria, the record reflects that the decision acknowledged and 
analyzed various documents on record, provided a reasoned consideration of the criteria under the 
preponderance of the evidence standard, and has made adequate findings that comply with 8 C.F.R. 
103.2(a)(l)(i) (requiring USCIS to explain in writing the specific reasons for denial). 
Further, for the reasons discussed below, the record supports USCIS' determination that the Petitioner 
has not demonstrated, as required, that he satisfies at least three of the ten initial evidentiary 
requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
1. Memberships 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility under this criterion based on his membership in the College of 
Engineers of Venezuela. He submitted: (1) a copy of his expired membership card; (2) Venezuela's 
"Law on the Practice of Architecture and Engineering Related Professions"; and (3) excerpts from the 
College of Engineers' web site with details regarding its history and code of professional ethics. 
USCIS concluded that the evidence demonstrated the Petitioner's membership in the College of 
Engineers of Venezuela but did not identify the association's requirements for membership or discuss 
how membership applications are judged. Therefore, USCIS concluded that the evidence did not 
demonstrate that the association requires prospective members to have "outstanding achievements" as 
a condition for membership and that those achievements are 'judged by recognized national or 
international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
3 
On appeal, the Petitioner emphasizes that the College of Engineers of Venezuela is a professional 
association, noting that, according to the USCIS Policy Manual, qualifying memberships may include 
"membership in certain professional associations." He asserts that "[t]he admission process involves 
completing an application and demonstrating educational and professional qualifications," and 
contends that "there is no doubt" that he demonstrated eligibility under this criterion. 
The referenced section of the USCIS Policy Manual states that membership in "certain professional 
associations" may satisfy the requirements of the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) and provides 
an illustrative example of circumstances under which such a membership may be qualifying. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The policy guidance does not state that a 
petitioner will automatically meet this criterion based on evidence of membership in a professional 
association. In fact, USCIS policy specifically states that a membership in an association that admits 
members solely based on possessing a certain level of education or years of experience in a particular 
field will generally not qualify as a membership that is based on outstanding achievements in the field. 
Id. Therefore, the Petitioner's claim that members in the College of Engineers of Venezuela are 
required to demonstrate their "educational and professional qualifications" is not sufficient to 
demonstrate that his membership satisfies the requirements of this criterion. 
As noted in USCIS' decision, the record lacks evidence documenting the requirements for membership 
and therefore does not demonstrate that the College of Engineers of Venezuela only admits members 
who have outstanding achievements as judged by recognized national or international experts in the 
field. Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets the criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
2. Published Material 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. 
The Petitioner claimed eli ibilit under this criterion based on an article he co-authored entitled 'D 
.______________________________," published in the journal 
Centro de Investigacion de Ciencias Administrativas y Generciales (CICAG) in 2012. The Petitioner 
states that the article "encourages the scientific community to work on the development of software 
that is necessary to increase regional and national competitiveness." 
To satisfy this criterion, the published material should be about the petitioner and relate to their work 
in the field. The published material may cover a broader topic if it also includes a substantial 
discussion of the petitioner's work and mentions the person in connection to the work. We may also 
consider published material that focuses on work or research being undertaken by a team of which the 
petitioner was a member, providing that the material mentions them in connection with the work or 
other evidence documents their significant role in the work or research. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

3. Participation as a Judge 
The regulation at 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) calls for "evidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification 
for which classification is sought." The alien must show that they not only received invitations to 
participate as a judge of the work of others in their field, but also that they have actually participated in 
such judging. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual, supra at F .2(B )( 1 ). Examples of judging may 
include peer review for a scholarly journal, conference, or government research funding program or 
serving as a member of a Ph.D. dissertation committee. Id. 
users concluded that the Petitioner did not submit evidence satisfying the plain language of this 
criterion, because he relied solely on statements made by counsel. In a cover letter submitted in 
response to a request for evidence (RFE), counsel asserted that the Petitioner has held "supervisory 
roles," has been "entrusted with the responsibility of assessing and evaluating the work ofothers, both 
individually and as part ofa panel," and has evaluated "the performance, skills, and outcomes ofteams, 
technicians and subcontractors." users observed that counsel's unsubstantiated assertions did not 
constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("statements in a brief, 
motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary weight"). 
We note counsel's RFE response letter referred to "evidence submitted in the initial package" that 
relates to this criterion. We have reviewed the initial evidence and observe that the Petitioner did not 
claim eligibility under this criterion at the time of filing or otherwise state he was providing evidence 
of his participation as a judge ofthe work ofothers. Both the initial submission and the RFE response 
included recommendation letters from prior employers, but the content of those letters did not 
corroborate counsel's assertions that the Petitioner's roles have included responsibility for assessing 
or evaluating the work of others in his field. 
Further, on appeal, the Petitioner does not address users' determination that the record lacks 
independent evidence demonstrating his participation as a judge of the work of others; rather the 
appellate brief repeats the same statements submitted in response to the RFE. The Petitioner's claimed 
judging activities have not been sufficiently documented in the record. Accordingly, we conclude that 
he has not satisfied this criterion.

4. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." In academia, a 
scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse, is generally 
peer reviewed, and may be published in professional or scientific journals or conference 
presentations. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
To determine whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication or other 
major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership relative to other media 
in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id. 
As noted above, the Petitioner claimed eli ibilit under this criterion based on his co-authored article 
entitled',_______________________________," published 
in CICAG in 2012. We conclude that the Petitioner has demonstrated both his co-authorship of this 
peer-reviewed article and the scholarly nature of the published work. Further, he met his burden to 
show that the article appeared in a professional publication based on evidence describing CICAG's 
intended audience. Accordingly, the Petitioner has demonstrated that he satisfies the criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
5. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is important to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities 
or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In determining whether the 
organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof: has a distinguished reputation, 
relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and relevant media 
coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
In its decision USCIS considered recommendation letters from six current and former employees of

lacked corroborating evidence demonstrating that he held a leadership position or that he contributed 
in a way that was important to the outcome or standing of the organization or its activities. Further, 
USCIS emphasized that the Petitioner did not submit evidence demonstrating that his former employer 
has a distinguished reputation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner does not directly address USCIS' stated reasons for denial. He summarizes 
his complete employment history dating back to 2008 and claims that his "array of position[s] and the 
responsibilities associated with him, coupled with the implicit reputation of the organizations 
involved, meets the criteria outlined."

It is the Petitioner's burden to demonstrate that he has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization or establishment that has a distinguished reputation. As noted, USCIS evaluates the 
reputation of an organization or establishment based on factors that include, but are not limited to, its 
relative size or longevity, relevant media coverage, evidence of the size of its customer base, industry 
rankings, and other relevant evidence. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner has not submitted this type of evidence and instead seeks to rely on an unsupported 
statement regarding his former employers' "implicit reputation." He has therefore not met his burden 
to demonstrate that any of his prior employers enjoyed a distinguished reputation. Absent such a 
showing, he cannot demonstrate that he meets this criterion; therefore, we need not reach the issue of 
whether he has performed in a leading or critical role forl I 
or any other organization. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets the criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii).

7. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) calls for "evidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 
The evidence must show that the volume of sales or box office receipts reflects the alien's commercial 
success in the performing arts relative to others involved in similar pursuits. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
In response to USCIS' request for evidence, the Petitioner stated that this criterion is not readily 
applicable to his occupation and indicated he was seeking to establish his eligibility based on 
comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). He emphasized that he has a bachelor's degree in 
engineering, completed "several complementary studies within the field," and has worked with 
"several employers in the field." 
USCIS determined that the Petitioner did not provide evidence of any experience in the performing 
arts or demonstrate that he meets this criterion based on comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)( 4). On appeal, the Petitioner repeats his assertion that he satisfies this criterion through 
comparable evidence based on his qualifications and work experience as an engineer. 
Ifa petitioner provides comparable evidence to establish eligibility under one ofthe criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), USCIS evaluates whether the evidence is comparable to the evidence required 
under that specific criterion. Here, the Petitioner has not met his burden to demonstrate that his 
8 
qualifications and work experience in the engineering field should be considered comparable to the 
evidence required by the "commercial successes in the performing arts" criterion. 
Under this criterion, the mere fact that a person has recorded and released musical compilations or 
performed in theatrical, motion picture or television productions would be insufficient in and of itself 
to meet this criterion because it focuses on the volume of sales and box office receipts as a measure of 
the person's commercial success. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
Similarly, while the Petitioner provided evidence that he possesses academic qualifications as an 
industrial engineer and has been steadily employed in a field related to his degree, he has not shown 
that merely working in the field is sufficient to show his success relative to others involved in similar 
pursuits. He has therefore not demonstrated that he meets this criterion based on the submission of 
comparable evidence. 
C. Summary and Reserved Criterion 
Of the seven evidentiary criteria discussed above, the Petitioner has demonstrated that he meets only 
one, relating to his authorship of scholarly articles at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). As he claims to meet 
only one additional criterion, he cannot demonstrate that he meets three criteria and satisfies the initial 
evidence requirement for the extraordinary ability classification. We therefore need not reach and 
hereby reserve consideration ofhis claim that he has made original contributions ofmajor significance 
in his field. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25 (holding that agencies need not make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he qualifies as an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (stating that 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) establishes a 
necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa). Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate and conclude it does 
not support a finding that the Petitioner has established the sustained acclaim and recognition required 
for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCIS has long held that 
even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary 
ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or 
international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(1 )(A) 
of the Act. The record does not sufficiently demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or 
9 
international acclaim in the field and that he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. Accordingly, the appeal will be dismissed.
