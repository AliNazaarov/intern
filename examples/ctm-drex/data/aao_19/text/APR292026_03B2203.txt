Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an art history professor and researcher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish, 
as required, either a one-time achievement or that she met at least three of the ten initial evidentiary 
criteria for this immigrant classification. SCOPS determined the Petitioner only met two of the ten 
initial criteria. The Petitioner later filed an appeal. On appeal, we determined the Petitioner met one 
additional criterion, and as a result, that she met at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria. 
However, we concluded following a final merits determination that she did not establish sustained 
national or international acclaim or that she is among the small percentage at the top of her field. The 
matter is now before us on a combined motion to reopen and reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motions. 
I. MOTION TO REOPEN 
A motion to reopen must state new facts and be supported by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(2). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the 
latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy 
these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. See Matter of Coelho, 20 
I&N Dec. 464, 473 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence have the potential to change the 
outcome). 
As discussed, we dismissed the prior appeal concluding that, following a final merits determination, 
the Petitioner did not sufficiently establish that she had sustained national or international acclaim, 1 is 
among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and was recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. 2 In making this determination, we reasoned that the limited press 
coverage the Petitioner received between November 2023 and August 2024 was not consistent with 
sustained national or international acclaim, nor had she shown how her media coverage was indicative 
of a level of success consistent with being among that small percentage who has risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Although 
we acknowledged the Petitioner's participation in peer review, we stated that this was not reflective 
of an individual with an "elite reputation" as claimed in the field consistent with sustained national or 
international acclaim, but instead may reflect expertise in the field, a lower threshold than being at the 
very top of her field. We also noted that the Petitioner did not provide sufficient supporting evidence 
to establish that the journals for which she provided peer review were top ranked. We concluded that 
although the record showed that the Petitioner occasionally evaluated others' work since earning her 
Ph.D., it did not demonstrate how performance of peer review activities and trainee candidate 
interviews elevated her above most others in the field. 
In addition, we indicated the Petitioner did not provide sufficient support to establish that her publication 
in certain journals was an accomplishment only achieved by those at the top ofthe field. We stated that 
the evidence reflected that the Petitioner had contributed to the pool of knowledge in her field, but that 
this alone was not reflective of sustained national or international acclaim. We noted that the Petitioner 
did not show that citations to her published research demonstrated recognition at a level consistent with 
being among the small percentage at the very top ofher field. Furthermore, we addressed support letters 
submitted from the Petitioner's colleagues within the field, indicating that these letters summarized 
aspects of her work and praised her skills and contributions as a scholar, but they did not address whether 
her specific achievements were widely recognized in her field and otherwise did not demonstrate that 
she has garnered the sustained national or international acclaim required for this classification.

In sum, the supplemental letters from colleagues provided on motion are very similar to those already 
submitted and they provide little additional probative value in demonstrating that the Beneficiary has 
received sustained acclaim and risen to very top ofher field. As we discussed in our previous decision, 
the supplementary letters attest to the Petitioner's skills and accomplishments, but they do not establish 
her extraordinary ability when considered in the totality of the evidence. The letters do not claim, nor 
do their contents indicate, that the Petitioner has achieved national or international acclaim from the 
field or attracted a level of attention placing her at the very top of her field. See Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 
23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding testimonial letters were insufficient to establish 
his relative status in the field and do not compare him to others). As noted, the letters state that the 
Petitioner's work is still emerging and that it could reshape the field, but not that it has already had 
this impact. The Petitioner's colleagues also did not provide sufficient information and explanation 
to demonstrate how the Petitioner is viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few. Mishra 
v. Richardson, No. l:20-cv-991 (LMB/TCB), 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) 
(finding that none of the plaintiff's letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his 
accomplishments to show sustained national or international acclaim). Although the letters highlight 
the Petitioner's work, they do not reflect a career ofacclaimed work in the field, garnering the required 
sustained national or international acclaim. See Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 1: 19-CV-706 (MAD/CFH), 
2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the submitted letters failed to establish the 
plaintiffs contributions resulted in significant acclaim).

In addition, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the limited press coverage she received from 
November 2023 until August 2024 was consistent with the sustained national or international acclaim 
necessary for this highly restrictive classification. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Nor has the 
Petitioner shown how her overall media coverage is indicative of a level of success consistent with 
being among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F .R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). Thus, the Petitioner has not established that the limited media reporting on her and her 
activities is reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. 
Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990). The commentary for the proposed regulations implementing 
section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high standard be 
set for aliens ofextraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present 
more extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 
30704 (July 5, 1991 ). The Petitioner's minimal media exposure does not meet this very high standard. 
See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 386-87 (stating that the narrow definition reflects the stringency of 
the extraordinary ability standard). The Petitioner has not addressed these reasonable conclusions 
made in our prior decision with additional evidence to demonstrate that press coverage of her work 
reflected sustained acclaim and her being at the very top of her field.

In sum, in considering the totality ofthe evidence, the Petitioner has not submitted sufficient new facts 
supported by documentary evidence to demonstrate her eligibility for the requested benefit and to 
overcome our prior dismissal of the appeal. As such, the motion to reopen will be dismissed. 
II. MOTION TO RECONSIDER 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l )(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner provides little discussion as to how our prior decision represented an 
incorrect application or law or policy. The Petitioner's contentions on motion are overwhelmingly 
focused on the claimed new documentary evidence we have discussed in the prior section. The 
5 
exception to this is the Petitioner's discussion of her citation record. The Petitioner asserts that we 
acknowledged a high citation record for her work and thereby her claimed sustained acclaim and top 
position within the field. 
When discussing the Petitioner's publication and citation history, we indicated that the Petitioner did 
not provide sufficient evidence that her publication in the listed journals was an accomplishment only 
achieved by those at the top of the field, or one which automatically elevates a researcher to that level, 
particularly since authoring scholarly articles is often inherent to the work of professors and 
researchers in academia. We further stated that the Petitioner did not discuss how many ofher citations 
were self-citations and reasoned that the comparative ranking for three works was not sufficient to 
establish that she is recognized as being among the small percentage of academics at the top of her 
field. We also reasoned that the Petitioner's discussion of her citation frequency was strictly 
quantitative and that she did not sufficiently provide context and discuss the reason for her citations, 
involving qualitative analysis. We concluded that, overall, the Petitioner did not show that the citations 
to her published research demonstrated recognition at a level consistent with being among that small 
percentage at the very top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). We further indicated that the statute 
requires "extensive documentation," while the Petitioner only relied on metrics and data to 
mathematically attempt to place herself at the top of the field. We stated that, however, the record did 
not sufficiently demonstrate the relevance and reliability ofthe metrics and comparative data provided 
and lacked other persuasive evidence of her sustained acclaim. 5 
The Petitioner contends on motion that her "heavy citation record" indicates that others in the field are 
"paying close attention to and showing great interest in [her] work," suggesting that she is a 
"nationally/internationally" acclaimed scholar. However, this contention merely reiterates the 
Petitioner's prior assertions and she provides little indication as to how we erred in our prior reasoning. 
The Petitioner does not specifically articulate how her citations place her at the top of her field, nor 
does she discuss the qualitative nature of her citations. As noted, many in academia may have 
significant citations to their work without having sustained acclaim or being accepted as at the very 
top of their field. 
As such, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that our conclusions with respect to her published works 
and our determination on the final merits were inconsistent with applicable law and policy. Therefore, 
the motion to reconsider must be dismissed. 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed.
