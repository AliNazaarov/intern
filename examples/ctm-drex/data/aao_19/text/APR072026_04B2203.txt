Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an information technology provider in the area of artificial intelligence (Al), seeks to 
classify the Beneficiary as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the 
Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that that the Beneficiary met the initial evidence requirements for this classification through evidence 
of either a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or by meeting at least 
three of the ten evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)( 1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Beneficiary is the Petitioner's founder and Chief Executive Officer (CEO). The Petitioner is a 
provider of an AI platform that improves the performance of machine learning models. The 
Beneficiary currently holds 0-1 nonimmigrant status and intends to continue to work in this capacity 
in the United States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must show that he satisfies at least three ofthe alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Beneficiary met one of the 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to his participation as a judge of the work 
of others. We agree with SCOPS' determination for this criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts 
that the Beneficiary also meets six additional evidentiary criteria, has sustained national or 
international acclaim, and is one of the small percentage at the top of his field. After reviewing all 
the evidence in the record, we conclude that he does not meet the initial evidence requirement for this 
classification and thus has not established eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
1. Evidence of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." The 
Petitioner bases the Beneficiary's claim to this criterion on two things: the Petitioner's receipt of 
$690,000 in funding from venture capital firms, and the Beneficiary's receipt of the first prize in a 
hackathon. We agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner has not established that either qualifies under 
this criterion.

4. Performed in a Leading or Critical Role for Organizations with a Distinguished Reputation 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
SCOPS acknowledged that the Beneficiary performs in a leading role for the Petitioner, but concluded 
that the evidence was insufficient to show that the Petitioner has a distinguished reputation. In 
particular, SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not show that the media articles about it published 
on several websites were sufficient indicators of a distinguished reputation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner emphasizes its participation in YC's accelerator program as an indicator of 
a distinguished reputation. We acknowledge the evidence showing that selection for YC's accelerator 
program carries a certain level of distinction given its low acceptance rate and high profile amongst 
technology entrepreneurs. But this is just one of several factors to consider in determining whether 
the Petitioner has a distinguished reputation, and per the foregoing analysis we conclude that the 
Petitioner has not established that it has achieved the requisite level of distinction. 
6 
The Petitioner also focuses on a statement in the USCIS Policy Manual that "significant funding from 
government entities, venture capital funds, angel investors, or other such funders commensurate with 
funding rounds generally achieved for that startup's stage and industry" may be a positive factor when 
considering the reputation of an entity. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). It asserts 
that since YC made its standard investment of $500,000, whereas some other startup accelerators 
provide $100,000 or $120,000 in funding, this is proof that its investment represents significant 
funding which shows the Petitioner's distinguished reputation. But the Policy Manual does not 
suggest that reputation may be demonstrated by examining each individual investment source 
piecemeal. Rather, it is the significance of the total funding received by an entity that may positively 
reflect on the entity's reputation. Here, the Petitioner has not provided evidence that its total funding 
of $690,000 can be considered significant when compared to the total funding received by startups in 
general at a comparable stage and in its industry. 
In addition, the Petitioner submitted evidence of media coverage it has received, which is another 
factor mentioned by the USCIS Policy Manual as probative in determining whether an organization 
or establishment has a distinguished reputation. Id. This included blog articles on the websites 
tryfondo.com and hiretop.com, as well as articles on other websites such as 
www.thefounderspack.beehiiv.com and www.wing.vc. But as noted in SCOPS' decision, the record 
does not include evidence of the readership or viewership of the websites where these articles were 
published, and so does not demonstrate the level of recognition or prestige that these articles might 
have generated for the Petitioner. 
Other factors discussed in the USCIS Policy Manual include the relative size or longevity of the 
organization (noted as not determining factors by themselves) and the scale of its customer base. 
Here, the evidence indicates that the Petitioner was established in 2024, and the record is silent 
regarding the size ofthe business or whether it has any customers. When considered together with the 
evidence discussed above, the evidence is insufficient to establish that the Petitioner has a 
distinguished reputation. Accordingly, the Petitioner has not shown that the Beneficiary has served in 
a leading or critical role for an organization having a distinguished reputation. 
5. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." The 
Petitioner asserts that the Beneficiary's 42.5% stake in the Petitioner is significantly high 
remuneration in relation to others in the field of technology entrepreneurialism. We first note that for 
purposes of this criterion, the comparison of the percentage of equity in the Petitioner held by the 
Beneficiary with that of executives in other companies is irrelevant because it does not take into 
account the value of that equity. The plain language of this criterion requires consideration of the 
value of the remuneration commanded in order to determine whether it is significantly high in relation 
to others in the field. 
Second, the figure suggested by the Petitioner for the value of the Beneficiary's equity ($10,625,002) 
is not supported in the record, which indicates that this figure is highly speculative. The Petitioner 
bases this figure on the Beneficiary's equity percentage multiplied by the "post-value money" stated 
in one ofthe Petitioner's SAFE agreements with a venture capital firm. However, the article at Exhibit 
7 
I.5 of the Petitioner's RFE response defines "post-money value" as "the amount that a given pre­
money value infers the company to be worth at the moment immediately following an investment." 
And the same evidence defines "pre-money value" as "the amount that an investor and the company 
agree to deem the company to be worth immediately prior to the investor's investment." After 
humorously explaining the process of determining pre-money value, the article explains that it "is 
often not derived from accounting measures such as revenue, free cash flow or EBITDA," and "is 
often highly negotiated, driven by market forces and inherently speculative." Accordingly, the figure 
proposed by the Petitioner for the Beneficiary's remuneration is insufficiently supported and will not 
be considered. 
Third, the Petitioner has not provided a relevant basis for comparison of the Beneficiary's 
remuneration, whether actual or speculative, to that of others in his field. As discussed above, the 
comparison of percentages of equity held versus industry norms is not relevant absent the context of 
valuation. 
For all of the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary meets 
this criterion. 
B. Other Evidentiary Criteria 
Beyond the five evidentiary criteria discussed above (in addition to the judging criterion which we 
acknowledged that the Beneficiary meets), the Petitioner also asserts on appeal that the Beneficiary, 
through comparable evidence, has established that he has displayed his work at artistic exhibitions or 
showcases. However, per our analysis above, the Beneficiary cannot satisfy the initial evidence 
requirement at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) by meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria. Therefore, 
we decline to reach and hereby reserve any discussion of this additional criterion. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
C. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F.Supp.3d 798,814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, 
evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). We therefore 
decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
C. 0-1 Classification 
The record reflects that the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification reserved for nonimmigrants 
of extraordinary ability. However, the prior approval does not preclude USCIS from denying an

immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a different standard - statute, regulations, and 
case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. 
June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 
21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). 
Many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., 
Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 (D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. US. Dep 't 
ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 (D.D.C. 1999), ajf'd sub nom. IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep 't ofJust., INS, 
No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 1999); Fedin Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 
1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), aff'd, 905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority over 
SCOPS, the office adjudicating nonimmigrant visa petitions, is comparable to the relationship between 
a court of appeals and a district court. Even if SCOPS has approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf 
of an individual, we are not bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another immigrant 
petition. See Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. CIV. A. 98-2855, 2000 WL 282785, at 
*2 (E.D. La. Mar. 15, 2000). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not established that the Beneficiary meets at least three of ten evidentiary criteria 
under 8 C.F.R. § 2045.5(h)(3), and therefore does not meet the initial evidence requirements for 
classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
