Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a professor and researcher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner had not established 
she had a major, internationally recognized award, or that she met at least three of the ten regulatory 
criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. On January 28, 2026, 
we issued a Notice of Intent to Dismiss (NOID) and received a response on March 5, 2026. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal and enter a separate finding of willful misrepresentation of a material fact 
against the Petitioner. 1 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).2 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCJS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation 
is frrst counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
However, if a petition includes serious errors and discrepancies, and the petitioner does not resolve 
those errors and discrepancies given the opportunity to rebut or explain, then the inconsistencies will 
lead USCIS to conclude that the claims stated in the petition are not true. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 
at 591-92. Additionally, misrepresentation of a material fact may lead to other adverse immigration 
consequences. See generally 8 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at J.2(A). A material misrepresentation 
is a false representation concerning a fact that is relevant to the alien's eligibility for an immigration 
benefit. See generally 8 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, at J.3(E). Willful misrepresentation requires 
that a petitioner willfully make a material misstatement to a government official for the purpose of 
obtaining an immigration benefit to which one is not entitled. See Matter ofKai Hing Hui, 15 I&N 
Dec. 288, 289-90 (BIA 1975); see generally 8 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, at J.2(B). 
Misrepresentations are willful if they are "deliberately made with knowledge of their falsity." Matter of 
Mensah, 28 I&N Dec. 288, 293-94 (BIA 2021) (citations omitted). 
A finding of willful misrepresentation in a visa petition may be considered in any future proceeding 
to determine that an alien is inadmissible to the United States. Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) of the Act, 
8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(6)(C)(i). Further, the USCIS "Instructions for Petition for Alien Worker" 
corresponding to the Form 1-140, Petition for Alien Workers, includes a "Penalties" section with this 
warning: "Ifyou knowingly and willfully falsify or conceal a material fact or submit a false document 
with your Form 1-140, we will deny your petition and may deny any other immigration benefit. In 
addition, you will face severe penalties provided by law and may be subject to criminal prosecution." 
See USCIS Form 1-140, Instructions for Petition for Alien Worker (rev. 06/07/24).

3. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. The visa classification is highly restrictive, intended for individuals already at the top of their 
respective fields. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) (explaining "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor"). Here, because the 
Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we 
cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits 
under the statute. See also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby 
reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) 
(per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that 
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
B. Material Misrepresentation 
For the reasons discussed below, we find that by submitting an untrue and inaccurate Google Scholar 
Profile and expert letter to USCIS, the Petitioner willfully misrepresented facts material to the 
adjudication of the instant petition. 
The Petitioner submitted a printout of her Google Scholar Profile and an expert letter, dated March 
2025, in response to SCOPS' RFE notifying her that the existing evidence did not meet the criterion 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). As discussed above, in order to meet this criterion, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate her original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of 
major significance in the field. The profile indicates the Petitioner had been publishing since 2004, 
published 20 articles, and has 1332 citations to her work. According to the Petitioner's brief in 
6 
response to SCOPS' RFE, her 1332 citations are a direct measure of the impact and influence of her 
work. According to the expert opinion letter, the Petitioner "published a total of about 20 articles, 
appearing in prominent publications." The expert also stated he is "thoroughly impressed with the 
number of citations [the Petitioner] ha[s] received, as even her least cited article has been cited 16 
times, but her most cited articles have received 58 and 69 citations." (emphasis added). The expert 
went on to say her citation record presents "strong indication of the extensive impact her research has 
had on the field, as it is inspiring further research and innovation." 
In its decision, SCOPS notified the Petitioner of its inability to verify the authenticity of her Google 
Scholar profile. The Petitioner did not address this issue on appeal. Rather, she submitted a new 
profile which lists 5 articles with publications starting in 2008 with a total of 4 citations. And, in the 
accompanying "cover letter," she states that she has "published 8 scientific works: 5 articles in 
scientific journals, of which 4 [are] in specialized publications recommended by the Ministry of 
Education and Science, Youth and Sports of Ukraine." But the Petitioner did not explain the 
significant discrepancies between these two profiles. 
We issued a NOID explaining that it appeared as if the Petitioner had provided a fabricated profile in 
support of the petition, which was also relied on by the expert in his assessment and resulted in an 
expert letter that was similarly not true and accurate. By issuing the NOID, we provided the Petitioner 
with an opportunity to respond to adverse findings, as required by 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(16)(i). Within 
the NOID, we advised the Petitioner that if she did not overcome the adverse findings then we would 
make a finding of willful misrepresentation of a material fact. The Petitioner' s response to the NOID 
included, in relevant part, a "statement of explanation," and documents regarding her Google Scholar 
profile. 
The Petitioner's statement of explanation, dated February 2026, provides that she created her Google 
Scholar profile "to support" this petition. She claims she had not previously maintained a public academic 
profile. She states an "error occurred automatically within the Google Scholar system" and her "profile 
at that time displayed approximately 20 articles and 1,324 citations" and that she "assumed the 
automatically populated content was how the platform functioned." She explains she was aware her 
Google Scholar profile was not publicly accessible, noting the "system was still prompting [her] to make 
the profile visible" in August 2025. She claims she engaged an independent expert and provided him 
with a "screenshot" of her Google Scholar profile "believing the data shown to be accurate." She states 
she did not make her Google Scholar profile publicly accessible until February 2026, after verifying and 
removing all articles that were not authored by her. 
The Petitioner' s response to the NOID does not sufficiently rebut or resolve the evidence of her willful 
misrepresentation in the record. Rather, it raises additional inconsistencies in the record. She does not 
address why she believed her profile was "accurate" ifshe created the profile and saw it had, for example, 
populated articles she did not author. She also does not explain why she would then provide her expert 
with supporting evidence that she did not confirm for accuracy when she also knew he could not confirm 
its accuracy because she had not made her profile public and searchable. Nor does she explain why she 
did not take steps to have the expert letter, which relied on her untrue profile, corrected. Further, she does 
not address why she then submitted the untrue and inaccurate profile and expert letter to USCIS. She 
states she made her profile publicly accessible in February 2026, after verifying and removing all articles 
7 
that were not authored by her. She provides no explanation as to why she did not perform this verification 
at the time she created her profile, or prior to providing it to the expert and to USCIS. 
Section 212(a)(6)(C) ofthe Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(6)(C), provides: 
Misrepresentation - (i) In general - Any alien who, by fraud or willfully 
misrepresenting a material fact, seeks to procure ( or has sought to procure or has 
procured) a visa, other documentation, or admission into the United States or other 
benefit provided under this Act is inadmissible. 
To find a willful and material misrepresentation in visa petition proceedings, an immigration officer 
must determine: 1) that the petitioner or beneficiary made a false representation to an authorized 
official of the United States government; 2) that the misrepresentation was willfully made; and 3) that 
the fact misrepresented was material. See Matter ofM-, 6 I&N Dec. 149 (BIA 1954); Matter ofL-L-, 
9 I&N Dec. 324 (BIA 1961 ); Kai Hing Hui, 15 I&N Dec. at 288; see generally 8 USCJS Policy Manual, 
supra, at J.2(B). Because of the potential, severe consequences to noncitizens, we must "closely 
scrutinize" findings of fraud or material misrepresentation. Matter ofY-G-, 20 I&N Dec. 794, 797 (BIA 
1994). 
First, the Petitioner submitted a Google Scholar profile and an expert letter that were not true and correct 
in support of her immigrant visa petition, which amounts to making a false representation to a U.S. 
government official. 
Second, the Petitioner willfully made the false representations. See Matter ofMensah, 28 I&N Dec. at 
293-94 (Misrepresentations are willful if they are "deliberately made with knowledge of their falsity.") 
We acknowledge the Petitioner's statements that she created her Google Scholar profile for the purpose 
of the petition and had not previously maintained a public academic profile. However, she explains that 
she was aware her profile was populated with incorrect information. She states an "error occurred 
automatically within the Google Scholar system" and her "profile at that time displayed approximately 
20 aiiicles and 1,324 citations" and that she "assumed the automatically populated content was how the 
platform functioned." Despite this knowledge, she then provided the profile to her expert and does not 
claim she made any efforts to have the expert letter corrected. She then submitted the Google Scholar 
profile and the expert letter to USCIS, and referred to them in her brief, stating they are a direct measure 
of the impact and influence of her work. Further, the Form I-140 contains the following certification: 
"I certify, under penalty of perjury, that I have reviewed this petition, I understand all of the 
information contained in, and submitted with, my petition, and all of this information is complete, true 
and correct." On the basis of the Petitioner's affirmation, made under penalty of perjury, we find that 
the Petitioner willfully and knowingly submitted her Google Scholar profile and the expert letter. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(a)(2) (providing that by signing the benefit request, the petitioner certifies under 
penalty ofperjury that the benefit request, and all evidence submitted with it, either at the time offiling 
or thereafter, is true and correct); Matter of Valdez, 27 I&N Dec. 496, 499-500 (BIA 2018) (The 
presence of the Petitioner's signature on the ce1iification in the petition creates a strong presumption 
that she knew the petition's contents and assented to it.) 
Third, the Petitioner's misrepresentation was material. The Petitioner sought to procure an immigrant 
visa as an alien of extraordinary ability pursuant to section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. She claimed she 
8 
met at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary requirements to satisfy the first step ofthe Kazarian analysis, 
including the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. The 
criterion requires her to demonstrate her original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business­
related contributions of major significance in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). She submitted to 
USCIS a Google Scholar profile and expert letter which were not true and correct in support of the 
criterion. Her brief, in response to the RFE, highlights the relevance of her Google Scholar profile, 
stating her 1332 citations were a direct measure of the impact and influence of her work. And, 
according to the expert opinion letter, her citation record presented "strong indication of the extensive 
impact her research has had on the field, as it is inspiring further research and innovation." This 
evidence was relevant and therefore material. See generally 8 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at J.3(E) 
(providing, as guidance, a "material" misrepresentation is a false representation concerning a fact that 
is relevant to the alien's eligibility for an immigration benefit.) Further, this evidence would be 
revisited again in a final merits determination, where we consider the totality of the material provided 
and assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that 
the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the fiel d of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. We therefore find that the Petitioner's misrepresentations were material to 
her eligibility. 
For the reasons discussed, the Petitioner's response to the NOID does not sufficiently cure the issues in 
the record, which demonstrate that she submitted documents she knew to be untrue and inaccurate to 
USCIS in support of her immigrant visa petition as an alien of extraordinary ability. Consequently, the 
facts and evidence presented warrant a finding of willful misrepresentation of a material fact against the 
Petitioner. The appeal is dismissible solely on this basis. See Matter of Ho, 19 I&N Dec. at 591 
(providing that if a petition includes serious errors and discrepancies, and the petitioner does not 
resolve those errors and discrepancies when given the opportunity to rebut or explain, then the 
inconsistencies will lead USCIS to conclude that the claims stated in the petition are not true); USCIS 
Form I-140, Instructions for Petition for Alien Worker (rev. 06/07/24) ("If you knowingly and 
willfully falsify or conceal a material fact or submit a false document with your Form I-140, we will 
deny your petition .. . . "); see also 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(a)(l) (the form instructions carry the weight of 
regulations). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability and the appeal is dismissed on this ground. Further, by submitting documents 
in support of the petition that were not true and accurate, the Petitioner has sought to procure an 
immigration benefit through willful misrepresentation of material facts. This finding of willful 
material misrepresentation shall be considered in any future proceeding where the Petitioner' s 
admissibility is an issue. See section 212(a)(6)(C) of the Act. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
