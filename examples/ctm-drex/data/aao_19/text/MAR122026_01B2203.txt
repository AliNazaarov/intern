Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner warranted a favorable determination in the case's final merits analysis. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F .R. § I 03 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner initially claimed he met six ofthese criteria. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner 
satisfied three of the criteria, relating to participation as a judge, authorship of scholarly articles, and 
performing in a leading or critical role. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi), and (viii). However, SCOPS 
ultimately determined that the totality ofthe evidence in the record did not warrant a favorable decision 
in the final merits determination. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he meets three additional criteria: published material, original 
contribution of major significance, and a high salary. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), and (ix). He 
further contends that SCOPS erred in not considering "evidence under the rejected criteria" and also 
erred in concluding that the record did not establish sustained national or international acclaim or that 
he is among the small percentage at the very top of his field. Here, we agree that SCOPS did not fully 
discuss the evidence relating to the denied criteria in their final merits determination. However, upon 
review, we conclude that the record does not sufficiently demonstrate that the Petitioner has sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that he is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field of endeavor. 
We first address the Petitioner's claim that he meets the additional criteria that SCOPS determined he 
did not satisfy. Since he meets the minimum required number of criteria, it is unnecessary that we 
make a decision regarding the additional criteria. We therefore reserve reaching a determination on 
the remaining criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
As the Petitioner has met the requisite initial evidentiary criteria we will evaluate whether he has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim and 
that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. In a final merits 
determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to

Furthermore, the Petitioner has not shown that he has risen to that small percentage at the very top of

standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the 
petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 
FR 30703-01, 30704. Here, the Petitioner has not established that he meets this very high standard. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner's success was limited to his employer and that he did not provide

I I," the author highlights the Petitioner's work to address deficiencies in food monitoring 
for products purchased online for delivery. However, the Petitioner did not show how this media

Again, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how his media coverage reflects a career of acclaimed work 
in the field or a very high standard to present more extensive documentation than that required. See 
Bodhankar v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 
777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the lack ofpress about the plaintiff was not consistent

The Petitioner contends that he has commanded a high salary in relation to others in his field and this 
is evidence that he is at the top of his field. The Petitioner asserts that his base salary is $158,490, and 
that this exceeds the 90th percentile listed nationally and in the I Iarea where he is employed. The 
Petitioner provides evidence of the average salary for the position of "Food Scientist and 
Technologist," stating that his core functions involve food safety leadership, product compliance, 
regulatory strategy, and scientific risk assessment and therefore his duties align with this job title. 
However, his resume lists his current position as "Manager III, Product Management, Fashion & 
Fitness." While his previous work included food safety and food science, his position and job duties 
listed since October 2023 do not; and therefore, we conclude that the evidence is not comparable to 
his current position and this does not farther establish sustained national or international acclaim or 
recognition as one of a small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 
8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, at *6 (M.D. Fla Sept. 30, 2025) (upholding that plaintiff 
did not provide sufficient evidence comparing his salary to other salaries in his field). 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the criteria for judging the work of others in his field, and 
we agree. However, as it relates to a final merits determination, an evaluation of the significance of 
his experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence indicates the required extraordinary ability 
for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22. The Petitioner provided 
evidence of judging various science competitions, peer reviewing journal articles, and providing 
auditing, candidate interviews, and performance reviews for his employer. However, the Petitioner 
did not establish that these instances contribute to a finding that he has a career of acclaimed work in 
the field or that this is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim. 
4 
The Petitioner did not show, for example, how his judging experience compares to others at the very 
top of the field. In addition, the Petitioner did not establish, for instance, that he garnered wide 
attention from the field based on his evaluation work. Moreover, providing audits, interviews, and 
performance reviews in furtherance of the job duties does not automatically demonstrate that an 
individual has extraordinary ability and sustained national or international acclaim at the very top of 
his field. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner apart from others in his field, such as evidence that 
he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging recognized, acclaimed experts in his field, the 
Petitioner has not shown that his experience places him among that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor. See Hamal v. United States Dep 't o_fHomeland Sec., No. 21-
5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 ( finding the petitioner did not show that he served as a judge of 
renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists or the competitions were considered prestigious or 
attracted significant attention by the field). 
Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The record 
reflects the Petitioner presented evidence showing that he published various articles within the food 
and science industry. However, the Petitioner did not demonstrate that his publication record is 
consistent with having a career of acclaimed work, sustaining national or international acclaim, and 
being among the small percentage at the very top of his field. The Petitioner, for instance, did not 
show the significance of his authorship or how his publication record compares to others who are 
viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 
(E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the Petitioner did not show the significance of authorships reflect 
that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field). 
In addition, the citation history or other evidence of the influence of one's written work can be an 
indicator to determine the impact and recognition that his publications have had on the field and 
whether such influence has been sustained. For example, numerous independent citations for an article 
authored by the Petitioner may provide solid evidence that his work has been recognized and that 
others have been influenced by his work. Such an analysis at the final merits determination stage is 
appropriate pursuant to Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1122. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he has 
"over a dozen scholarly publications with 25000 plus citations;" however, the record reflects that this 
is the citation history of one of the journals his article is featured in. The citation history of a journal 
does not equate to the citation history of the Petitioner's article within a journal. Without showing 
that his published work was unusually influential or pioneering in the field and it placed the Petitioner 
among the top leaders in his area, the evidence of his publication history does not establish his 
sustained national or international acclaim and that he is one of that small percentage at the very top 
of the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner provided numerous recommendation letters generally praising his work. However, the 
letters do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that he is viewed by the overall 
field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that he has garnered recognition on a 
national or international scale, consistent with being among the small percentage at the very top of the 
field of endeavor. Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's specific projects discussed above, 
they do not reflect a career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or 
international acclaim. For example, in the letter written by I-N-, the author states that the Petitioner's 
work presents "frameworks that can be adapted across industries not only in e-commerce, but in

pharmaceuticals, logistics, and consumer health." But the record does not provide sufficient evidence 
of his work being adapted across industries or that this would establish he is at the top of his field. In 
an additional letter, M-B- states that his work "has produced a set of standards and systems that others 
in the industry now emulate." However, the record does not provide information on who in the 
industry is emulating his standards and systems and how this would be evidence ofthe Petitioner being 
at the top of his field. A petitioner must support assertions with relevant, probative, and credible 
evidence. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. Here the record does not contain sufficient 
evidence to support these assertions. 
The Petitioner included additional evidence to demonstrate his sustained acclaim and that he is at the 
top of his field. This includes evidence of memberships and awards granted to the Petitioner. 
However, the record does not establish how these memberships or awards are evidence of sustained 
acclaim or that he is at the top of his field. For example, the Petitioner was inducted into ....l ____.

additional evidence to support these awards establishing sustained national or international acclaim or 
that he is at the very top of his field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that "[ w ]hen the evidence is properly considered in its full scope 
and cumulative weight, it demonstrates not isolated achievements, but a continuous and independently 
verified record of distinction over many years." Although we acknowledge the Petitioner has 
spearheaded successful projects, written scholarly articles, and has been granted memberships and 
awards in his field; the record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish 
the Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa 
classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those 
progressing toward the top. Price, 20 I&N Dec. at 954 (concluding that even major league level 
athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that 
extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Onaghise v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., 
No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding that extraordinary 
ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are "reserved for only the 
most talented" aliens"). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is 
indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of 
acclaimed work in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish 
that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient 
to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top ofhis field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons. In visa petition 
proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The 
Petitioner has not met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
