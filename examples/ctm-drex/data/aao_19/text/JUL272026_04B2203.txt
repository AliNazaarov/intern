Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a culinary writer and social media content developer, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( I )(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had satisfied at 
least three often initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as required. The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that he or she: 
• Has "extraordinary ability in the sciences, acts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seeks to continue work in his or her field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit prospectively the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 1 The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial

evidence requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing him or her among the small 
percentage at the very top of his or her field. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) 
(finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and 
regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner initially claimed eligibility for the classification based on the following criteria: 
• published material about her in professional or major trade publications or other major media, 
relating her work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include 
the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation. (8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii)). 
• performance in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a 
distinguished reputation (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)). 
• evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration

• documentation of commercial successes in the performing arts, as demonstrated by evidence

§ 204.5(h)(3)(x)) 
SCOPS issued a request for evidence (RFE) to the Petitioner to provide her with the opportunity to 
submit additional documentation because the initial record did not demonstrate by a preponderance of 
the evidence that she met any of the criteria she claimed. 
The Petitioner timely responded to the RFE. SCOPS subsequently issued its decision and concluded 
the Petitioner met the following criteria:

§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii)).

field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1110-20 (citing section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2) and (3)). Accordingly, we reserve those issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 
25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). So, we will dismiss the Petitioner's appeal. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
1. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that his salary or remuneration is high relative to 
the compensation paid to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Evidence relevant to demonstrating high remuneration may 
include: (1) tax returns, pay statement or other evidence of past salary or remuneration for services, 
(2) contract, job offer letter, or other evidence of prospective salary or remuneration for services, and 
(3) comparative wage or remuneration data for the person's field, such as geographical or position 
appropriate compensation surveys. Id. Evidence regarding whether the person's compensation is high 
relative to that of others working in the field may include: (1) geographical or position appropriate 
compensation surveys and (2) organizational justifications to pay above the compensation data. Id.

When evaluating a petitioner's documented remuneration and other data or information submitted as 
a point ofcomparison, we consider amongst other things the description ofthe occupation, the validity 
of a survey (if that is what is used as a point of comparison), the location and currency of the wage 
earned, and the salary rate being measured. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(B)(l ). 
We conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility under this criterion because the 
evidence in the record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's compensation is high relative to others 
working in the field. 
In the first instance, it does not appear that the evidence the Petitioner submitted applies to her field. 
The Petitioner submitted an abstract of an article by Arielle Feger from an unknown source to support 
her proposition that "[ c ]ooking is the most popular form of influencer content on social media." The 
Petitioner describes herself as a culinary writer and social media content developer. But the evidence 
she submitted to demonstrate that her salary or other remuneration for services is high in relation to 
other applies to many different fields, such as content creators, authors, content writers, and an 
unspecified universe of influencers. The data does not cater specifically to culinary writers and social 
media content developers like the Petitioner claims to be. 
And, even setting that aside, we would still conclude the record does not demonstrate the Petitioner's 
salary or other remuneration for services is high in the field because the documentation does not 
amount to comparative wage or remuneration data. For example, the Petitioner submitted an 
Instagram influencer earnings calculator developed by Influencer Marketing Hub aspiring to "provide 
earning potential guidelines" to influencers in general. It does not consist of actual salary or wage 
data, nor does it directly apply to culinary writers and social media content developers like the 
Petitioner. 
The Petitioner submitted a table of pricing per video across platforms appearing on Billo. The table 
expressed what "[i]nfluencers can charge." (emphasis in original). But a suggestion of market pricing 
for services does not provide a basis for a comparison of whether the salary or remuneration an 
individual receives or commands is higher relative to others working in the field such that we can 
evaluate that the Petitioner's recompense is indicative of extraordinary ability in her field. 
And the Petitioner's purported status as a "micro-influencer" or a "macro-influencer" on the social 
media platforms she participates with does not demonstrate that her salary or other remuneration is 
high in relation to others in her field. The influencer rate sheet the Petitioner submitted does not 
support the Petitioner's assertion of eligibility under the claimed criterion because it states "the 
numbers [in the rate sheet] are just ballpark figures. The actual rates depend on multiple factors, not 
just macro vs. micro-influencers."

2. Comparable "evidence of commercial successes in the performing arts, as shown by box office 
receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x). 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that she has enjoyed commercial success 
in the performing arts. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). This criterion 
focusses on volume of sales and box office receipts as a measure of a person's commercial success in 
the performing arts. Id. The evidence must show the volume of sales and box office receipts reflect 
her commercial success relative to others involved in similar pursuits in the performing arts. Id. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) provides petitioners with the opportunity to submit 
comparable evidence to establish eligibility if a regulatory criterion or criteria do not readily apply to 
the Petitioner's occupation. When evaluating such comparable evidence, we consider whether the 
regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence 
provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in the regulation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). A general unsupported assertion that the listed evidentiary criterion does 
not readily apply to the person's occupation is not probative. Id. If a petitioner demonstrates that a 
particular criterion does not readily apply to the person's occupation, a petitioner may submit evidence 
that is of comparable significance to the criterion to establish sustained acclaim and recognition. Id.

and conclude the Petitioner did not demonstrate that she met this criterion with comparable evidence. 
A statement from a petitioner can be sufficient to establish whether a criterion is readily applicable if

B. Summary and Reserved Issues 
As stated above, the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement for three criteria under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Consequently, we reserve evaluation of the Petitioner's claim of performance 
in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation and 
need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25 (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision. 
Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not 
support a finding that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the 
classification sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. 
Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league 
level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability"); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) 
(finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't 
o_fHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316 at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), ajf'd, 
2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan 41, 2023) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very 
small percentage of prospective immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), 
No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3,2020) (citing Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non­
Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished 
individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F.Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding 
that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for the visa 
as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of her work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress, H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has 
garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that she is one of the small percentage who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2).

C. 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
Service records reflect that the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification reserved for 
nonimmigrants of extraordinary ability. Although USCIS has approved an 0-1 nonimmigrant visa 
petition filed on behalf of the Petitioner, that prior approval does not preclude USCIS from denying 
an immigrant visa petition which is adjudicated based on a different standard - statute, regulations, 
and case law. Many Form I-140 immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior 
nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25 (D.D.C. 2003); 
IKEA US v. US Dept. ofJustice, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22 (D.D.C. 1999); Fedin Bros. Co., Ltd. v. Sava, 724 
F. Supp. 1103, 1108 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), affd, 905 F. 2d 41 (2d. Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority 
over the USCIS service centers is comparable to the relationship between a court of appeals and a 
district court. Even if SCOPS has approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf of an individual, we 
are not bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another nonimmigrant or immigrant visa 
petition. Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. 98-2855, 2000 WL 282785, at *2 (E.D. La. 
2000). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, and the petition 
remains denied.
