Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an artificial intelligence powered investment bank, seeks classification on behalf of the 
Beneficiary, its founder and a technology entrepreneur, under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for 
U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate a beneficiary's "sustained national or 
international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of his or her achievements in the field. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Beneficiary satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as 
required. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that a beneficiary: 
• Has "extraordinary ability in the sciences, acts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seeks to continue work in his or her field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit prospectively the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 1 The implementing 
1 If an evidentiary criterion does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, he or she may submit "comparable

regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial 
evidence requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish "sustained national or 
international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing a beneficiary among the small percentage 
at the very top of his or her field. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must demonstrate that the Beneficiary satisfies at least three ofthe 
alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). In the initial petition, the Petitioner 
claimed the Beneficiary satisfied seven criteria. SCOPS evaluated the Petitioner's claims in the initial 
petition as well as in response to the RFE and concluded the Beneficiary did not satisfy any of the 
criteria the Petitioner claimed. On appeal, the Petitioner claims the Beneficiary meets the same criteria 
and alleges error. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant classification for the 
reasons we will discuss below. 
1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards for Excellence in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 
USCIS determines if the person was the recipient of prizes or awards. See generally 6 USC IS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The description of this type of evidence in 
the regulation indicates that the focus should be on the person's receipt of the awards or prizes, as 
opposed to the employer's receipt of the awards or prizes. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l). USCIS determines whether the award is a lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prize or award which the person received for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Relevant considerations regarding whether 
the basis for granting the prizes or awards was excellence in the field include but are not limited to the 
criteria used to grant the awards or prizes, the national or international significance of the awards or 
prizes in the field, the number of awardees or prize recipients, and limitations on competitors. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

3. Published Material About the Petitioner Relating to His Work in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) seeks "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought." It also requires that such evidence includes "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." 
USCIS determines whether the published material was related to the person and the person's specific 
work in the field for which classification is sought. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The published material should be about the person, relating to 
the person's work in the field, and not just about the person's employer and the employer's work or 
about another organization and that organization's work. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l). 
In support of its claim to this criteria, the Petitioner submitted an article authored by~I----~

by simpleweb and evidence describing simpleweb. The record farther contains search engine 
optimization information about TechCrunch, such as its "website authority" and its "domain rating." 
And the record has documentation demonstrating TechCrunch has appeared on two "top 10" lists for 
technology news websites and biogs. 
SCOPS concluded that the article did not support the Petitioner's claim that the Beneficiary met this 
criterion because it was not about him. See Joseph v. Dir. ofTexas Serv. Ctr., United States Citizenship 
& Immigr. Servs., No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001, at *3 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (finding the plain 
language of the criterion requires the publication to be about the alien and his work in the field). We 
agree. Apart from mentioning the Beneficiary's name, identifying him as the Petitioner's founder, 
and including his photograph, the article does not relate to the Beneficiary and his specific work in the 
field. See Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *3-4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 
19, 2023) (upholding published material must be specifically about the applicant and focus on his 
personal work in the relevant field, not merely relate to the applicant's work, mention his name, or 
include his photo). And the Beneficiary has a single quote in the article about the involvement of 
humans in important transactions and how artificial intelligence software is used as a primary versus 
a supportive tool. See Nelson v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. CV 21-10334-LTS, 2022 WL 
20583561, at *5 (D. Mass. June 15, 2022) (upholding articles that only cited or quoted the petitioner 
are not about him). The Beneficiary is also credited with a demo of the artificial intelligence agents 
the Petitioner's employees utilize in the course of supporting the Petitioner's work as an artificial 
intelligence powered investment bank. But the article does not identify or otherwise discuss the 
Beneficiary specifically or attribute any specific contributions or achievements to him. The article 
instead appears dedicated to describing the Petitioner and its business offering paradigm. So, it does 
not appear that the article is about the Beneficiary and his work. See Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 
8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *4 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (finding the regulation and USCIS policy 
clearly intend for evidence under this criterion to say something substantive about the petitioner and 
her work). 
As the Petitioner submitted an article that is not about the Beneficiary and his work, we need not 
discuss whether TechCrunch is a professional or major trade publication or other major media. But, 
setting aside that dispositive issue, we would still conclude that the Petitioner did not establish the 
Beneficiary met this criterion because the record does not adequately support that TechCrunch is a 
professional or major trade publication or other major media. 
USCIS determines whether the publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Examples of qualifying media may include, but are not limited 
to: professional or major print publications (newspaper articles, popular and academic journal articles, 
books, textbooks, or similar publications) regarding the person and the person's work; professional or 
major online publications regarding the person and the person's work; and transcripts of professional 
or major audio or video coverage of the person and the person's work. Id. In evaluating whether a 
submitted publication is a professional publication, major trade publication, or major media, relevant 
factors include the intended audience (for professional and major trade publications) and the relative 
circulation, readership, or viewership (for major trade publications and other major media). Id.

The evidence the Petitioner submitted of TechCrunch's readership as described by simpleweb, its

intended audience, or its relative circulation, readership, or viewership such that we could determine 
if TechCrunch was a professional or major trade publication or other major media. 
So, we conclude the Petitioner has not met this criterion. 
4. Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) requires "[e]vidence ofthe alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." USCIS 
determines whether the person has authored scholarly articles in the field. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. As defined in the academic arena, a 
scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse. It is written 
by a researcher or expert in the field who is often affiliated with a college, university, or research 
institution. Scholarly articles are also generally peer reviewed by other experts in the field of 
specialization. In general, a scholarly article should have footnotes, endnotes, or a bibliography, and 
may include graphs, charts, videos, or pictures as illustrations of the concepts expressed in the article. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For other fields, a scholarly article should 
be written for learned persons in that field. Learned persons include all persons having profound 
knowledge of a field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(8)(1 ). 
In the first instance, the Petitioner contends that the Beneficiary meets this criterion and submits 
evidence of his authorship of four purported articles in the Forbes Technology Council newsletter. 
We note that three of the Beneficiary's articles are in fact not articles he has authored. None of the 
articles are a cohesive published piece. The articles are merely a collection of 19 or 20 quotes relating 
back to the subject of the posted article, with one of the myriad quotes being attributed to the 
Beneficiary. 
One of the articles,~-----------------~' did contain a byline crediting the 
Beneficiary and was a cohesive published piece. But the article's scholarly attributes are not 
sufficiently patent. In non-academic fields like that of the Petitioner, a scholarly article outside of 
academia can meet the requirements ofthe criterion ifit is published in one ofthe medium's described 
in the criterion and is for learned persons in the field. The Petitioner notes that the Beneficiary's 
article's scholarly attributes are patent because the Beneficiary "cites case studies and empirical data 
and philosophical views." But this conflicts with information in the record, which indicates that the 
Beneficiary's article does not identify any case studies, sources of empirical data, or foundation for 
his philosophical views. It appears that the Beneficiary's article provides an overview suggesting that 
generic "AI" or artificial intelligence tools can be of use to small business owners in mergers and 
acquisitions." The Petitioner also stresses that the article's appearance in Forbes Technology 
Council's newsletter hosted on the Forbes.com website supports the article's scholarly credentials 
because Forbes has "a learned global readership of C-Level Executives, Entrepreneurs, and IT 
Leaders." But evidence in the record describes Forbes.com, the website hosting the Forbes 
Technology Council newsletter, as "reaching the most 'influential and engaged audience of C-Suite, 
8 
business owners, IT Decision Makers, Business Decision Makers, and Millennials. "' So, the record 
reflects the Forbes Technology Council newsletter is targeted at individuals involved in business at a 
variety of levels as well as others who may have no interest in the Petitioner's field. Consequently, 
we conclude that the Petitioner's articles as appearing in Forbes Technology Council newsletter on 
Forbes.com are not sufficiently scholarly to meet the requirements set forth in the criteria. 
The Petitioner also avers that the article the Beneficiary published in Fully Distributed is a scholarly 
article and that Fully Distributed is a professional publication. We disagree that Fully Distributed is a 
professional publication. 
The term "profession" shall include but not be limited to architects, engineers, lawyers, physicians, 
surgeons, and teachers in elementary or secondary schools, colleges, academics, or seminaries. 
See section 101(a)(32) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(32). Moreover, "profession" means one of the 
occupations listed in section 101(a)(32) ofthe Act, as well as any occupation for which a United States 
baccalaureate degree or its foreign equivalent is the minimum requirement for entry in the occupation. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(k)(2). In evaluating whether a submitted publication is a professional 
publication, a relevant factor includes the intended audience. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
Fully Distributed is the Beneficiary's public blog. It purportedly "caters to a global community of 
technologists and futurists who share a common passion and curiosity about cutting-edge tech and 
how it will shape our society." The record does not demonstrate with adequate clarity how "a global 
community oftechnologists and futurists" is a "profession" as defined by the law and regulations. The 
record contains a printout featuring analytics purporting to describe the demographics of the 
Beneficiary's blog's followers. But the analytics are expressed in percentages and do not contain raw 
numbers. Because the Petitioner did not demonstrate Fully Distributed's intended audience meets the 
definition of "profession" as defined in the law and regulations, the Petitioner did not establish the 
Beneficiary's personal blog qualifies as a professional publication. 
So, we conclude the Petitioner has not met this criterion. 
5. Display 
The Petitioner avers that the Beneficiary's participation inl I Demo Day, where he 
presented the Petitioner's work-product and software platform before a group of investors, is 
comparable evidence of display of his work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. 8 C.F.R.

We may consider non-artistic exhibitions or showcases as part of a properly supported claim of 
comparable evidence. As we stated earlier, a Petitioner can submit comparable evidence to meet a 
criterion that does not readily apply to the person's occupation. When we evaluate comparable 
evidence, we consider whether the regulatory criterion is readily applicable to the person's occupation 
and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in the regulation. 
But in all cases, exhibitions must be public showings. The Petitioner's participation in 1....______. 
Demo Day was not a public showing. The record reflects that I IDemo Day is "an

invite-only audience of approximately 1,500 investors and media." Or in other words, it is a gathering 
of select individuals closed off from general availability for review. The record does not address how

we conclude the Petitioner has not met this criterion with its comparable evidence. 
6. We Reserve The Remaining Claimed Criteria 
We note the Petitioner claims the Beneficiary met an additional criterion related to performing in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 
However, as noted above, the Petitioner's unsuccessful demonstration of the Beneficiary's eligibility 
under the criteria we discuss means the Beneficiary cannot numerically meet the required number of 
at least three criteria out of the 10 available and the seven the Petitioner claimed the Beneficiary met. 
Accordingly, discussion of the remaining claim would serve no legal purpose and we reserve it. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Beneficiary has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
C. 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
We note that the record reflects that the Petitioner received 0-1 petition approval on behalf of the 
Beneficiary, a classification reserved for nonimmigrants of extraordinary ability. Although USCIS 
has approved the Beneficiary's 0-1 nonimmigrant visa classification, that approval does not preclude 
USCIS from denying an immigrant visa petition which is adjudicated based on a different statute, 
regulations, and case law. Many Form 1-140 immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves 
prior nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25 (D.D.C. 
2003); IKEA US v. US Dept. ofJustice, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22 (D.D.C. 1999); Fedin Bros. Co., Ltd. v. 
Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103, 1108 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), ajj'd, 905 F. 2d41 (2d. Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our 
authority over the USCIS service centers is comparable to the relationship between a court of appeals 
and a district court. Even if SCOPS has approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf of an individual, 
we are not bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another nonimmigrant or immigrant visa 
petition. Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. 98-2855, 2000 WL 282785, at *2 (E.D. La. 
2000). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established the Beneficiary's eligibility as an 
individual of extraordinary ability.
