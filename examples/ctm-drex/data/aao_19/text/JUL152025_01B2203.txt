Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a content developer and influencer from Brazil, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner 
met three of the initial ten evidentiary criteria for this classification listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claims to have met six criteria relating to lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized awards, authorship of scholarly articles, performing a leading 
or critical role, commanding a high salary, and commercial success under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), 
(vi), (vii), (viii), (ix), and (x), respectively. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner satisfied two of the six claimed initial evidentiary requirements 
relating to published material and to display of her work at artistic exhibitions or showcases but did 
not show that she met the remaining four claimed criteria. SCOPS therefore found that the Petitioner 
did not satisfy at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria for extraordinary ability. We agree that the 
Petitioner has satisfied the published material criterion. However, upon de novo review we will 
withdraw SCOPS' finding that the Petitioner met the criterion related to display of her work at artistic 
exhibitions or showcases. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that she meets the remaining five claimed criteria relating to lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized awards, authorship of scholarly articles, performing a leading 
or critical role, commanding a high salary, and commercial success. For the following reasons, we

the awards criterion). The Petitioner therefore has not met this criterion.

On appeal, the Petitioner also contends that her book qualifies as a professional publication. However,

term "profession" includes but is not limited to architects, engineers, lawyers, physicians, surgeons,

See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(k)(2). In the Petitioner's book itself, the publisher states that it seeks to connect

give everyone the opportunity to tum these stories and ideas into books that can be read by everyone," 
indicating that the publisher targets a general audience rather than influencers and content creators.

book is a major trade publication or other major media. She therefore does not satisfy this criterion.

3. "Evidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in

determines whether the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the person's work was displayed were 
artistic exhibitions or showcases. Id. While Merriam-Webster's online dictionary definition of

the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases 
be artistic in nature. Id.

5. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
Id. 
To establish that the Petitioner satisfies this criterion, she submitted, in relevant part, tax returns 
reflecting that that she earned a salary of R$ 96,656 Brazilian Reais, a Salary Express report providing 
an annual average salary for content creators inl I Brazil, and an expert opinion letter. She 
contends that, as she commands a salary that is 2.87 percent higher than the annual average salary 
earned by content creators, she satisfies this criterion. Here, the Petitioner compares her salary as an 
influencer and content creator to that of salaries of content creators not performing the additional role 
of influencer. She does not establish that she commanded a high salary in relation to those who are 
both influencers and content creators. Both precedent and case law support this application of 8 C.F.R.

§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix). See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a 
professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dept. of 
Homeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those 
performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 
968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 
891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) (comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of 
other NHL defensemen). Here, the Petitioner did not show that the comparison of average and median 
salaries of single-role positions reflects she commanded a high salary in relation to others performing 
multiple roles. 
Further, the Petitioner provides a Salary Expert report that provides average salary statistics for content 
creators. Average and median salaries are insufficient to establish the Petitioner commanded a high 
salary in relation to others in the field. See Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, No. 3: l 8-CV-01200-H-AGS, 
2019 WL 2330181, at *7 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019) (upholding evidence of average salary levels is an 
insufficient basis for comparison to show high salary in relation to others in the field); see also 
Kinuthia, 702 F. Supp. 3d at 11; Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 853 (C.D. Cal. 2022). 
Proving a salary is "above average" typically doesn't suffice to establish a "high salary" or 
"significantly high remuneration." More comprehensive wage data is needed to effectively 
demonstrate high earnings relative to peers rather than to incumbents into the field of content creation. 
Moreover, according to the SalaryExpress report in the record, the Petitioner's salary is 2.87 percent 
higher than that of the "typical incumbent in the position" of content creator and does not account for 
years of experience. The Petitioner does not show or otherwise explain how a salary report for a 
content creator not accounting for years of experience reflects an "apples to apples" comparison ofthe 
salary of someone with her stated level of experience. This salary report is therefore insufficient to 
demonstrate that she commands a high salary in relation to others in the field of content creation. See 
Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and 
recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding 
plaintiff must show that salary reports reflect an apples to apples, rather than an apples to oranges, 
comparison). 
In addition, according to an expert opinion letter submitted by the Petitioner and included in the record 
below, "the degree of difference (2.87 %) may not establish a significantly high renumeration in a 
global context." USCIS may, in its discretion, use advisory opinion statements submitted as expert 
testimony. See Matter of Caron International, 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988). However, 
USCIS is ultimately responsible for making the final determination regarding an alien's eligibility for 
the benefit sought and may reject or give lesser evidentiary weight to expert testimony that contradicts 
other information or "is in any way questionable." Id. Here we acknowledge that this letter further 
clarifies that "contextualizing inl lmarket illustrates [the Petitioner's] professional success 
and recognition," and concludes that she meets the "high remuneration" threshold for her field and 
location. However, this letter does not provide probative details showing or otherwise explaining how 
the Petitioner's success and recognition contextualize her salary inl lmarket in a manner 
that would indicate that it is high relative to others in the field. USCIS need not accept primarily 
conclusory statements. 1756, Inc. v. The US. Atty Gen., 745 F. Supp. 9 at 15. The Petitioner therefore 
has not met this criterion.

B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve arguments concerning 
the remaining criteria, as well as a final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad 
429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
C. 0-1 Classification 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS erred in failing to consider that she previously was 
approved for 0-1 nonimmigrant status, a classification reserved for nonimmigrants of extraordinary 
ability. We acknowledge that the record reflects that the Petitioner was approved for 0-1 
nonimmigrant status. However, this prior approval for a nonimmigrant classification does not 
preclude USCIS from denying an immigrant visa petition, which is adjudicated based on a different 
standard, statute, regulations, and case law. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3 :23-CV-0 1412-N, 2025 
WL 1708126, at *9 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025); Cuckic v. Jaddou, No. 21-CV-8395 (JPO), 2023 WL 
2586031, at *2 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 21, 2023); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, 
at * 1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025). Many immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves prior 
nonimmigrant petitions. See, e.g., Sunl[ft Int'!, Inc. v. Mayorkas, No. 20-CV-08869-JCS, 2021 WL 
3111627, at *11 (ND. Cal. July 22, 2021); Q Data Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25, 29-30 
(D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US, Inc. v. US. Dep't ofJust. INS, 48 F. Supp. 2d 22, 23 (D.D.C. 1999), aff'd 
sub nom. IKEA US, Inc. v. Dep't ofJust., INS, No. 99-5159, 1999 WL 825420 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 27, 
1999); Fedin Bros. Co. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103, 1104 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), aff'd, 905 F.2d 41 (2d Cir. 
1990). Furthermore, our authority over SCOPS, the office adjudicating nonimmigrant visa petitions, 
is comparable to the relationship between a court of appeals and a district court. Even if SCOPS has 
approved a nonimmigrant petition on behalf of an individual, we are not bound to follow that finding 
in the adjudication of another immigrant petition. See Louisiana Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, No. 
CIV. A. 98-2855, 2000 WL 282785, at *2 (E.D. La. Mar. 15, 2000). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
