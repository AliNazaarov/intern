Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an executive specializing in transit-oriented development (TOD), seeks classification as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act ( the Act) section 203 (b)( 1 )(A), 
8 U.S .C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
the initial evidentiary requirements, it did not establish in a final merits determination, as required, 
that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage 
at the very top of the field. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

On appeal, the Petitioner cites a district court decision, Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24-CV-3170 (D. 
Neb. Jan. 28, 2026), in which the court objected to USCIS' reliance on Kazarian to justify its use of 
the final merits determination as the basis for denying a petition. The Petitioner contends that Mukherji 
v. Miller is a "controlling decision" and "governing legal authority" in the present case. 
We disagree with the Petitioner's reliance on this non-precedent district court decision. In contrast to 
the precedential authority of the case law of a United States circuit court, we are not bound to follow 
a decision of a United States district court, even in cases arising within the same district. See Matter 
ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993). In this instance, theMukherji decision was issued by a district 
court in Nebraska. In the present case, the petition was denied at the Texas Service Center, outside 
the jurisdiction of the Nebraska court that decided Mukherji. 
Moreover, while we may consider the court's reasoning when it is properly before us, unpublished 
opinions are not considered binding precedent. See Suchite-Salguero v. Att'y Gen. United States, 
147 F.4th 355, 359-60 (3d Cir. 2025) ("[W]e have cautioned over and over again that non-precedential 
opinions carry little to no weight for future cases."); Wang v. Holder, 569 F.3d 531, 538 n.5 (5th Cir. 
2009); cf Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 719 ( considering the reasoning of district court decisions). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). 
The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
The final merits determination hinges on whether the Petitioner has established sustained national or 
international acclaim and has reached the small percentage at the very top of the field. This 
determination is related to, but separate from, the antecedent question of whether the Petitioner has 
satisfied at least three of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x).

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 l&N Dec. 
953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
see also Hamal v. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 
3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial ofpetition ofa published theoretical physicist 
specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even 
highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" 
did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree ofrecognition ofhis work that rises to the level ofsustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(l)(A) 
ofthe Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
