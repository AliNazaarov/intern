Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a mechanical engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F .R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

Regarding the published material criterion under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), SCOPS indicated that 
"[t]he petitioner refers to both publications as both major media and academic journals" without 
identifying "both publications." Moreover, SCOPS stated that "[t]he evidence submitted in support 
of the petition, depending on the source, describes the publication differently." However, SCOPS did 
not identify the evidence and sufficiently explain how the evidence or source "describes the 
publication differently." 
As it pertains to the original contributions criterion under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), SCOPS 
acknowledged the Petitioner's submission ofletters, authorship of scientific articles, citation evidence, 
and peer review service, as well as his 25 national patents. Furthermore, SCOPS generally discussed 
the Petitioner's "research-driven" field and indicated an "approximately 114 citations to the 
[Petitioner's] work." However, the Petitioner did not claim to have 114 citations to his written work. 
Rather, the Petitioner asserted that his "patented technologies have been cited 114 times" and his

written work received "more than 1,700 times by peers." 1 Besides acknowledging the Petitioner's 
patents, SCOPS did not farther elaborate and explain how his patents did not qualify as majorly 
significant contributions and confused his patent citations with his written citations. 
Because SCOPS did not sufficiently address the Petitioner's claims and evidence, including 
referencing incorrect or inaccurate information, we will remand the matter to issue a new decision that 
addresses the Petitioner's particular claims and evidence. If SCOPS decides that the Petitioner does 
not fulfill any additional criteria satisfying at least criteria, then it must explain its determination by 
addressing the Petitioner's claims and evidence contained in the record. If SCOPS concludes that the 
Petitioner meets at least three criteria, then SCOPS must evaluate the totality of the evidence in the 
context of a final merits determination. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
