Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a salsa dancer, choreographer, and instructor, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 
8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to 
those who demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not satisfy the 
initial evidentiary criteria as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We dismissed the Petitioner's 
appeal and her two subsequent combined motions to reopen and reconsider. This matter is now before 
us on a third combined motion to reopen and reconsider. Upon review, we will dismiss the motion. 
A motion to reopen must state the new facts to be provided in the reopened proceeding and be 
supported by affidavits or other documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). A motion to 
reconsider must show that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of law or policy 
and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record at the time of the decision. 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). The scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision and we will thus only 
review our latest decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant a motion that meets these 
requirements and establishes eligibility for the benefit sought. Matter ofCoelho, 20 I&N Dec. 464, 
473 (BIA 1992) (holding that a party seeking reopening bears a heavy burden ofproving that the new 
evidence would likely change the outcome). The Petitioner must establish her eligibility by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter this country to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry will substantially benefit 
prospectively the United States. 
"Extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of 
the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). A petition based on extraordinary ability must include evidence of 
sustained acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Such 
evidence shall include evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, international recognized 
award) 1 or evidence that satisfies at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
(including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial regulatory evidence requirements, we then consider the totality 
of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record demonstrates 
sustained national or international acclaim and the individual is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part analysis, first determining whether the documentation satisfies the required number of 
regulatory criteria and then conducting a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 
24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022) (affirming agency decision denying a petition under Kazarian analysis). 
The Petitioner previously claimed before SCOPS that she has met seven of the ten regulatory criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(v), (vii), and (viii). SCOPS found, and we agreed on appeal, that she met 
only two criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) (participation as a judge of others' work) and (vii) 
( display ofwork in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases). Over the course ofthese proceedings, 
including her appeal and two subsequent motions, she did not address and thus waived three of the 
seven regulatory criteria she initially claimed. Of the two remaining criteria she continued to contest 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) (publications about her relating to her work in the field) and (v) (original 
contributions ofmajor significance in the field), we determined in dismissing her last combined motion 
that she has met the plain language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) based on new motion evidence of 
published materials about herself and their viewership, thereby satisfying at least three regulatory 
criteria and necessitating a final merits determination. 2 
However, we dismissed her last motion to reopen because the totality of the evidence did not 
demonstrate that she has sustained national or international acclaim and she is in that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of the field to be eligible for this highly restrictive visa 
classification based on extraordinary ability, as required under section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act and 
Kazarian's final merits analysis. See also 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
F.2(B)(2), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (stating that final merits determination evaluates the 
entirety ofthe evidence together to determine whether a person has the requisite high level of expertise 
and sustained acclaim and is among the small percentage of individuals at the very top of their field). 
We also dismissed her last motion to reconsider because she did not specify any legal or policy error 
in our prior decision dismissing her first combined motion, and therefore did not meet the requirements 
of that motion as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). 
On instant motion to reconsider, the Petitioner generally asserts that "the conclusion" that the evidence 
does not show sustained national or international acclaim or that she is not among the small percentage 
at the very top of her field "may not have folly considered the comprehensive framework established 
by Kazarian v. USCIS [ ... ],and farther interpreted in Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022)." 
But the Petitioner does not explain how we misapplied these two cases, otherwise specify any error, 
or claim that we incorrectly applied any law or policy in dismissing her last combined motion based 
on the evidence then before us. Consequently, she has not met the requirements of a motion to 
reconsider and we will thus dismiss this motion. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3); see also Matter of O-S-G-,

As stated, where an alien meets the plain language of at least three threshold regulatory criteria, we 
then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination under Kazarian and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and the alien is among the 
small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See also section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
The above motion documents do not overcome our prior determination that the Petitioner has not met 
her burden of establishing sustained national or international acclaim and is among the few individuals 
at the very top of their field at the time of filing her petition, as required. 
In our decision dismissing the prior motion to reopen, we addressed in detail why relevant evidence 
in the record did not show that she has sustained acclaim and is in that small percentage of individuals 
who have risen to the very top of the field. We acknowledged that she has been an active salsa dancer, 
choreographer, artistic director, and instructor since around 2000 when she founded a dance school 
I Colombia, with her family. We addressed two awards she received: 
a 20 ~versity award, which was sponsored byOtourism department, and a 2019 award from a

schools there. The record, however, lacked probative evidence that these awards based on her work 
through her dance school were of national or international acclaim or otherwise indicative of prestige 
in her field as a whole. We also addressed another 2019 award she claimed to have received but noted

The Petitioner still has not provided any explanation or evidence that may help resolve this 
discrepancy. Although we also considered the claimed successes ofdancers and dance groups trained

visa classification limited to those among the very top of the field. Further, we noted that she did not 
address or dispute our prior determination on appeal that the record lacked objective evidence showing 
that any ofthe awards she and her trainees have received were nationally or internationally recognized.

On motion, the Petitioner reiterates her past accomplishments; reemphasizes her training program; and 
reasserts that she continues to be very active in the United States as a guest artist in reputable dance 
events and workshops and as a competition judge, since her last admission into this country in 2022. 
But we have considered these and other similar assertions and related evidence in dismissing her prior 
motion to reopen, and concluded that the record did not show she has enjoyed sustained national or 
international acclaim and is among the small percentage at the very top of the field, as required. We 
also noted that most of her activities following her 2022 admission, including invitation to judge a 
competition in 2025, did not establish her eligibility at the time of filing. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l), (12); 
Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N Dec. 45, 49 (Reg'l Comm'r 1971) (holding that an alien must establish 
eligibility at filing and taking additional actions to acquire necessary qualifications after the filing do 
not overcome initial deficiency); see also Matter oflzummi, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 175-76 (Comm'r 1998). 
The Petitioner avers that the fact that she recently opened her own dance school and registered a dance 
event inl ITexas in 2025, as well as her participation as a respected artist at dance events and 
as a judge of various competitions, show that she continues to expand her distinguished career. We 
acknowledge this assertion and related evidence ofher continued activities. But, again, the additional 
experiences do not establish her claimed eligibility at the time of filing. Even if we were to consider 
all her qualifications she acquired after filing her petition in 2022, they do not demonstrate that she 
has enjoyed sustained national or international acclaim and is among the few at the very top of the 
field who has continued to maintain such level of acclaim. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-1120; see also 6 USCIS Policy Manual, F.2(B)(2) (stating that final merits 
analysis evaluates the entire evidence to determine whether a person has shown, by a preponderance 
of the evidence, the required level of expertise and acclaim indicating that the person has maintained 
such acclaim and is among the very few at the very top of the field). 
She also submits many one-page recommendation letters from colleagues and her current and former 
students, all dated July 2025. These additional support letters generally praise the Petitioner; 
personally reiterate her influence and past and recent accomplishments; and attest to her activities in 
the United States as a teacher, guest artist, and a judge. Some of these letters are from the same 
individuals who had previously provided similar letters in support ofher prior motions; and many now 
also refer to her as a "he" or "him," undermining the accuracy of the translations and thus the 
evidentiary value of the letters. Further, as we noted in our decision dismissing the prior motion,

which specifically considered similar recommendation letters, the new letters still lack probative detail 
as to the claimed uniqueness and widespread impact of her teaching methods and whether or how her 
skills as a dancer, choreographer, and instructor were specifically adopted by others or garnered 
sustained acclaim in the field as a whole. As we have explained previously, the record lacks verifiable 
and probative evidence that may independently show the requisite sustained national or international 
acclaim that she and her letter writers claim places her among the very few elites in the field. 
The remaining evidence, including the 2012, 2013, 2021, and 2023 articles and photographs of the 
Petitioner and letter writers in various events, do not add new, substantive evidentiary value that would 
warrant reopening, as some of them are duplicates and others appear to provide different descriptions 
of the same or similar events they participated in, which do not show the kind of renown or prestige 
only few elite individuals are able to attain in the field, as she claims. The Petitioner also does not 
clarify which of her assertions and documents specifically constitute "new" evidence for purposes of 
satisfying the motion requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2), rather than continued reiteration of her 
past and current accomplishments as similarly highlighted when she filed her petition in 2022 and two 
subsequent motions to reopen in 2024 and 2025. Many of the documents she now submits in support 
of her motion to reopen also, as stated, do not demonstrate her eligibility at the time of filing, and the 
overall evidence do not otherwise now establish her eligibility. 
The Petitioner's assertions and documents on this third motion to reopen do not overcome the 
evidentiary deficiencies we specifically addressed in dismissing her prior motion to reopen or 
otherwise now demonstrate the requite sustained national or international acclaim that only few 
individuals at the very top ofthe field may enjoy while still maintaining such comparable level of high 
acclaim to warrant reopening. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed. 
FURTHER ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
