Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a professor, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b0(1)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner qualifies as 
an alien of extraordinary ability either as the recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, or 
that the Petitioner meets at least three of the ten initial regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
1. the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
11. the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, 
and 
m. the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

international relations from the I !Nigeria. She states that she is a political science

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or demonstrated that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must instead satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed to meet nine criteria: receipt of lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards (i); membership in associations (ii); published material 
about the alien (iii); judge of the work of others (iv); original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field (v); authorship of scholarly articles 
in the field (vi); display of the alien's work (vii); performed in a leading or critical role (viii); and high 
salary or remuneration (ix). 
USCIS evaluated the claims the Petitioner made in the initial petition and in response to the request 
for evidence and concluded the Petitioner satisfied only one of the regulatory criteria, authorship of 
scholarly articles in the field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that USCIS' decision lacked due 
diligence, misapplied the applicable law and policy, and was based on factual inconsistencies, 
misleading statements, and other errors. She farther maintains that the evidence demonstrates she 
satisfies all nine regulatory criteria. While we may not discuss every document in the record, we have 
reviewed and considered each one. Based on our de novo review, we conclude the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant classification for the reasons we will 
discuss below.

1. Receipt of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or A wards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." We 
first determine whether the alien is the recipient of prizes or awards, and then whether the award is 
nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence in the alien's field of 
endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this criterion based on formal recognition from educational 
institutions and social development organizations. Specifically, the Petitioner states that she has 
received several research rants from various organizations, including the I I

She also states that she has received institutional recognitions, 
including "Best Graduating Student," "Best Teacher," "Outstanding Research Paper," and 
"Outstanding Research and Presentation," and contends that these honors reflect "a high standard of 
achievement in the field and serve as strong indicator of national and international recognition." 
The Service Center Operations (SCOPS) determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion 
because the evidence did not establish that the claimed prizes and awards are nationally or 
internationally recognized. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that the organizations awarding these 
grants and recognitions are nationally and internationally recognized. 
We disagree. The record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's grants, fellowships, or institutional 
recognitions constitute nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the 
field, as required by this criterion. Unlike awards such as the Nobel Prize which recognize 
demonstrated past achievements, grants and fellowships are often bestowed in response to applications 
by prospective recipients, who describe the research or project which they seek to undertake. 
Additionally, grants and fellowships generally support future research rather than recognize prior 
achievements for excellence in the field of endeavor. For exam le, the Petitioner submitted 
information regarding the ~------------------------~ which 
provides fonding for "a nine-month period" to "carry out foll-time independent research" at the Center 
"in fulfillment of the proposal outlined in [the Petitioner's] application." The fellowship guidelines 
indicate that applications are evaluated based on factors such as the "significance of the proposed 
research," "the importance and originality of the project," relevance of the project to current policy 
issues, and the project's ability to resolve "a larger problem." These selection criteria demonstrate 
that the fellowship is awarded primarily on the merits of the proposed research and its potential for 
success, rather than the excellence of the applicants themselves. 
Similarly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the institutional recogmt10ns she received, 
including "Best Graduating Student," "Best Teacher," "Outstanding Research Paper," and 
"Outstanding Research and Presentation," constitute nationally or internationally recognized awards for 
excellence. Although these honors may reflect recognition within her employing institutions, the record 
does not establish that they are recognized beyond the awarding organizations or that they are nationally 
and internationally recognized. 
Accordingly, the evidence does not demonstrate that the Petitioner meets this criterion.

2. Membership in Associations 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements oftheir members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
Relevant factors that may lead to a conclusion that the person's membership in the association(s) was not 
based on outstanding achievements in the field include, but are not limited to, instances where the person's 
membership was based solely on the following factors (by themselves or in the aggregate): 
• A level of education or years of experience in a particular field; 
• The payment of a fee or by subscribing to an association's publications; and 
• A requirement, compulsory or otherwise, for employment in certain occupations, as commonly 
seen with union membership or guild affiliation for actors. 
Id. 
The Petitioner claims eligibility under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) based on her memberships in the 
Midwestern Political Science Association, the Athen Institute for Education and Research, the Nigerian 
Political Science Association, the Society for Peace Studies and Practice, the Teachers Registration 
Council of Nigeria, the International Association of Innovation Professionals, and the International 
Society for Research and Development. In support of her claim, the Petitioner submitted membership 
certificates, email correspondence, and informational materials regarding these organizations. SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the evidence did not establish that the 
associations require outstanding achievements oftheir members as a condition of admission. On appeal, 
the Petitioner maintains that these organizations are globally recognized in the field and that her 
memberships are based on "exceptional achievements." 
We disagree. The plain language of the regulation focuses not on the reputation or global recognition of 
the association itself: but on the organizations' membership requirements. To satisfy this criterion, a 
petitioner must demonstrate that membership is limited to individuals who have attained outstanding 
achievements in the field and that such achievements are evaluated by recognized national or international 
experts. The fact that an organization is well known or internationally recognized does not, by itself: 
establish that membership satisfies the regulatory requirements. Here the evidence does not demonstrate 
that any of the Petitioner's organizations restrict membership to individuals with outstanding 
achievements. The Petitioner's supporting documents demonstrate that the Petitioner is a member of 
these organizations. However, they do not demonstrate that admission or membership in these 
organizations are limited to individuals who have outstanding achievements in the field. 
Accordingly, the evidence does not demonstrate that the Petitioner meets this criterion.

3. Published Materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work ( or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. 
The Petitioner submitted several articles to demonstrate eligibility for this criterion and claims that her 
research and its impact on "national development and peacebuilding efforts" have been featured in 
"widely circulated and respected Nigerian media outlets." She further states that she has been featured 
in international media and articles published on websites, including accord.org.za; wilsoncenter.org; 
iprafoundation.org; hrfn.org; Amazon.com; and Goodreads, as well as Nigeria's Vanguard 
Newspaper; Eggonnews; Daily Sun Newspaper; The Sun; Daily Tribute; and Nigerian Tribune. 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the evidence did not 
establish that the published materials appeared in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that her submitted evidence demonstrates that the 
publications have significant national and international circulation and are recognized in the field of 
political science. 
We disagree. To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that the published material is 
about her in professional or major trade publications or other major media and related to her work in 
the field for which classification is sought. Here, although the record contains copies of articles and 
circulation data and readership statistics for some articles, it does not sufficiently establish that 
publications qualify as professional publications, major trade publications, or other major media. 
Additionally, the Petitioner did not establish the significance or relevance of the readership statistics 
to show the major standing of the publications. Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, 
at *5 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024) (indicating that the failure to provide context to circulation figures 
makes it difficult to assess their significance). While some articles briefly mention the Petitioner and 
her research, the record does not sufficiently demonstrate that they constitute professional publications or 
major media within the meaning of the regulation. 
For all these reasons, the Petitioner did not show she meets this criterion. 
4. Participation as a Judge of the Work of Others 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) calls for "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization for which classification is sought." The alien must show that they not only have been 
invited to participate as a judge of the work of others in their field, but also that they have actually 
participated in such judging. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), www.uscis.gov/policy-
5 
manual. Examples of judging may include peer review for a scholarly journal, conference, or 
government research funding program or serving as a member of a Ph.D. dissertation committee. Id. 
The Petitioner asserts that she satisfies this criterion based on her service as an editor-in-chief, editorial 
board member, peer reviewer, external examiner, panel discussant at academic conferences, as well 
as academic assessor, reviewer of research grant proposals, and evaluator of undergraduate and 
postgraduate students. In support ofthis claim, the Petitioner submitted letters, email correspondence, 
certificates reflecting her service on editorial boards, certificates identifying her as an external 
examiner or panel participant, and certificates recognizing her peer review activities. SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the evidence did not sufficiently 
document the nature, scope, or context of the claimed judging activities. On appeal, the Petitioner 
contends that she submitted sufficient evidence demonstrating that she has judged the work of others, 
including invitation letters and emails requesting peer reviews, peer review certificates, documentation 
of her service on conference panels, and evidence that these activities occurred within her field of 
expertise. 
We disagree. Although the record establishes that the Petitioner has participated in certain activities, 
the evidence does not sufficiently demonstrate that those activities satisfy the regulatory requirements. 
The submission of invitation emails, certificates of appreciation, or acknowledgements of service, 
standing alone, does not establish the extent or significance of the Petitioner's participation as a judge 
of the work ofothers. For example, while the Petitioner submitted evidence indicating that she served 
as a peer reviewer and editorial board member, the record does not sufficiently identify the specific 
documents, articles, or research proposals she reviewed, as well as the number of reviews completed 
or the role she played in the evaluation process. Additionally, although the Petitioner asserts that she 
served as an external examiner and academic assessor, the evidence does not sufficiently explain the 
duties associated with those positions or demonstrate that they involved the independent evaluation of 
the work ofothers within the meaning ofthe regulation. Similarly, the record lacks sufficient probative 
details such as when, where, and how the Petitioner judged the work of others and what criteria or 
methods the Petitioner used in the evaluation process. 
The record also lacks sufficient evidence of the selection process for these roles or establishes that the 
Petitioner was chosen because of recognized expertise in the field. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established that she has met the requirements 
of this evidentiary criterion. 
5. Original Scientific, Scholarly, Artistic, Athletic, or Business-related Contributions of Major 
Significance 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that their contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, have 
remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance. 
See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an original 
contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
6 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner asserts that her career has been "dedicated to advancing peacebuilding, conflict 
transformation, and sustainable development through impactful academic research, public engagement, 
and institutional collaboration." According to the Petitioner, her research has "led to the creation of 
actionable knowledge and policies that influence both local communities and international platforms 
committed to social justice and peace." In support ofthis claim, the Petitioner relies on her peer-reviewed 
publications and asserts that they address "critical issues in conflict, conflict resolution, terrorism, 
counterterrorism, gender, and peacebuilding." She further states that her work has "introduced original 
theoretical frameworks and models for understanding resource-based conflict, youth engagement in 
peacebuilding, and the gendered dynamics of extremisms" and that her books "shaped the field related to 
conflict and political science education." 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the evidence did not 
establish that her contributions are of major significance in the field. Specifically, SCOPS found that 
the record did not demonstrate that the Petitioner's work has been "widely adopted recognized, or 
transformative." On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS "deliberately ignored" her evidence, 
including the citation history of her publications and emphasizes her contributions to the political 
science field through her work and her foundation for women's right and development. 
We disagree. To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must establish not only that her work is original, 
but also that it has been of major significance in the field. The focus of the inquiry is not solely on 
whether the Petitioner has produced original research or professional accomplishments, but whether 
those contributions have had a demonstrable and significant impact on the field as a whole. Although 
the Petitioner submitted evidence of peer-reviewed publications, authorship of books, and citation of 
her scholarly work, such evidence does not, by itself: establish that her contributions are of major 
significance. Similarly, the Petitioner's assertions that she "introduced original theoretical frameworks 
and models" are not accompanied by objective evidence demonstrating that these frameworks have 
been widely adopted, incorporated into professional practice, relied upon by other researchers, or 
otherwise recognized as having substantially influenced the field. The record lacks sufficient evidence 
showing that her books have received widespread recognition within the field or remarkably impacted 
the field. 
Moreover, although the Petitioner states that she has established a foundation, the record does not 
sufficiently establish that the creation of this foundation constitutes an original scholarly contribution 
of major significance to the political science field. While the Petitioner also submitted letters praising 
her work and her research skills, the record lacks specific examples demonstrating contributions of 
major significance. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that she satisfies this criterion.

6. Performed in a Leading or Critical Role 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof: has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. 
The Petitioner claims that she satisfies this criterion based on service as a department head, 
participation on strategic and library committees, mentorship of graduate students, service as a 
conference panelist, contributions as an author and conference participant, and her roles as an editorial 
board member and peer reviewer. In support of this claim, the Petitioner submitted letters of support, 
appointment letters, and email correspondence describing her various professional activities. SCOPS 
determined that the Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because the evidence did not establish that 
she performed in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations. On appeal, 
the Petitioner maintains that she submitted sufficient evidence demonstrating her service as a 
department head, conference panelist, member of review boards, committee leader, and committee 
member. 
We disagree. The regulation requires more than evidence that a petitioner held leadership titles or 
participated in important professional activities. Rather, the Petitioner must establish both that the 
organization possesses a distinguished reputation and that her role was leading or critical to the 
organization's success or essential functions. Although the record indicates that the Petitioner served 
as a department head and held various committee and editorial appointments, it does not sufficiently 
establish the significance of those roles within the respective organizations. The evidence does not 
describe the Petitioner's specific responsibilities, the scope of her authority, or how her performance 
materially influenced the organization's operations or achievements. Similarly, the Petitioner's 
service as a conference panelist, mentor, editorial board member, reviewer, or committee participant 
demonstrates professional involvement but does not, without additional evidence, establish that she 
performed in a leading or critical role for the organizations themselves. Moreover, the Petitioner has 
not submitted sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the organizations hold distinguished reputations. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that she satisfies this criterion. 
7. Display of the Alien's Work in the Field at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to demonstrating that the displayed work 
was the alien's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the work was displayed must be 
8 
artistic exhibitions or showcases. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. USCIS may consider non-artistic exhibitions or showcases as 
part of a properly supported claim of comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). Id. While 
Merriam-Webster's online dictionary definition of "exhibition" includes public showings other than 
those that are artistic in nature, the plain language of the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and 
explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in nature. Id. 
The regulatory provision at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) provides petitioners the opportunity to submit 
comparable evidence to establish the person's eligibility, ifit is determined that the evidentiary criteria 
described in the regulations do not readily apply to the person's occupation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. When evaluating such comparable 
evidence, officers must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's 
occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that 
regulation. Id. 
The Petitioner asserts that her presentations at conferences should be considered to be comparable 
evidence under this criterion per the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). Specifically, the Petitioner 
asserts that she "actively participated in numerous academic and professional conferences," including 
conferences in the United States, United Kingdom, Italy, Greece, South Africa, Ghana, and Nigeria. 
She states that she has presented approximately "21 scholarly papers at reputable conferences, 
symposia, and academic forums" and has delivered more than "10" poster presentations. In support 
of this claim, she submitted conference certificates, programs, and letters from experts. SCOPS 
determined that the evidence did not satisfy the regulatory requirements because presentations at 
scientific conferences are not comparable evidence for the purposes of establishing eligibility under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). On appeal, the Petitioner argues that her participation in "more than 20 
conferences spanning three continents," along with letters of recommendation, certificates of 
participations, and letters of appreciation, establish that she satisfies this criterion. 
We disagree. The comparable evidence provisions at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) only applies where the 
criteria set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) do not readily apply to the Petitioner's occupation. The 
Petitioner has not demonstrated that the existing regulatory criteria are inapplicable to her profession 
as a professor and researcher. Moreover, the Petitioner has not explained how the presentations of 
posters at conferences and forums are comparable to the display of art in a gallery or museum. As 
previously noted, the plain language of the criterion explicitly requires that the exhibitions or 
showcases be artistic in nature. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1122 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(affirming our decision that self-publication of a textbook, college lectures, and conference 
presentations did not constitute displays at artistic exhibits or showcases). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that she satisfies this criterion.

We note the Petitioner claims she meets additional criterion related to high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services (ix). However, as noted above, the Petitioner's unsuccessful 
demonstration of her eligibility under the criteria we discuss means she cannot numerically meet the 
required number ofat least three criteria out ofthe 10 available and the nine she claimed. Accordingly,

we need not address the Petitioner's claimed eligibility for the additional criterion. We, therefore,

C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we decline to reach and hereby 
reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for 
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
