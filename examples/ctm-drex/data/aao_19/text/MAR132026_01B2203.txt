Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a digital media marketing strategist who seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 
1153(b)(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner had received a major, internationally recognized award, nor did 
she demonstrate that she warranted a favorable finding in the final merits analysis. The matter is now 
before us on appeal under 8 C.F .R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate 
eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Section 291 of the Act; Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the 
questions in this matter de novo. Matter of Christo 's Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). 
Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a digital media marketing strategist who founded and was the chief executive officer 
of her own marketing agency. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met seven of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied four of the criteria relating to membership, published 
material, judging, and the authorship of scholarly articles but that she had not satisfied the criteria

eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant category.

B. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether she has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim and 
that she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that her 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits 
determination, we analyze the Petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to 
determine if her successes are sufficient to demonstrate that she has extraordinary ability in the field 
of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(2) (stating that USCTS 
officers should then evaluate the evidence together when considering the petition in its entirety to 
determine if the petitioner has established by a preponderance of the evidence the required high level 
of expertise of the immigrant classification). In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not 
documented her eligibility. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at 
*12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. 
Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015); Bharathm Ram 
Nagaiah, v. USCIS, No. CV 23-2077-KSM, 2025 WL 1508028, at *14 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025). This 
approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the congressional record, the governing 
statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme intended to ensure only those at the very 
top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative 
framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough analysis ofthe legislative and agency history. 
The Petitioner alleges that within the final merits analysis, SCOPS "applies an improperly heightened 
standard to the step-two evaluation, demanding evidence akin to global celebrity status, worldwide fame, 
or recognition comparable to major international awards." After reviewing the denial decision, we do not 
agree that SCOPS applied any standard other than the preponderance of the evidence to the Petitioner's 
case. 
Prizes or awards. The record does not show that the two awards the Petitioner received are of such 
stature that they indicate she is among the small percentage at the very top of her field. The evidence 
reflects recognition from relatively limited or specialized programs, with no persuasive showing that 
these awards are highly selective, broadly known, or widely regarded in the field as premier national 
or international honors for excellence. There is little indication that they are comparable to major 
prizes conferred by well-established national institutions or leading professional organizations, or that 
they attract a substantial pool of top competitors. Accordingly, while the awards may demonstrate a 
degree of professional success and positive reputation, they do not, even in the aggregate, rise to the 
level of acclaim contemplated for this classification or establish that the Petitioner is at the pinnacle 
of her field.

reflect it is a highly-ranked journal. We observe the same shortcoming for the single article related to the

and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy

In evaluating a petitioner's authorship of scholarly articles at the final merits stage, the type of evidence 
that can show they are among the small percentage at the very top of the field includes documentation 
that their work appears in particularly highly ranked or otherwise distinguished journals, or conference 
proceedings, relative to other outlets in the discipline. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2). That may be demonstrated, for example, by impact factor data or other objective measures of 
journal standing, especially where a petitioner is the sole author, senior author, or primary contributor. 
Id. In addition, persuasive comparative citation data may indicate top-of-field status. Id. For instance, 
evidence that the total citation rate to their body ofwork, including a high h-index relative to others in the 
same field and at a similar career stage, can place them well above most peers. When supported by 
reliable, field-specific benchmarks, such publication venue quality and citation metrics may together 
reflect that the Petitioner's scholarly contributions have achieved a level of influence and recognition 
consistent with being among the small percentage at the top of the field. Id. 
Here, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how her limited publication record is consistent with having 
a career of acclaimed work and sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not 
submit evidence showing the significance of her authorship or how her publications compare to others 
who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) and 56 Fed. Reg. at 
30704. 
Leading or critical role. SCOPS determined the single entity for which the Petitioner claimed she 
performed in a leading or critical role did not enjoy a distinguished reputation. On appeal, the Petitioner 
only disagrees with that outcome, but she does not identify what, if any, evidence SCOPS might have 
disregarded or how her previous claims demonstrated eligibility. Instead, she only provides a bulleted 
list of elements she asserted before SCOPS that were already considered. 
At the final merits stage, evidence of leading or critical roles is most probative where it shows that the 
person has consistently occupied positions of genuine decision-making authority or indispensable 
responsibility in organizations that are widely recognized for their excellence in the field. This 
typically involves roles where the person directs or substantially shapes key strategies, programs, or 
outcomes; where the success of significant projects or business lines depends heavily on the person's 
contributions; and where there is credible, detailed corroboration from employers or other 
knowledgeable parties about the importance and impact of the person's work. In addition, the 
organizations ( or divisions) themselves should have a recognized standing-such as a strong 
reputation in the industry, a large or high-value client or user base, notable achievements, or 
meaningful positive media or industry recognition-so that serving in a leading or critical capacity for 
them reflects more than routine managerial or specialist responsibilities. When both elements are 
present-high-impact roles and genuinely distinguished organizations-they more strongly support a 
finding that the person is among the small percentage at the very top of the field.

The Petitioner's single instance of acclaim in this area, that was inadequate to satisfy the criterion's 
requirements, is not indicative of or consistent with sustained national acclaim or a level of expertise 
representing that she is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofher field. 
Compensation. The Petitioner claims that SCOPS erred by comparing her salary to those ofmultinational 
corporations or conglomerates. A review of SCOPS' denial does not support her allegation as they 
directly discussed the evidence she provided for the record and decided "the relevant salary data for 
comparison is[] a senior-level President and CEO ...." SCOPS then noted that the Petitioner's highest 
earnings year was well below the average of such positions. The Petitioner has not explained how she 
has demonstrated sustained national or international acclaim or that her compensatory achievements are 
such that they are commensurate with those who are part of the small fraction of individuals at the very 
top of her field of endeavor. 
Final merits summary. Even affording the Petitioner the benefit of the doubt that certain items may 
satisfy the plain language of some criteria, the record as a whole does not illustrate a strong or persuasive 
presentation under any final merits category. The claimed awards are not shown to be highly selective or 
widely recognized; the memberships do not reflect exclusive admission based on outstanding 
achievements; the published material and judging evidence indicate only routine professional visibility; 
there is no record of influential scholarly articles or significant citation impact; the roles described do not 
demonstrate sustained leadership or indispensable responsibility in organizations of clearly distinguished 
reputation; and the compensation evidence does not convincingly place the Petitioner among the top of 
the pay scale for comparable positions. Viewed in the aggregate, this limited and modest evidentiary 
record fails to establish that the Petitioner has achieved the sustained national or international acclaim­
or is among the small percentage at the very top of the field-required for this most elite of immigrant 
classifications. In other words, we are not persuaded that her achievements spanning from 2019 through 
2025 are the types of sustained acclaim that are required by the statute and regulation. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's 
burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that 
burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
