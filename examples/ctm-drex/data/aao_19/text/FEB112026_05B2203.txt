Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a founder and CEO of a rapeseed oil production company in China, seeks classification 
under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with 
"extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 
203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claims to have satisfied four of these criteria, summarized below: 
• Published material about the individual; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• Commanding of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), (viii), (ix). 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner only met one criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) 
( commanding a high salary or renumeration relative to others in the field). On appeal, the Petitioner 
asserts that he satisfies the remaining three criteria. After reviewing the evidence in the record, we 
conclude that the Petitioner meets one more criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) (published material 
about the individual). However, we agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner does not satisfy other two 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) (original contribution of a major significance) and (viii) 
(performing a leading or critical role). Therefore, the Petitioner has not established he meets at least 
three of the initial evidentiary criteria, as discussed below.

1. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly 
high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). Evidence 
regarding whether the person's compensation is high relative to that ofothers working in the field may 
take many forms. Examples may include, but are not limited to, geographical or position-appropriate 
compensation surveys and organizational justifications to pay above the compensation data. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1), Criterion 9. 
We agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner meets this criterion. The Petitioner submitted his own 
income certificate and tax payment certificate from 2024 and comparative salary data from several 
sources, including PayScale, the Economic Research Institute, and Pageinsights, sufficient to 
demonstrate that his salary was high in relation to other similarly employed CEOs in China. 
Accordingly, this criterion has been met. 
2. Published Materials 
This criterion requires "published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such 
evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion as the published articles did not 
substantially discuss the Petitioner's work in the field and appear to be marketing materials about the 
company's products or services. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that these articles repeatedly 
mention his name and contained substantive discussion ofhis work as the founder and president ofthe 
company. 
Upon review, we withdraw SCOPS' decision. The published articles feature the Petitioner's 
company's origin and vision, activities regarding its rapeseed oil industry, efforts to integrate aid and 
tourism projects into the business model, and the Petitioner's role in all these advancements. The 
articles address not just the company itself but the Petitioner's role in leading this company. In 
addition, the Petitioner submitted information regarding the Chinese newspapers and magazines that 
have produced these articles along with the dates and authors and showed that they are major business 
publications in China. 
Therefore, we conclude that the Petitioner has met this criterion. 
3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or 
business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). For this 
criterion, the Petitioner must establish that not only has he made original contributions but that they 
have been of major significance in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
3 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. For example, a petitioner may show that his contributions have been widely 
implemented throughout the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance in the field. 
See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022) (for a petitioner's contributions to be "ofmajor 
significance," evidence must demonstrate that their work has been widely adopted or replicated by 
industry actors unaffiliated with them). 
The Petitioner offered several patents filed with the National Intellectual Property Administration in 
China, including "a grapeseed impurity removal device," "a cooling device for grain and oil processing 
production," "an oil press oil residue scraping and recovery device," and "a flow guide mechanism for 
oil press to prevent oil leakage." The Petitioner asserted that these patents are original contributions 
rising to a level of major significance in the field of agricultural manufacturing and submitted copies 
of executed contracts with other agricultural manufacturing companies in China for use of these 
patented devices. The Petitioner also provided letters from some of these manufacturing companies 
that have entered agreement withl lhis colleagues and business partners, a local community 
government leader, and other professional acquaintances. 
Although evidence that an individual's work has been patented may potentially demonstrate the 
work's originality, it will not necessarily establish that the work is of major significance to the field, 
without more. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), supra. For example, evidence that the person 
developed a patented technology that has attracted significant attention or commercialization may 
establish the significance of the person's original contribution to the field. See id. 
Here, while the Petitioner provided copies of registered patents of various oil processing devices, the 
supporting letters do not offer probative details on how any one of the patented processes or devices 
had attracted significant attention within the field. The letters generally praise the Petitioner's 
entrepreneurial endeavor, his expertise in sustainable agriculture practices and business development 
through his company, and the impact on the local community and his customers, but do not offer 
insight into how these specific patents are significantly utilized in the field of agricultural 
manufacturing overall. Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013) (finding the term 
"major significance in the field" can be reasonably interpreted to mean that there must be some impact 
"in the field as a whole"); Goncharov v. Allen, No. 3:21-CV-1372-B, 2022 WL 17327304, at *5 (N.D. 
Tex. Nov. 29, 2022) (stating that "[c]ourts have routinely affirmed agency decisions that held 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v) 'requires substantial influence beyond one's employers, clients, or customers'). 
Therefore, the Petitioner has not identified any original contributions of major significance in his field. 
4. Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). A critical role means an alien contributed in a significantly important way 
to the activities ofthe organization, establishment, division, or department. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.(2)(B)(l), Criterion 8. Next, the Petitioner must establish that the organization, 
establishment, division, or department has a distinguished reputation. The word "distinguished" 
ordinarily means "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." 
Distinguished, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant

However, the record does not establish that the Petitioner's company has a distinguished reputation. 
Reputation is a measure of outside perception, and therefore simply submitting general and internal 
information about the company, such as letters from officials ofthat company, projects that a company 
undertook, or awards that the company received as a result of those projects, does not sufficiently 
establish a company's reputation. The Petitioner must also provide a basis for comparison to show 
that company is distinguished in relation to othy compries in the same industry. In this case, the 
Petitioner has not provided evidence to show that has distinguished reputations in comparison 
to other manufacturing firms in China. Although the Petitioner asserted tha~.___.....~s "the largest all­

successful, the Petitioner has not provided the scale of its customer base or other media coverage relative 
to other companies of the same nature to evaluate its distinction and eminence. 
Therefore, the Petitioner has not met the requirements of this criterion. 
B. Final Mertis Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility 
for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
