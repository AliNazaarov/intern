Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an alien ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Beneficiary had received a major, internationally recognized award, nor did 
it demonstrate that it warranted an approval when considering the totality of the evidence. The matter 
is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to 
demonstrate eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of 
the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We 
review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 
2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)( 4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable material if he or she is able to demonstrate that 
the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A One-time Achievement 
On appeal the Petitioner discusses a one-time achievement and appears to incorrectly surmise that if a 
petitioner shows they have received a qualifying one-time achievement, that would be the end of the 
adjudication and USCIS would not perform a final merits determination. The Petitioner does not offer 
any citation to support this assumption. But the only EB- lA regulation that discusses a one-time 
achievement is 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) that states: "A petition for an alien ofextraordinary ability must 
be accompanied by evidence that the alien has sustained national or international acclaim and that his 
or her achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise." 
The regulation provides two methods to make such a showing indicating a petitioner's "evidence shall 
include evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, international recognized award), or at 
least three of the following," then it lists the ten regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Also, a one-time achievement is listed in the regulation as initial evidence along with the ten criteria 
further reflecting it is the first of two steps in this adjudication. 
USCIS policy also refutes the Petitioner's contention. Within the initial evidence section, the USCIS 
Policy Manual provides: 
The first step of the evidentiary review is limited to determining whether the evidence 
submitted with the petition meets the regulatory criteria.[] The evidence must be 
comprised ofeither a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized 
award) or at least three of the ten regulatory criteria or be comparable to at least three 
of the ten regulatory criteria.[] .... 
For purposes of the first step ofthe analysis, officers should consider the quality and 
caliber of the evidence to determine whether a particular regulatory criterion has been 
met, to the extent the criterion has qualitative requirements.[] Officers should not yet 
make a determination regarding whether or not the person is one of that small 
2 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field or if the person has sustained 
national or international acclaim.[] 
So, regardless of whether one claims eligibility through a one-time achievement or the ten criteria, 
they must still move on to step two and show eligibility through the final merits process in which 
USCrS evaluates the totality of their evidence. 
B. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed the Beneficiary met all ten of 
the regulatory criteria either directly or through comparable evidence. SCOPS decided that the 
Petitioner satisfied four of the criteria relating to: published material, judging, the authorship of 
scholarly articles, and performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner continues to 
claim each of the denied criteria. We find it necessary to address one criterion that SCOPS previously 
found satisfied, but upon review, is not actually met. 
l. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
Although SCOPS made a favorable determination under this criterion under step one, it did so without 
any explanation. We do not agree with, and we withdraw that favorable outcome. 
A review of the articles reveals that instead of being about the Beneficiary and his work in the field, the 
material is either about the company in which he serves as the chief executive officer and he is briefly 
mentioned, or the article is about several startup companies and it simply mentions him. 'The published 
material should be about the person, relating to the person's work in the field, and not just about the 
person's employer and the employer's work or about another organization and that organization's work." 
See general~v 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual; Gadhave v. 
Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *3-4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) (upholding 
published material must be specifically about the alien and focus on their personal work in the relevant 
field, not merely relate to their work, mention their name, or include their photo); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, 
No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *4 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (finding the regulation and USCrS policy 
clearly intend for evidence under this criterion to say something substantive about the petitioner and their 
work). 
It is insufficient that the Beneficiary be mentioned within published material. As the regulation does not 
provide that one may satisfy this criterion by relying on evidence that he or she was simply noted within 
published material, the published piece itself must be about the person and relating to his or her work in 
the field for which classification is sought. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii); Kumar v. USCIS, No. CV RDB-
23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding determination that the alien's 
evidence did not include a discussion ofhim and his work). Articles that primarily focus on a company's 
3 
work do not automatically qualify as articles about its leader, even if that leader is the company's head. 
Joseph v. USCIS, No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001 *3 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025). 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements ofthis criterion, 
and we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination on this issue. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether it has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, the Beneficiary's sustained national or 
international acclaim and that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, 
and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. In a 
final merits determination, we analyze the Beneficiary's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine ifhis successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability 
in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2) (stating that 
in a final merits determination USCIS officers should then evaluate the evidence together when 
considering the petition in its entirety to determine if the petitioner has established by a preponderance 
of the evidence the required high level of expertise ofthe immigrant classification). In this matter, we 
determine that the Petitioner has not documented his eligibility. 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 810 (E.D. Pa. 2025); 
Agrawalv. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. 
v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. 
Bankers Ass'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of 
the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3 l 70, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 
2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
The Beneficiary is the co-founder and chief executive officer of the petitioning organization, an 
automated quality assurance platform for software coding. Prior to starting that business, he worked 
at another company as an engineer with the Petitioner's co-founders. He also earned multiple 
computer science degrees. 
We begin by noting that within the appeal brief, the Petitioner attempts to point out issues with SCOPS' 
request for evidence (RFE). But what the Petitioner perceives was an issue in the RFE is not a proper 
basis for the appeal unless the same issues were replicated in the denial decision. The reason for filing 
an appeal is to provide an affected party with the opportunity to "identify specifically any erroneous 
4 
conclusion oflaw or statement offacf' in the below decision. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l )(v). We therefore 
will not address any issues the Petitioner raises that were only in the RFE. 
Additionally, the Petitioner may identify what it considers to be errors in how SCOPS evaluated its claims 
and evidence under the individual criteria in step one ofthe adjudication, but even ifwe agree that any of 
those claims would result in the satisfaction ofadditional criteria, that does not necessarily have a decisive 
impact in the case in step two in the final merits determination. SCOPS did not deny the petition because 
of a failure to satisfy the criteria. Instead, they denied it because the Petitioner did not offer extensive 
evidence showing how his achievements illustrated his sustained acclaim on a national or international 
level, and placed him among the small fraction of individuals at the very top of the Petitioner's field of 
endeavor. The Petitioner must still explain how SCOPS erred in denying the petition in the final merits 
analysis. 
On appeal, the Petitioner places the majority ofits focus on attempting to demonstrate that it has satisfied 
each of the criteria in step one of this adjudication and it neglects to home in on the actual basis SCOPS 
put forth in the denial which was its failure to demonstrate eligibility in step two of the final merits 
determination. When the USCIS Policy Manual discusses the final merits determination process, it 
requires more than vague references to a petitioner's supporting materials when it provides: 
In all cases, the petitioner must provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence 
and considerations to demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to 
establish the person's extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances. This 
means that the petitioner must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and 
how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2). It remains the Petitioner's burden to provide 
sufficient context about the evidence and explain its significance. But as we explain below, it has not 
done so in this appeal. A petitioner's burden of proof comprises both the initial burden of production, 
as well as the ultimate burden ofpersuasion. Matter ofY-B-, 21 I&N Dec. 1136, 1142 n.3 (BIA 1998). 
As discussed above, the remaining record does not demonstrate the Beneficiary's sustained national 
or international acclaim or that he has risen to the very top of his field. 
SCOPS acknowledged that the Beneficiary obtained venture funding, memberships such as Forbes 
Councils and Y Combinator, and some media coverage, but concluded that these items do not demonstrate 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence, nor a level of press 
commensurate with a "career of acclaimed work." SCOPS decided the record in its totality did not 
illustrate that the funding was broadly recognized in the field beyond the organizations that provided it, 
and the memberships were not shown to signify that only top of the field individuals are selected. 
Likewise, a few recent articles over a decade-long career were deemed insufficient to establish sustained 
national or international acclaim or that the Beneficiary is among the small percentage at the very top of 
the field. 
First, we generally agree with SCOPS that the evidence fails to show that the Petitioner's receipt of 
venture capital funding confirms that the Beneficiary has received a qualifying award. Second, the record 
does not clearly show that the funding was awarded to the Beneficiary personally, or that he alone was 
5 
responsible for securing it, as opposed to the company or other executives collectively. Third, even ifwe 
presume the funding can be credited to him, the Petitioner only stated the total amount raised and did not 
provide comparative evidence showing how this level of funding ranks against other technology startups 
or indicates that such funding shows he is among the top in his field. Despite these achievements 
occurring at a notable level, these examples are not sufficiently representative of sustained acclaim nor 
are so few accolades indicative of the very high standard to present more extensive documentation. See 
H.R. Rep. No. IO 1-723 at 59, Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. at 30703, 30704 (July 5, 
1991). 
Under the claimed memberships, Forbes Technology Council membership is primarily a fee-based 
networking community and conditioned on basic business metrics such as annual recurring revenue or 
venture funding, rather than on evaluating and selecting only those with outstanding achievements. 
Further, Y Combinator is an investment program that funds startups, not an association that confers 
individual "membership" based on evaluating and admitting only those with outstanding achievements. 
Also, the Petitioner did not establish that recognized national or international experts in the Beneficiary's 
field judged and approved what the Petitioner characterizes as memberships. Even in instances in which 
a petitioner's memberships demonstrate their professional stature, they will not establish the exceptional 
level ofdistinction required for this restrictive immigrant classification where they do not show that those 
organizations limit membership to renowned personnel in the field, or that membership requires 
outstanding achievements. Hamal v. United States Dep't of Homeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 
1156801, at *3 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023). 
The Beneficiary was included in three articles, only one of which was in a notable source. Even if that 
were not the case, the articles only briefly mentioned the Beneficiary and they were not primarily about 
him. We do not agree with the Petitioner that because "there are multiple articles within the record about 
[the Beneficiary] relating to his work in the field, [is] itself favorable" under the final merits analysis. 
Examples such as this, ifthey are all the Petitioner can muster, are not indicative of"that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor." A lack ofprominent press about the Beneficiary 
is not consistent with national or international acclaim. Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 
l 19CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020). Thus, the Petitioner did not 
establish that the limited media reporting on the Beneficiary and his activities reflects a career of 
acclaimed work in the field or that it was indicative ofthe very high standard to present more extensive 
documentation. See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59 (1990); Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. 
Reg. at 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991 ). 
SCOPS further found that, although the Beneficiary has served as a judge of others' work, authored a 
small number of articles, given presentations, and held noteworthy roles, the record did not demonstrate 
that any of these activities resulted from, or in tum reflected, a top of the field reputation. The judging 
was not shown to have been based on recognized national or international acclaim, the publications record 
was too limited and not demonstrated to be significant relative to others at the top of the field, and the 
presentations were not analogous to the exclusive exhibitions contemplated for visual artists under the 
regulations. 
While the Petitioner demonstrated the Beneficiary met the judging criterion through his service as a judge 
of applications to Y Combinator's Summer 2024 program, a single instance ofjudging at this level falls 
far short of depicting a career of acclaimed work at the national or international level. One example that 
6 
he is trnsted within a certain professional circle is insufficient evidence that he has occupied roles that are 
reserved for a small percentage ofleading figures in the field or are widely regarded as prestigious, highly 
selective appointments on a national or international scale. "The second step requires USCIS to evaluate 
the person's judging participation to determine whether it was indicative ofthe person being ofthat small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and enjoying sustained national or 
international acclaim." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). But the Beneficiary 
does not measure up to that level in this area. 
Further, the Beneficiary authored one article. But as agency policy provides, authorship and publication 
do not automatically place one at the top of the field. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2) (providing that publications should be evaluated to determine whether they were indicative of 
being one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor and enjoying 
sustained national or international acclaim). The Petitioner did not demonstrate how the Beneficiary's 
limited publication record is consistent with having a career of acclaimed work and sustaining national 
or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not submit evidence showing the significance of his 
authorship or how his publications compare to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. 
See H.R. Rep. No. at 59 and section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
SCOPS decided against allowing the Petitioner's comparable evidence claims under the display criterion. 
Notwithstanding that, the Petitioner claims the Beneficiary's work in the field was displayed at Y 
Combinator's Demo Day and that performance's qualitative attributes demonstrate his eligibility when 
considering the totality of the evidence in the final merits determination. While participation in Y 
Combinator's Demo Day is an indicator of entrepreneurial merit, it is more of an investor pitch event 
designed to secure seed funding and having less to do with being a recognized exhibition or industry 
showcase of an alien's work. The record contains no evidence that the Beneficiary's work was selected 
for display at other prominent industry showcases, national trade expos, or public exhibitions. The 
Petitioner's single presentation does not demonstrate the sustained national or international acclaim 
required for this stage of this restrictive immigrant visa. 
SCOPS acknowledged the Beneficiary's role with the Petitioner is noteworthy, but determined it did not 
rise to the level of establishing that he was at the top ofthe "field oftechnology entrepreneurship," as the 
Petitioner characterizes it. And we agree that his performance for the petitioning organization not only 
satisfies the step one requirements, but it is also notable. But the Petitioner's appeal, and the Petitioner's 
appellate arguments are not persuasive. The Petitioner claims SCOPS applied an elevated standard of 
review, and it intermingles elements from this adjudication's step one and step two when it states: 
The USCIS Denial concedes the regulatory elements of the criterion at 8 CFR 
204.5(h)(3)(viii) are objectively met, which, while accepting facts of [the Beneficiary's] 
critical leadership of [the Petitioner] to numerous distinguishing milestones, (1) USCIS 
narrowly scrntinizes facts for this criterion per an elevated standard that should consider 
the full record, and (2) fails to qualitatively review facts by which this criterion is a mark 
ofthe beneficiary's national or international acclaim. 
Considering their totality argument, the Beneficiary's performance for the Petitioner is one instance in 
which his role at a distinguished organization was leading or critical. But the Petitioner does not explain 
how this singular achievement in the Beneficiary's performance history places him among the top ofthe 
7 
field of technology entrepreneurship. Chwyumov v. USCIS, No. 2: l 8-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 WL 
5802041, at *3 (W.D. Wash. Nov. 7, 2019), aff'd, No. 2:18-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 WL 5802041 (W.D. 
Wash. Nov. 7, 2019) (upholding that success of one business does not equate to national or international 
acclaim). 
The Beneficiary's performance in their leading or critical roles should reflect their performance led to, or 
illustrates, sustained national or international acclaim. Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 820. The Petitioner 
has not demonstrated that one successful venture is demonstrative of acclaim that has been sustained. A 
leading role in a successful or distinguished company does not demonstrate that a beneficiary "has a 
sustained career or that the impact of his work has risen to the level where it places him at the very top of 
the field." Joseph, No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001 *4. See also Agrawal, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 
2879217, at *23 (concluding that a petitioner's roles at the second ranked hospital in the nation and the 
first ranked in heart care were inadequate to establish the alien's place at the top of the field or to 
demonstrate sustained national or international acclaim). 
SCOPS also determined that the Beneficiary's original contributions, while valuable to his employers and 
clients and supported by letters and funding articles, did not rise to the level of major significance in the 
field as a whole and did not show that his work was recognized as being at the level of one of that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top oftheir field. They also indicated that although his salary and 
compensation were a positive element, it did not elevate him in a manner this benefit requires. 
While the appeal contests SCOPS' analysis relating to the Beneficiary's contributions to the field under 
step one's individual criterion, they devote no time responding to the adverse final merits analysis on this 
subject. There is little doubt regarding the originality about the Beneficiary's role in establishing and 
developing a technology company that has some impact in the field. But what's not reflected in the record 
is the level at which the Beneficiary's contributions have impacted his field as a whole. That is a key 
factor in this analysis as it helps show whether he is among that small percentage who has risen to the 
very top ofthe field of endeavor and has sustained national or international acclaim at such an elevated 
level. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Even though the Petitioner's appeal brief quotes from SCOPS' final merits analysis relating to his 
remuneration, the appeal brief cites to the portion of the users Policy Manual under the individual 
criteria----or step one-and not how his achievements in this area pan out when considering the record in 
its totality. Even if we were to agree that the submitted evidence satisfies a criterion either directly or 
through comparable evidence, that has an insignificant effect in step two. For users to evaluate whether 
his compensation packages are high in relation to others in the field, that requires a comparison to others 
similarly situated at or near the top ofthe field. So even ifthe Petitioner's evidence met the criterion, the 
record does not show that the Beneficiary's "earnings were so high as to indicate that they are one of a 
small percentage at the top of the field, or that the salary is reflective of sustained national acclaim." 
Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 820. 
Finally, SCOPS noted that certain criteria, such as commercial success in the performing arts, did not 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation, and found that the Petitioner had not shown that the regulatory 
criteria were inapplicable so as to justify reliance on "comparable evidence." Ultimately, SCOPS decided 
that the Beneficiary's claims of sales failed to provide a comparison to others involved in similar pursuits 
and that the success of the petitioning company did not illustrate his sustained national or international 
8 
acclaim as one who has risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor. Setting aside that some of SCOPS' 
discussion on this topical area included analysis that belonged under the individual criterion in step one, 
it remains that the Petitioner still does not advance any rebuttal to the denial that in the final merits, the 
Beneficiary's commercially successful achievements do not place him among the top echelon in his field. 
The appeal brief' s commercial success portion is completely lacking in that area. 
When the denial decision contains a specific basis for the adverse decision, it is the Petitioner's 
responsibility to demonstrate in their appeal that SCOPS made a prejudicial error when it denied on that 
basis. The failure to make such a demonstration-as we have here-means the below decision on this 
topic will stand. And by not raising issues relating to a final merits totality analysis, as the Petitioner has 
failed to do in several topics here, they have waived those topics in this appeal. Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N 
Dec. 693, 693 n.l (BIA 2023) (citing Matter of R-A-M-, 25 I&N Dec. 657, 658 n.2 (BIA 2012) and 
finding issues that are not meaningfully challenged in the appeal are waived); Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 T&N 
Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (finding when a filing party mentions an issue without developing an 
argument, the issue is deemed waived). 
And the Petitioner cannot revive it by filing a motion on this decision as a filing party cannot cure their 
failure to preserve an argument in a previous stage of the same proceedings through a post-adjudicative 
motion. Philipp v. Stiflung Preussischer Kulturbesitz, 77 F.4th 707, 709-10 (D.C. Cir. 2023); United 
States v. Campbell, 26 F.4th 860, 875-76 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 143 S. Ct. 95,214 L. Ed. 2d 19 (2022) 
( finding that unless it would result in a miscarriage ofjustice, in most cases an issue abandoned on appeal 
should still be dismissed in any subsequent motion without addressing the merits). Instead, if they wish 
to raise those issues again, it should achieve that by filing a new petition. 
Overall, SCOPS concluded that, even considering all the evidence in the aggregate, the Petitioner had not 
demonstrated a record of sustained national or international acclaim or that the Beneficiary has risen to 
the very top of the field of technology entrepreneurship, and therefore that he did not qualify for this 
classification. 
In summary, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top oftheir respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCTS has long 
held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the 
"extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954-55 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). 
The Petitioner's evidence confirms that the Beneficiary has but one example for each of the following: 
securing funding for a startup, contributing to the field through that startup's work, judging others in the 
field, authoring scholarly work, displaying his work at an investor event, performing in a leading role for 
a distinguished organization, commanding a salary or remuneration, and enjoying commercial success. 
He further claims two memberships that were not shown to set him apart from others in the field, and 
only three published articles that briefly mentioned him. And the vast majority of these claims emanate 
from his dealings with the one organization. 
Considering the full measure of the Beneficiary's ability and achievements, the level of his national or 
international acclaim and the extent to which his achievements have been recognized in the field are not 
indicative of a record of sustained acclaim. Also, the Petitioner has not submitted extensive 
9 
documentation exhibiting the Beneficiary has attained a level of expertise placing him among that small 
percentage that has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a 
petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not 
met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
