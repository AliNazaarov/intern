Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Exceptional Ability) 
The Petitioner, a jeweler, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in the arts. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, 
as required, that the Petitioner received a major, internationally recognized award or met the 
requirements of at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If the petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he 
or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner, a native and citizen of Uzbekistan, is a jeweler who specializes in making traditional 
Uzbek jewelry. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the criteria relating to judging under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vii). We agree with SCOPS' determinations. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he 
also meets the criteria relating to membership in associations pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), 
published material about the alien pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), original contributions 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), and high salary under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). Upon de novo 
review, the Petitioner has not established that he meets at least three criteria.

The duplicative language within the support letters and their similarity to the Petitioner's own 
statement casts doubt on their authenticity. In general, when a petitioner has provided affidavits from 
different persons that contribute to the eligibility claim, but the language and structure contained 
within the affidavits is notably similar, the trier of fact may treat those similarities as a basis for 
questioning a petitioner's claims. See Singh v. Bondi, No. 23-1247, 2025 WL 828637, at *6 (9th Cir. 
Mar. 17, 2025) (citing Singh v. Garland, 118 F.4th 1150, 1161 (9th Cir. 2024)); Singh v. Garland, 20 
F.4th 1049, 1053 (6th Cir. 2021); Surinder Singh v. Board ofImmigration Appeals, 438 F.3d 145, 148 
(2d Cir. 2006); Wang v. Lynch, 824 F.3d 587, 592 (6th Cir. 2016); Dehonzai v. Holder, 650 F.3d 1, 8 
(1st Cir. 2011); Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Security, No. 19-2534, WL 2338316, at *4, n.3 
(D.D.C. June 8, 2021). When affidavits contain such similarities, it is reasonable to infer that the 
petitioner who submitted the notably similar documents is the actual source from where the suspicious 
similarities derive. Cf Mei Chai Ye v. US. Dept. ofJustice, 489 F.3d 517, 519 (2d Cir. 2007); Wang 
v. Lynch, 824 F.3d at 592. Furthermore, support letters "that merely reiterate USCIS' definitions 
relating to this classification or make general and expansive statements regarding the [petitioner] and 
[their] accomplishments are generally not persuasive." 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3).

Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
According to the Petitioner, he meets this criterion because he was mentioned in an article about the 
claimed benefits and characteristics of silver jewelry. The one-page article, titled I I I Istates that the Petitioner believes "only hand jewelery [sic] made of pure silver with 
natural stones is beneficial to its owner," that certain types of stones may impact the wearer's health 
or are more appropriate for wear by certain people, and that jewelry may "transmit energy" from the 
4 
maker. The article appeared on a website called Caravan.kz, about which the Petitioner provided 
general information including web traffic data. But this evidence does not show that Caravan.kz, a 
website in Kazakhstan, is a professional or major trade publication or other major media, nor does the 
article indicate that it relates specifically to the Petitioner's work in the field of traditional Uzbek 
jewelry rather than jewelry in general. 
The Petitioner also claims that he meets this criterion based on an interview about his background and 
work that appeared in a publication called Business & Market Newspaper. He asserts that this 
"multilingual newspaper reaches a broad audience across New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania." 
A photocopy of a portion of the relevant issue of Business & Market Newspaper and an "About Us" 
page both state that it is a "Multilingual Informative Advertisement" where businesses can advertise 
their services to Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish communities. The "About Us" page also states, "What 
you pay for - is simply space on our pages." A translation of another page of the publication states 
the editorial board "does not bear responsibility for the accuracy of the information contained in 
advertisements" and "[m]anuscripts are neither reviewed nor returned." This does not support the 
Petitioner's claim that Business & Market newspaper qualifies as a professional or major trade 
publication or other major media relating to his work, as 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires. 
Promotional or marketing materials are not generally considered to be published material about the 
person. This includes seemingly objective content about the person in major print publications that 
the person or the person's employer paid for. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). 
Additionally, the Petitioner's interview in Business & Market Newspaper occurred inl 12024, 
after SCOPS issued the RFE in August 2024, and does not establish that the Petitioner had met this 
criterion at the time of filing, as required. 8 C.F.R. §§ 103.2(b)(l), (12); Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N 
Dec. 45, 49 (Reg'l Comm'r 1971). A petition cannot be approved at a future date after the petitioner 
becomes eligible under a new set of facts. Matter ofIzummi, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 175 (Comm'r 1998). 
That decision, citing Matter ofBardouille, 18 I&N Dec. 114 (BIA 1981 ), farther provides that USCIS 
cannot "consider facts that come into being only subsequent to the filing of a petition." Id. at 176. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not met this criterion.

Evidence that the alien has commanded a high sala,y or other significantly high 
remuneration for services, in relation to others in the.field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he meets this criterion based on evidence of his salary as a 
master jeweler at a company from 2014 to 2017. He argues that his employment records and data 
about average wages in Uzbekistan show that he earned thirteen times the average salary in his field 
and region. In support of his petition, he submitted letters from his prior employer confirming his 
employment; a record of his income from the employer; exchange rates for Uzbek currency; and a 
chart of his income in equivalent U.S. dollars. However, this evidence does not provide a comparison 
between the Petitioner's salary and others in his field. He also submitted a printout from a website 
about the average salary for a person working in jewelry sales in I IUzbekistan in 2024. 
However, the Petitioner does not claim that he is in the field of jewelry sales, but instead that he 
possesses extraordinary ability as a master jeweler who specializes in making traditional Uzbek 
jewelry. The Petitioner must submit documentation of the earnings of those in his occupation 
performing similar work. While we acknowledge that a district court's decision is not binding 
precedent, we note that in Racine v. INS, 1995 WL 153319 at *4 (N.D. 111. Feb. 16, 1995), the court 
stated, "[T]he plain reading of the statute suggests that the appropriate field of comparison is not a 
comparison of Racine's ability with that of all the hockey players at all levels of play; but rather, 
Racine's ability as a professional hockey player within the NHL. This interpretation is consistent 
with ... the definition of the term 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), and the discussion set forth in the preamble 
at 56 Fed. Reg. 60898-99." 
Additionally, as SCOPS explained, average salary information for 2024 does not provide data relevant 
to the income records he provided from 2014 to 2017. Although he argues that the regulation "does 
not require exact year-to-year correspondence between the beneficiary's earnings and comparison 
data," he does not submit evidence to show that average salaries in 2024 provide a relevant comparison 
with his salary as much as a decade earlier. The Petitioner must present evidence ofobjective earnings 
data showing that they have commanded a "high salary" or "significantly high remuneration" in 
comparison with those performing similar work during the same time period. See Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering professional golfer's earnings versus other 
PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dept. ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th 
Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others 
in the field); Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's 
salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N.D. Ill. 1995) 
( comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
As supporting evidence on appeal, the Petitioner provides a certificate from the State Statistics 
Committee,! IUzbekistan, confirming that he worked as a master jeweler and earned

at least 210,332,100 Uzbek Soms per year from 2014 to 2017. An I11formation Letter from the State 
Statistics Committee indicates that the average monthly salary in Uzbekistan in 2017 was 1.5 million 
Soms, and the highest average earnings were in the fields of finance and insurance. This evidence 
relates generally to Uzbekistan as a whole and does not provide the required comparison between the 
Petitioner's salary and others in his field. An Order ofthe Ministry ofEconomy and Finance ofthe 
Republic of Uzbekistan shows that wages, pensions, scholarships, and benefits were increased in 
August 2016, although it contains a note that the order became invalid in accordance with another 
order issued in December 2017. Similarly, a 2018 order relating to average salary retained during 
annual leave contains a note that it became invalid in accordance with a resolution dated January 2025. 
Even if valid, these orders discuss general wage information in Uzbekistan rather than specifics 
relating to salaries in the Petitioner's field. Two articles about the average monthly wage in 
Uzbekistan in 2017 and 2018 similarly lack relevant evidence about wages in the Petitioner's field as 
a master of traditional jewelry making. Average and median salaries are insufficient to establish the 
Petitioner commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field. Kinuthia, 702 F. Supp. 3d at 11; 
Mussarova v. Garland, 562 F. Supp. 3d 837, 853 (C.D. Cal. 2022). Proving a salary is "above 
average" typically does not suffice to establish a "high salary" or "significantly high remuneration." 
More comprehensive wage data is needed to effectively demonstrate high earnings relative to peers. 
Based on these deficiencies in the evidence, the Petitioner has not submitted documentation that meets 
the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
