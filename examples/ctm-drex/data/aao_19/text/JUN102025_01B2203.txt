Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, asquash coach, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by apreponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de nova. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable 
evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner competed as a junior squash player in Egypt and has been working as a squash coach. 
He is seeking to be classified as an alien of extraordinary ability with the intent of continuing to work 
in the United States as a squash coach. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, the issue to be addressed on appeal is whether the Petitioner satisfied at least three 
of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). In denying the petition, SCOPS 
determined that although the Petitioner fulfilled two criteria, memberships in associations and leading 
or critical role, he did not fulfill five claimed evidentiary criteria: 
• lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards or prizes, 
• published materials about the individual in professional publications relating to work in the 
field, 
• participation as a judge of others' work, 
• original contributions of major significance, or 
• high salary or other remuneration in relation to others in the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that the evidence demonstrates he meets four of the claimed criteria. 
He does not dispute SCOPS' conclusion that he did not meet the criterion for high salary or other 
remuneration. We, therefore, deem this issue to be waived and will not address this criterion further.

the regulation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i).

7. Comparable evidence 
Where an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not "readily apply" to the 
Petitioner's occupation, he may submit "comparable evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(4). 
The Petitioner contends the criterion of awards and prizes does not easily apply to his occupation as a 
coach, submitting evidence of his students' squash player awards as comparable evidence under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). He, however, does not submit evidence to support his general assertions that 
6 
the awards or prizes criterion does not readily apply to his occupation as a coach. "A general 
unsupported assertion that the listed evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's 
occupation is not probative." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(1). 
Moreover, he claims that we should consider his students' awards and prizes as comparable evidence 
because two other criteria, display of work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases and 
commercial successes in the performing arts, do not readily apply to his coaching occupation. He, 
however, does not explain how submitting evidence ofhis students' awards or prizes are ofcomparable 
significance to these criteria. "[I]f a petitioner demonstrates that a particular criterion does not readily 
apply to the person's occupation, the petitioner may submit evidence that is ofcomparable significance 
to that criterion to establish sustained acclaim and recognition." Id. The Petitioner does not explain 
or provide evidence showing how his having been a coach of players who won awards or prizes is of 
comparable significance to display of work in the field under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) or 
commercial success in performing arts under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x). 
Lastly, he claims his being the coach of players who won awards and prizes constitutes evidence 
comparable to original contributions of major significance under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). The 
Petitioner points to the USCIS Policy Manual, which provides examples where comparable evidence 
may apply, "A person who is an Olympic coach whose athlete wins an Olympic medal while under 
the person's principal tutelage would likely constitute evidence comparable to that in 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v)." Id. 
However, the record does not include evidence demonstrating that the athletes coached by the 
Petitioner won awards or prizes under his principal tutelage, or that his contributions were of major 
significance to his field. The Petitioner submitted letters from the student athletes indicating they won 
the awards or prizes at the time that the Petitioner coached them. He did not, however, provide 
evidence to corroborate the students having won the awards or prizes, such as an official award 
certificate or letter from the indicated awarding organization, 2 or that the Petitioner was their principal 
coach. While a letter from the former head coach of thel Iindicates that the

B. Reserved Issues 
Because the Petitioner has not fulfilled the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Accordingly, we will reserve this issue. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). Nevertheless, we 
advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a finding 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
C. 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
The record reflects that in 2022 the Petitioner received 0-1 status, a classification reserved for 
nonimmigrants of extraordinary ability. Although USCIS has approved at least one 0-1 nonimmigrant 
visa petition filed on behalf of the Petitioner, this approval does not preclude USCIS from denying an 
immigrant visa petition which is adjudicated based on a different standard in statute, regulations, and 
case law. Many Form 1-140 immigrant petitions are denied after USCIS approves nonimmigrant 
petitions. See, e.g., Sun lift Int 'I v. Mayorkas, et al., 2021 WL 3111627 (N.D. Cal. 2021); Q Data 
Consulting, Inc. v. INS, 293 F. Supp. 2d 25 {D.D.C. 2003); IKEA US v. US Dept. of Justice, 48 F. 
Supp. 2d 22 (D.D.C. 1999); Fedin Bros. Co., Ltd. v. Sava, 724 F. Supp. 1103, 1108 (E.D.N.Y. 1989), 
~ff'd, 905 F. 2d 41 (2d. Cir. 1990). Furthermore, our authority over the USCIS service centers, the 
office adjudicating the nonimmigrant visa petition, is comparable to the relationship between a court 
of appeals and a district court. Even if a service center has approved a non immigrant petition on behalf 
of an individual, we are not bound to follow that finding in the adjudication of another immigration 
petition. See, e.g., La. Philharmonic Orchestra v. INS, 248 F.3d 1139 (5th Cir. 2001) (per curiam). 
Nor are we required to approve applications or petitions where eligibility has not been demonstrated 
merely because of prior approvals that may have been erroneous. See Matter of Church Scientology 
In 'I, 19 l&N Dec. 593, 597 (Comm'r 1988); see also Constr. & Design Co. v. Bureau of Citizenship 
& lmmigr. Servs., 2008 WL 2074097 *5 (N.D. 111. May 14, 2008) (describing as "prudent" that the 
AAO "assess each application on its own, rather than rubber stamping applications merely because of 
prior approvals"), aff'd sub nom. Constr. & Design Co. v. US. Citizenship & lmmigr. Servs., 563 F.3d 
593 (7th Cir. 2009). 
111. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter of Price, 20 l&N Dec. 
953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability Visi nscaia v. 
Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131 (D.D.C. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the 
extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland 
Sec. (Hamal 11), No. 19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 
1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small 
percentage of prospective immigrants"); See also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal I), No. 
19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 
1122 (upholding denial of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian 
8 
theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals 
fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d at 918 (finding that "arguably one of the 
most famous baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a '"career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(1)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has garnered national 
or international acclaim in the field, and he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(1)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
record does not contain sufficient evidence establishing the Petitioner among the upper echelon in his 
field. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
