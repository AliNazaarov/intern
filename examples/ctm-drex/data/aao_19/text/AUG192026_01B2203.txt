Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a manager partner and business unit head - healthcare payer, seeks classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( I )(A), 
8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner met the 
initial evidentiary requirements through evidence of a one-time achievement or meeting at least three 
of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant 
to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b )(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). USCIS found that the Petitioner did not meet any of the four claimed 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (v), (viii), and (ix). On appeal, the Petitioner again 
asserts that he meets the same four criteria. 1 
Under the preponderance standard, we consider not only the quantity, but also the quality of the 
evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 
at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989). We also note that it is the 
Petitioner's burden to submit evidence that sufficiently corroborates his claims. Statements made 
without supporting documentation are of limited probative value and are insufficient to satisfy the 
Petitioner's burden of proof Matter ofSoffici, 22 I&N Dec. 158, 165 (Comm'r 1998). While we may 
not address each piece of evidence individually, we have reviewed and considered each one. 
1. Receipt oflesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the 
field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
To fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must first demonstrate that he was the recipient of prizes or 
awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
Second, the award must be a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize or award, which the 
petitioner received for excellence in the field of endeavor. Id. Relevant considerations regarding 
whether the basis for granting the prizes or awards was excellence in the field include: (1) the criteria 
used to grant the awards or prizes, (2) the national or international significance of the awards or prizes 
in the field, (3) the number of awardees or prize recipients, and ( 4) limitations on competitors. Id.

Relevant evidence of the significance of an original contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work;

3. Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
In order to meet this criterion, the Petitioner must first show he has performed a leading or critical role 
for an organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For 
a leading role, we look at whether the evidence establishes that the petitioner was a leader within the 
organization or establishment or a division or department thereof. Id. For a critical role, we look at 
whether the evidence establishes that the petitioner has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. Id. 
Second, the Petitioner must demonstrate that the organization or establishment for which he held a 
leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. Id. The relevant size or longevity of an 
organization or establishment is not a determining factor but is considered together with other 
information to determine whether a distinguished reputation exists. Id. Other relevant factors for 
evaluating the reputation of an organization or establishment include the scale of its customer base or 
relevant media coverage. Id. Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary defines "distinguished" as "marked 
by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
W ebster.com, www .merriam-webster.com. 
Upon review, we withdraw USCIS' determination as to this criterion. The record reflects that the 
Petitioner served as a managing partner and business unit head, was a member of the company's 
executive leadership team, and was responsible for a business unit accounting for a substantial part of 
the company's revenue. The record therefore sufficiently demonstrates the significance of his role 
within the organization. 
The record also contains evidence regarding his employer's, I I reputation, including its 
substantial healthcare client base, work with major healthcare companies, global operations, and 
indTtry rTognition. Considered together, the evidence establishes by a preponderance of evidence 
that has a distinguished reputation. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has established that he performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization with a distinguished reputation. 
4. Evidence that that alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to other in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." USCIS 
5 
determines whether the person's salary or remuneration is high relative to the compensation paid to 
others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner asserts that he satisfies this criterion based on his compensation as a senior executive 
in the healthcare information technology industry. The record includes Form W-2 documenting his 
earnings, including approximately $498,000 in wages in 2024. The Petitioner also states that he 
received a base salary of approximately $350,000, variable compensation, and equity-based 
compensation. 
The Petitioner compares his compensation primarily to wage information for the broad occupational 
category of chief executive. The record includes information from CareerOneStop, O*N et, Monster, 
CareerBuilder, and other sources regarding compensation for chief executives. The Petitioner 
maintains that this is the appropriate comparison because he performs executive-level duties. The 
evidence does not sufficiently support this comparison. The record describes the Petitioner's position 
as involving healthcare information technology, client management, cloud infrastructure, artificial 
intelligence, automation, and business leadership. The Petitioner has not shown that the chief 
executive included in the submitted salary data performs similar work or have responsibilities 
comparable to those of the Petitioner. A broad comparison to chief executives does not establish how 
the Petitioner's compensation compares to that of similarly situated professionals in his field. 
We acknowledge that the Petitioner's compensation included equity and performance awards in 
addition to his salary. The awards may qualify as other remuneration under the regulation. However, 
the Petitioner must still show that his remuneration is significantly high compared to others in the 
field. The Petitioner's compares his total compensation, including equity and other awards, to wage 
data that reflects salary or wages. Without comparable data showing the total compensation or 
similarly situated professionals, this evidence does not establish that the Petitioner's remuneration is 
significantly high in relation to others in the field. See, e.g., Onaghise, 2022 WL 4073344, at *7 
(upholding petitioner did not provide information about what other compensation or renumeration, 
such as living allowances, stock options, incentives, or bonuses, in relation to what others have 
earned); Kinuthia, 2018 WL 1243955, at *6, aff'd, 2019 WL 11671989, cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 2643 
(upholding the petitioner's receipt of supplemental pay did not establish that his salary was high in 
relation to others in the field because the supplemental pay was reserved for those with "Engineering 
Related Classifications"). 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not demonstrate he fulfills this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability Visinscaia v. 
Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 
19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), ajf'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 
31, 2023) ( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective 
6 
immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't of Homeland Sec. (Hamal /), No. l 9-cv-2534, 2020 WL 
2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1122 (upholding denial 
of petition of a published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) 
( stating that "[ c ]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this 
designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the 
most famous baseball players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(l)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate the Petitioner has garnered national 
or international acclaim in the field, and he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
record does not contain sufficient evidence establishing the Petitioner among the upper echelon in his 
field. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-mentioned reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
