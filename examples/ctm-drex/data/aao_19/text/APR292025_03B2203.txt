Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a chemical engineer in the nondestructive testing (NDT) field. He seeks classification 
as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not 
establish that he satisfied at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria. We dismissed a subsequent 
appeal. The matter is now before us on a combined motion to reopen and reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
First, we will address the Petitioner's motion to reopen which must state new facts and be supported 
by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(2). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(l)(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. See Matter ofCoelho, 20 I&N Dec. 464, 473 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence have 
the potential to change the outcome). 
On motion, the Petitioner disputes our decision to dismiss the appeal and states that "no new evidence 
is presented." Therefore, the Petitioner has not satisfied the requirements of a motion to reopen. 
Next, we will address the Petitioner's motion to reconsider, which must establish that our prior 
decision was based on an incorrect application of law or policy and that the decision was incorrect 
based on the evidence in the record ofproceedings at the time ofthe decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). 
Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the latest decision 
in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these 
requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner contests our prior decision arguing that we incorrectly applied the law and 
policy and did not properly consider the evidence, thereby resulting in an incorrect conclusion on the 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (v), and (viii), which served as the basis for dismissing the appeal. 
The Petitioner also challenges our decision to reserve a fourth criterion. 1 
First, the Petitioner challenges our conclusion regarding the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), 
which requires evidence of the Petitioner's membership in associations in his field that require 
outstanding achievements of its members as determined by experts in the field. 
In dismissing the appeal, we agreed with the denial's conclusion that the Petitioner's highest level of 
certification, which he was awarded by a technical society for non-destructive testing (NDT) 
professionals, did not constitute membership in that society. Although we acknowledged the 
Petitioner's submission of materials about the society, we noted that the materials discussed 
certification by, but not membership in the society, and did not establish that the society requires 
outstanding achievements as judged by national or international experts in their fields. We also 
pointed out that the biographical information on record about members of the society's board of 
managers did not establish that the board members have been recognized as experts or that they judge 
the achievements of others. 
On motion, the Petitioner argues that we "overlooked substantial evidence" showing that the society's 
certification "involves recognition by national and international experts"; the Petitioner states that 
"made a critical error by disregarding" the involvement of expert evaluation in the society's 
certification process. We disagree with these contentions which do not address the core of our 
conclusion on this criterion, mainly that the Petitioner did not establish that he is a member of an 
association in his field when he submitted evidence that he obtained certification that was awarded by 
an organization in that field. As discussed, obtaining certification, regardless of the level, is not 
synonymous with membership in the society that awarded that certification. The Petitioner's 
contention that we neglected to consider "the cross-disciplinary nature" of the NDT field does not 
address the cited evidentiary deficiency, which is that the Petitioner did not establish that he has 
membership in an association in his field. As such, the Petitioner has not established that we erred in 
applying the law or USCIS policy regarding this criterion. 
Next, the Petitioner challenges our conclusion regarding the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), 
which requires evidence of the Petitioner's original contributions of major significance in the field. 
Although we acknowledged the Petitioner's claims that his NDT methodologies have been broadly 
recognized and used in industries that rely on NDT technologies, we determined that the record lacked 
sufficient evidence to support these claims. We noted that the Petitioner neither named organizations 
that have adopted his techniques nor identified technical papers or best practices that cite his 
methodologies. We noted that financial benefits to the Petitioner's contractors and their clients do 
not automatically indicate that his contributions have had major significance in his field. In sum, we 
concluded that the Petitioner did not: 1) provide independent evidence of his claimed "technical

On motion, the Petitioner asserts that we did not properly assess the originality of his work, claiming 
that he submitted "substantial evidence" that detailed his "expertise in radiographic testing (RT) and 
phased array ultrasonic testing (PAUT)." The Petitioner points to previously submitted letters from 
and "independent industry sources," claiming that they ----------------~ serve as evidence that he "introduced innovative technologies and enhanced existing methodologies." 
However, the Petitioner does not specify the "independent industry sources" and contrary to his 
assertion, the letter from the engineering and construction firm contains only broad references to the 
Petitioner's "advanced methods," "expertise" in RT, and "exceptional proficiency" in PAUT, but it 
does not describe the Petitioner's methods to convey an understanding of how they are advanced; nor 
does that letter identify the Petitioner's original contribution or meaningfully discuss the originality of 
his "unique skill set" with respect to NDT methodologies. Further, the Petitioner asserts that the letter 
from engineering and construction firm describes his "critical contribution," which does not accurately 
convey this criterion's requirement that the contribution must be original. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. 
The Petitioner also challenges our reliance on Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 
2013). He asserts that unlike the petitioner in the cited case, he has provided "detailed and credible 
documentation from independent sources that confirm the originality and significance of his work." 
We disagree. Aside from making these broad references, the Petitioner has not adequately addressed 
our evidentiary analysis where we discussed the content of previously submitted recommendation 
letters from three companies that contracted the Petitioner's NDT services. Importantly, aside from 
disagreeing with our conclusion, the Petitioner does not identify specific errors in our assessment of 
the evidence, nor does he clarify his reference to "independent sources" or elaborate on the "detailed 
and credible documentation" that he believes supports his claims regarding this criterion. The 
Petitioner also does not specifically discuss the broadly referenced "contractors and experts" who he 
claims have explained how his innovations have "influenced the best practices and elevated the 
technical capabilities ofNDT professionals throughout the region." 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not established that we erred in applying the law or USCIS policy 
regarding this criterion. 
Lastly, the Petitioner challenges our conclusion regarding the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), 
which requires evidence of the Petitioner's performance in a leading or critical role in an organization 
with a distinguished reputation. The Petitioner's claim regarding this criterion is based primarily on 
the previously mentioned recommendation letters from companies that contracted the Petitioner to 
perform NDT services and state that the Petitioner performed in a critical role within their respective 
organizations. We discussed the statements made in each letter and concluded that they lacked 
sufficient detail to show that the Petitioner's contributions were of "significant importance to the 
outcome of the organization or establishment's activities." See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. 
First, regarding the letter from the engineering and management services company, the Petitioner 
asserts that we were disputing "the lack of precise documentation regarding total revenue or the 
3 
percentage attributed to the [P]etitioner's contributions." Contrary to this assertion, we pointed out 
that the letter in question did not specify how much total revenue the company generated and therefore 
it did not adequately corroborate the Petitioner's claim that his clients accounted for about 74% of the 
contractor's revenues. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 376 (AAO 2010) (stating that a 
petitioner's assertions must be supported with relevant, probative, and credible evidence). 
We then addressed the letter from the boiler rehabilitation contractor. On motion, the Petitioner asserts 
that we "erroneously discount[ ed] the evidence by requiring numerical detail." However, the 
Petitioner's assessment of our analysis in incorrect as we made no mention of "numerical detail." We 
pointed out that while the letter referred to the Petitioner's role in enhancing operational efficiency 
and reducing "shutdown time" needed for boiler maintenance, it lacked sufficient detail and therefore 
did not establish that the Petitioner's work was of significant importance to the company's activities. 
In other words, we did not highlight the absence of "numerical detail" and focused on the general lack 
of detail about the Petitioner's specific contributions towards reductions in shutdown time or other 
efficiency improvements. 
Finally, we addressed a letter discussing the Petitioner's role in a large oil refinery project in Egypt 
and found that the Petitioner provided sufficient evidence to establish that the heavy construction and 
engineering contractor that was responsible for the project had a distinguished reputation. We 
determined, however, that the contractor did not discuss the project's importance to the company's 
overall activities and therefore did not establish that the Petitioner's work was of significant 
importance to the company. On motion, the Petitioner highlights the amount of foreign investment 
funds that the contractor claimed were prompted by the oil refinery project. However, because the 
Petitioner does not address the key issue of the project's significance to the contractor's overall 
activities, he has not established that he played a critical role in the organization. 
The Petitioner also contends that we improperly dismissed the distinguished reputation of the other 
contractors, finding only the heavy construction and engineering contractor to have satisfied this 
portion of the criterion. However, because we determined that the Petitioner did not establish that he 
assumed a leading or critical role within those contractors' respective organizations and the Petitioner 
has not established that we incorrectly made that determination, he has not satisfied this criterion, and 
we need not address the issue of whether these contractors had distinguished reputations. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
In sum, the Petitioner has not established that we erred in applying the law or USCIS policy regarding 
the three criteria discussed above. Accordingly, we properly determined that there was no need to 
address a fourth criterion, given that the Petitioner would not meet three of the criteria as required for 
a final merits determination. Therefore, while the Petitioner challenges our decision to reserve any 
discussion of the fourth criterion, he has not established that our approach was inconsistent with law 
or USCIS policy. 
As stated earlier, the Petitioner has not offered new facts or submitted additional evidence in support 
of the motion to reopen. On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not established that our previous 
decision was based on an incorrect application of law or policy at the time we issued our decision. 
Therefore, the motion will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4).
