Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a research scientist in the field of pharmaceutical science, seeks classification as an 
alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 
U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available 
to aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their fields through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three ofthe ten initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before 
us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS' failure to 
properly review and analyze his evidence in accordance with the USCIS policy amounts to an abuse 
of discretion and that SCOPS deemed his evidence insufficient by deploying a standard of proof that 
well exceeds the preponderance ofthe evidence. He maintains that the evidence supports his eligibility 
for the EB-1 classification. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) ("EB-1 
petitions, nicknamed Einstein visas, are coveted visas reserved for only the most talented noncitizens 
with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
sustained acclaim and recognition of the beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement, that is, a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit this 
evidence, then they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items, such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence 
to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two criteria relating to judging and 
scholarly articles but did not satisfy the remaining criterion claimed relating to contributions. After 
reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility 
for this highly restrictive immigrant classification by a preponderance of the evidence. While we may 
not address each piece of evidence individually, we have reviewed and considered each one. 
1. "Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work 
of others in the same or an allied field of specialization for which classification is sought." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
To fulfill this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that he has acted as a judge of the work of 
others in the same or an allied field of specification. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner established eligibility 
for this criterion, and we agree. The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has reviewed manuscripts

for scientific journals, thereby establishing that he meets the plain language of the regulation at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). Therefore, the Petitioner meets this criterion. 
2. "Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
To satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must first establish that he has authored scholarly articles in the 
field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate that the publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade publication, or 
major media publication. Id. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner established eligibility for this 
criterion, and we agree. The Petitioner submitted copies ofthe first few pages of scholarly articles co­
authored by him and published in professional publications, thereby establishing that he meets the 
plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). Accordingly, he satisfies this criterion. 
3. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes major, 
significant contributions to the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
Evidence that the person's work was funded, patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating 
the work's originality, will not necessarily establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance 
to the field. Id. For example, published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its 
importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative 
to others' work in the field, may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the 
field of endeavor. Id. Similarly, evidence that the person developed a patented technology that has 
attracted significant attention or commercialization may establish the significance of the person's 
original contribution to the field. Id. Detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature 
and significance ofthe person's contributions may provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed 
original contributions of major significance, particularly when the record includes documentation 
corroborating the claimed significance. Id.

The Petitioner's statement and letters from his employers describe the Petitioner's job responsibilities 
as a research scientist for his current employer and as a formulation and validation associate for his 
former employer and his research projects and explain the importance of the projects and the 
Petitioner's contributions to his employers. However, they do not sufficiently demonstrate that the 
Petitioner's work constitutes major, significant contributions to the field of pharmaceutical science. 
The Petitioner must establish that his original contributions have been "of major significance in the 
field." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). The evidence does not rise to the level of original scientific 
contributions "of major significance in the field." Recognition received from his employers for his 
contributions to their investigative drug programs or other research projects does not necessarily 
equate to major significance in the field. 
The Petitioner's statement and a letter from a professor ad Iin India indicate 
that the Petitioner and the professor have worked on a patent to create a novel technique. But the 
record lacks sufficient evidence showing that the technique has attracted significant attention or 
commercialization. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Courts have routinely 
affirmed our decisions concluding that 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) "requires substantial influence 
beyond one's employers, clients, or customers." Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 
(S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019) (upholding an agency decision that held "[a] patent is not necessarily 
evidence of a track record of success with some degree of influence over the field as a whole."); see 
also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding agency decision that held 
evidence insufficient "because it did not show widespread replication of [the petitioner's invention]"). 
The significance of innovation must be determined on a case-by-case basis. Here, neither the 
Petitioner nor the professor indicates that the patent has been granted or that they have licensed or 
marketed their patented technology, if the patent has been granted. Thus, the Petitioner has not 
sufficiently established the impact of the novel technique. 
The record also includes copies of articles co-authored by the Petitioner and his citation record. The 
Petitioner's field, like most science, is research-driven, and scientists are unlikely to publish research 
that does not add to the general pool of knowledge in the field. According to the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v), an alien's contributions must be not only original but of major significance. For 
example, published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others 
working in the field may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of 
endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Otherwise, the impact of the 
petitioner's work is difficult to gauge. Cf Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d 126, 134-35 (D.D.C. 2013) 
(upholding a finding that a ballroom dancer had not met this criterion because she did not demonstrate 
her impact in the field as a whole). Publications and presentations are not sufficient evidence under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they were of "major significance." Kazarian v. USCIS, 
580 F.3d 1030, 1036 (9th Cir. 2009) aff'd in part 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). In 2010, the Kazarian 
court reaffirmed its holding that the AAO did not abuse its discretion in finding that the alien had not 
demonstrated contributions of major significance. 596 F.3d at 1122. 
Regarding the Petitioner's citation record, while the number of total citations is a factor, it is not the 
only factor to be considered in determining the Petitioner's eligibility for this criterion. Citations 
demonstrate that the field has taken some interest in the Petitioner's work. However, they are not an 
automatic indicator that the Petitioner's work has been of major significance in the field. In this case, 
the number of the Petitioner's citations is not reflective that his work has been "of major significance 
4 
in the field." Furthermore, the Petitioner did not submit sufficient documentary evidence 
demonstrating that his articles have been unusually influential. The Petitioner's documentary evidence 
is not reflective of having a significant impact on the field. Submitting documentation reflecting that 
the Petitioner's work has been cited by others in their published material is insufficient to establish 
eligibility for this criterion without documentary evidence reflecting that his work has been "of major 
significance in the field." The Petitioner did not sufficiently establish how those findings or citations 
of his work by others have significantly contributed to his field as a whole. See 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The support letters indicate that the Petitioner has presented his research findings at a conference held 
by the American Association of Pharmaceutical Scientists. The Petitioner also submitted email 
messages showing that he was invited to speak at conferences and contribute to articles and 
newsletters. Many professional fields regularly hold meetings and symposia to present new work, 
discuss new findings, and to network with other professionals. These conferences are promoted and 
sponsored by professional associations, businesses, educational institutions, and government agencies. 
Participation in such events, however, does not equate to original contributions of major significance 
in the field. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1115 (stating that publications and presentations are 
not sufficient evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) absent evidence that they were of "major 
significance"). The record does not include sufficient documentary evidence showing that any of the 
Petitioner's specific conference presentations are frequently cited by other researchers, have 
significantly impacted the field, or otherwise rise to the level of contributions "of major significance 
in the field." See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). While presentation ofthe Petitioner's work demonstrates 
that his findings were shared with others and may be acknowledged as original contributions based on 
their selection for presentation, he has not established the impact or influence ofhis presentations once 
disseminated in the field. 
The Petitioner submitted support letters from experts in the field. The opinions of the Petitioner's 
references are not without weight and have been duly considered. We may, in our discretion, use as 
advisory opinions statements submitted as expert testimony. See Matter of Caron International, 19 
I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988). However, we are ultimately responsible for making the final 
determination regarding an alien's eligibility for the benefit sought. Id. The submission ofreference 
letters supporting the petition is not presumptive evidence of eligibility; we may evaluate the content 
of those letters as to whether they support the alien's eligibility. See id. at 795-796; see also Matter 
of V-K-, 24 I&N Dec. 500, n.2 (BIA 2008) (noting that expert opinion testimony does not purport to 
be evidence as to "fact"). Thus, the content of the references' statements and how they became aware 
ofthe petitioner's reputation are important considerations. Even when written by independent experts, 
letters solicited by an alien in support of an immigration petition are of less weight than preexisting, 
independent evidence that one would expect of a scientist who has made original contributions of 
major significance in the field. Cf Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d at 135 (concluding that USCIS' 
decision to give little weight to uncorroborated assertions from professionals in the field was not arbitrary 
and capricious).

204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The Petitioner contends that SCOPS failed to properly review and analyze his evidence and applied a 
standard of proof that far exceeds the preponderance of the evidence standard. Under the 
preponderance standard, we consider not only the quantity, but also the quality of the evidence, 
including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; 
see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 1989). When USCIS provides a reasoned 
consideration to the petition and has made adequate findings, it will not be required to specifically 
address each claim the petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it to address every piece of evidence 
the petitioner presents. Guaman-Loja v. Holder, 707 F.3d 119, 123 (1st Cir. 2013) (citing Martinez v. 
INS, 970 F.2d 973, 976 (1st Cir. 1992); see also Kazemzadeh v. US. Atty. Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 
(11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 ( 4th Cir. 1993). Here, although SCOPS did 
not address every piece of evidence the Petitioner presented, the record reflects that SCOPS 
acknowledged and analyzed various documents on record, provided a reasoned consideration to the 
petition under the preponderance of the evidence standard, and has made adequate findings. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a major one-time achievement 
or documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we do not need to provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCIS has long held that 
even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary 
ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or 
international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(l)(A) 
of the Act. The record does not sufficiently demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or 
international acclaim in the field and that he is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons we have discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated by a preponderance of 
the evidence his eligibility for the EB-1 classification. The appeal will be dismissed for the above­
stated reasons, with each considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
