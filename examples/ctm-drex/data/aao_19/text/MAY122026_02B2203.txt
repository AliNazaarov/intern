Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a business manager and consultant, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements for this classification through evidence of a 
one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or by meeting at least three of the 
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a business manager and consultant who has operated his own businesses for several 
years in Brazil, providing administrative support and financial and tax consulting services. The 
evidence also indicates that he has been an advocate for patient blood management (PBM), an 
approach to blood transfusion said to improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs. He states 
that he will provide business and management consulting services in the United States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not meet any of the six 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) that he claimed to meet. On appeal, the Petitioner 
asserts that he meets the same six evidentiary criteria that he initially claimed. After reviewing all of 
the evidence in the record, we conclude that the Petitioner has not satisfied the initial evidence 
requirement for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.

3. Performed in a Leading or Critical Role for Organizations with a Distinguished Reputation 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence of a leading role should 
show that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may 
help top establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof, has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual.

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A

or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field.

We first note that this criterion seeks evidence of either "high salary" or "significantly high 
remuneration" relative to others in the field. So when a petitioner submits evidence relating to 
compensation, it must also include comparative wage or remuneration data showing that they received 
either a high salary or significantly high remuneration. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l). Second, that comparative evidence must provide an appropriate comparison to their salary 
or remuneration in terms of position, location, and other relevant factors. Id.; see also Xia v. Miller, 
No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation 
adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding that a petitioner's 
burden under this criterion includes the need to explain how the comparative salary evidence presented 
provides "a relevant, appropriate, and fair comparison"). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the salary survey he submitted, which does not differentiate 
between chief executives based on industry or company size, is an appropriate basis for comparison 
to his salary earned as the owner of a small consulting business with the discretion to set salaries and 
distribute earnings. In addition, since the survey does not consider bonuses earned by chief executives, 
which it acknowledges are common and can be substantial for senior executives, it does not provide 
sufficient information for comparison to the Petitioner's total remuneration. Accordingly, the record 
does not establish that the Petitioner's salary can be considered to be high or his total remuneration to 
be significantly high, and we conclude that he does not meet this criterion. 
5. Other Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner also asserts that he meets the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (v), 
relating to his participation as a judge of the work of others and his original contributions of major 
significance to the field, respectively. But as the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary 
requirement by meeting at least three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the identified bases for 
denial discussed above are dispositive of the appeal. We therefore reserve the issue of whether the 
Petitioner meets these additional criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien ofextraordinary ability and need not further analyze 
the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. 
Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, 
evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we 
decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25.
