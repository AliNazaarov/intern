Appeal of Texas Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a commercial photographer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Director ofthe Texas Service Center denied the petition for abandonment, and reopened the matter 
because that decision was in error. The Director then denied the petition on the merits, concluding 
that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied the initial evidentiary requirements, it 
did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is 
an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. The matter is now before us on appeal 
under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner began working as a photographer in Brazil in 1985, establishing his own studio there in 
2000 and working in freelance photojournalism since 2014. The Petitioner has spent much ofhis time in 
the United States since 2017, in I nonimmigrant status as a representative of the foreign press, operating 
a photography studio in Florida. His most recent arrival was in May 2020. He operates a Florida business 
that offers commercial photography, video production, and related services. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claims to have satisfied six of these criteria, summarized below: 
• (ii), Membership in associations that require outstanding achievements; 
• (iii), Published material about the alien in professional or major media; 
• (v), Original contributions of major significance; 
• (vii), Display at artistic exhibitions or showcases; 
• (viii), Leading or critical role for distinguished organizations or establishments; and 
• (x), Commercial success in the performing arts. 
The Director concluded that the Petitioner met three of the criteria, pertaining to memberships, 
published material, and display. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he also meets the three other 
claimed criteria. 
As discussed below, we disagree with the Director's conclusions regarding memberships, published 
material, and display. We will also explain why we conclude that the Petitioner has not satisfied other 
claimed eligibility criteria.

associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields.

The person and the person's work need not be the only subject of the material; published material that 
covers a broader topic but includes a substantial discussion ofthe person's work in the field and mentions 
the person in connection to the work may be considered material about the person relating to the person's 
work. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. In this 
instance, the Petitioner has not established that any qualifying publication carried substantial discussion 
of the Petitioner's work. The Petitioner's name in a photographer's credit is not substantial discussion. 
Marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the person's products or promoting the person's 
services are not generally considered to be published material about the person. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). Most ofthe materials the Petitioner has submitted under this criterion 
have been promotional in nature, in the context of publications whose purpose appears to be helping 
couples to locate and choose wedding photographers. 
The Petitioner has not shown that he and his work have been the subjects of qualifying media coverage. 
3. Artistic Display 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) calls for evidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. 
The Director concluded that the Petitioner had satisfied this criterion, but the record does not establish 
that the Petitioner's work was displayed at artistic exhibitions or showcases as the regulation requires. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Instead, the Petitioner has documented 
commercial exhibition of his work at trade shows. 
4 
The Petitioner also stated that clients in various industries have used his photographs in their advertising 
campaigns and for virtual tours on Google Street View. The Petitioner did not explain how, or establish 
that, these commercial applications of his work amount to artistic exhibitions or showcases. Google 
shows these photographs not for artistic purposes, or to draw attention to the Petitioner's work as a 
photographer, but to show images ofthe business establishments depicted in the photographs and thereby 
drive commercial traffic to those businesses. 
The Petitioner has not met the burden of proof regarding the requirements of this criterion. 
We now turn to the remaining criteria that the Petitioner claimed, but which the Director did not grant. 
4. Leading or Critical Role 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation. 
The Petitioner submitted letters from commercial clients in Brazil and South Florida, stating that the 
Petitioner's work has helped them to promote their businesses. The clients include a swimming pool 
construction and service company, a chemical manufacturer, a hamburger restaurant, a film festival in
I khat exclusively shows Brazilian films, and other businesses. The Petitioner did not submit 
objective, documentary evidence to show that these clients have distinguished reputations. The entities' 
own promotional materials and press releases lack sufficient weight. See Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06-
5105 SJO, 2007 WL 9229758, at *7 (C.D. Cal. July 6, 2007) aff'd 317 F. App'x. 680 (9th Cir. 2009) 
(stating that the AAO "should not rely" on self-serving assertions on the cover of a magazine as to the 
magazine's status as major media). 
The letters credit the Petitioner with helping the companies to grow, but they provide few details regarding 
these general claims and the extent of the Petitioner's effect on the businesses. The observation that the 
Petitioner's photography has been helpful to the Petitioner's clients is not sufficient to show that the 
Petitioner's roles have been critical. To establish a critical role, a petitioner must show that the person 
has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities. Letters describing the role should contain detailed and probative information 
that specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization or establishment was or critical. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The general letters from local businesses do not 
rise to this standard. 
The Petitioner, on appeal, asserts that his work produced "tangible results and measurable outcomes," but 
when elaborating on this point, the Petitioner offers only vague assertions. For example, the Petitioner 
states that his work for the film festival "gained significant traction in American and Brazilian media 
outlets," and his work for a realtor "directly impacted the effectiveness of property listings, enhancing the 
organization's overall success." 
We agree with the Director that the Petitioner has not met the criterion's regulatory requirements.

5. Commercial Success 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) calls for evidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales. 
The Petitioner asserted that "he achieved a commercial success in his field" because his photographs 
appeared "on the cover of newspapers, magazines, and websites," and his "images have been utilized by 
Google Maps ... boasting over seven (7) million views ... drawing attention to his acclaimed work as a 
Photographer and helping to build his renowned reputation." 
The wording of the regulatory criterion requires documentation of box office receipts or sales of 
recordings to establish commercial success in the performing arts. The Petitioner, on appeal, contends 
that "viewership numbers serve as modem equivalents of traditional box office receipts or sales, 
demonstrating widespread public consumption of his artistic work." The Petitioner, however, did not 
establish that the use of his photographs in Google Street View and related applications amounts to 
commercial success, involving monetary transactions akin to the sale of tickets or recordings. 
The Petitioner has not shown that users visit Google Street View in order to appreciate his photographs 
as art. Rather, the Petitioner's panoramic photographs show the interiors of shops, warehouses, and other 
business establishments, and serve a utilitarian, promotional purpose. 
Even ifwe were to disregard the regulatory reference to "sales" and the "performing arts" in favor of the 
Petitioner's proposed standard of "public consumption," the evidence would still have to show that the 
volume of that consumption reflects commercial success relative to others involved in similar pursuits. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1 ). The Petitioner has not provided any basis for 
comparison to show that his photographs on Google consistently receive substantially more views than 
those of other photographers. 
The Petitioner's work has been used commercially, but the Petitioner has not established commercial 
success in this respect. The Petitioner submitted copies of his studio's tax returns, indicating gross 
receipts of $66,919 and net profit of $26,184 in 2021 and gross receipts of $78,209 and net profit of 
$29,346 in 2022. The Petitioner did not provide any basis for comparison to show that this level ofincome 
and business activity reflects commercial success relative to others in his field. 
We agree with the Director that the Petitioner has not satisfied the regulatory requirements ofthe criterion. 
In light of the above conclusions, the Petitioner does not meet the initial evidentiary requirement of 
three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Detailed discussion of the remaining claimed criterion at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), concerning original contributions of major significance, cannot change the 
outcome of this appeal. Therefore, we reserve this issue. 1

B. Final Merits Determination 
Because the Director concluded that the Petitioner submitted the required initial evidence, the Director 
evaluated whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, sustained 
national or international acclaim and that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field 
of endeavor, whose achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. 
Although we disagree with the underlying determination that the Petitioner had satisfied the required 
criteria, nevertheless we will discuss the final merits determination here. 
A final merits determination entails analyzing a petitioner's accomplishments and weighing the 
totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 2 In this matter, we agree with the Director 
that the Petitioner has not established eligibility. 
On appeal, the Petitioner states that the Director "erred in not continuing with the Final Merits 
Evaluation." But the record shows that the Director did undertake a final merits determination, resting 
on the following conclusions: 
• Letters from clients do not establish the significance ofthe Petitioner's photographs. The Director 
stated: 'The testimonials do not demonstrate that the petitioner's work provoked widespread 
public commentary, nor do they show that experts throughout the field applied and implemented 
the petitioner's art ofphotographer." 
• The Petitioner had not submitted "preexisting, independent, and objective evidence of major 
contributions that would be expected of one whose original work has contributed to the field in a 
major and significant way." 
• The record does not include "extensive documentation" of "sustained national or international 
acclaim," as section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) ofthe Act requires. 
• The Petitioner has documented practical applications of his work that have benefited his 
customers, but he has not shown that he has reached the top of his field and achieved sustained 
national or international acclaim. 
• The Petitioner has documented the publication of some of his photographs, but has not shown a 
publication record that reflects sustained acclaim at the top ofthe field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he "has published over 1,000 photos, collectively garnering more 
than 12 MILLION views in a well-recognized platform, a strong indication of his prominence in his 
field." These figures relate to Google's use of his photos in virtual tours on Google Street View.

Screen captures from Google Street View show credits naming the Petitioner as the photographer who 
took the images, but the burden is on the Petitioner to show that these credited images have resulted in 
sustained acclaim. The Petitioner has not established that the use of his images on Google is, itself: 
evidence of such acclaim. The purpose ofthe images is not to display the work of the photographer, but 
to promote the businesses shown in the photographs. These photographs may have helped to increase 
traffic to those businesses, but the Petitioner's involvement in this promotional strategy does not 
inherently demonstrate that the Petitioner is at the very top of the field of commercial photography. The 
Petitioner has not established that his uploading of images to Google distinguishes him from other 
commercial photographers, or that his photographs on Google have received more attention than images 
uploaded by other commercial photographers. 
The Petitioner emphasizes that his Google photos have been viewed millions of times. He had posted 
thousands of photographs to that platform, thus averaging a few thousand views per photograph. The 
raw number of views does not establish acclaim or recognition as a photographer. The Petitioner has 
not shown that the view count results from his reputation or skill as a photographer, rather than interest 
in the subject matter shown in those photographs. Also, the Petitioner has provided no context to show 
the significance of that number of views. For instance, the Petitioner submits no evidence to show 
that his view count per photograph is higher than that ofmost other photographers who have uploaded 
photographs to the same platform. 
Client satisfaction establishes a measure of success in the Petitioner's field, but it cannot suffice for 
the Petitioner to show that his work has benefited his customers. The statute and regulations require 
the Petitioner to submit extensive documentation showing that his work has earned him sustained 
acclaim and recognition not only among local businesses but at a national or international level. The 
Petitioner has not met this substantial burden. 
The Petitioner has documented a viable career as a professional photographer, attracting local clients 
first itj land later in Florida. But we agree with the Director that the Petitioner has not 
submitted objective, documentary evidence that would distinguish him from others in his field to an 
extent that we could conclude that he is nationally or internationally acclaimed and at the very top of 
his field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The record does not show that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for 
the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 
19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 
31, 2023) ( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective 
immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't of Homeland Sec. (Hamal I), No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 
8 
2934954, at *1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) (citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (stating that "[c]ourts have found 
that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 
2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean 
history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree ofrecognition ofhis work that rises to the level ofsustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section203(b)(l)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one ofthe small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
