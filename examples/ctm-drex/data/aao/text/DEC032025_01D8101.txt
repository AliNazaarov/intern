The Petitioner, an individual who intends to represent the Beneficiary as his agent, 1 seeks to classify 
the Beneficiary as an 0-1 nonimmigrant, to work as an actor and a production crew member in the United 
States. The 0-1 nonimrnigrant visa classification is available to aliens who can demonstrate a record of 
extraordinary achievement in motion picture and television productions, and whose achievements have 
been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the 
Act) Section 10l(a)(l5)(O)(i), 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(l5)(O)(i). 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy, as required, the evidentiary 
criteria applicable to individuals of extraordinary achievement in the motion picture or television 
industry: nomination for or receipt of significant national or international prizes or awards, or 
submission of evidence meeting at least three of six possible forms of documentation. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(v)(A)-(B). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 10l(a)(15)(O)(i) of the Act establishes 0-1 classification for an alien who has, with regard to 
motion picture and television productions, a demonstrated record ofextraordinary achievements that have 
been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, and who seeks to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary achievement. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 214.2(o)(3)(ii) 
explains that "[ e ]xtraordinary achievement with respect to motion picture and television productions, as 
commonly defined in the industry, means a very high level of accomplishment in the motion picture or 
television industry evidenced by a degree of skill and recognition significantly above that ordinarily 
encountered to the extent that the person is recognized as outstanding, notable, or leading in the motion 
picture or television field." 
1 In an April 2024 letter, the Petitioner indicates that the Beneficiary is her grandson. 
The relevant regulations set forth the initial evidentiary criteria for establishing an alien's record of 
extraordinary achievement. First, a petitioner can submit evidence ofthe beneficiary's nomination for, 
or receipt of: significant national or international awards or prizes in the field, such as an Academy Award, 
an Emmy, a Grammy, or a Director's Guild Award. 8 e.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(v)(A). Ifthe petitioner does 
not offer this type of evidence, then he or she must submit sufficient qualifying materials that satisfy 
at least three of the six forms of documentation listed under 8 e.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(v)(B)(l)-(6). 
The submission of documents satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria does not, in and of itself, 
establish eligibility for 0-1 classification. See 59 Fed. Reg. 41818 (Aug. 15, 1994) (stating that "[t]he 
evidence submitted by the petitioner is not the standard for the classification, but merely the 
mechanism to establish whether the standard has been met"). Accordingly, where a petitioner 
provides qualifying evidence satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria, we will determine whether the 
totality of the record and the quality of the evidence establish eligibility for 0-1 classification under 
Section 101(a)(l5)(O)(i) of the Act and 8 e.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii), (v). 
In addition, the US. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Policy Manual explains that an 
alien could be classified as an 0-1 nonimmigrant ifhe or she is: 
(1) An alien of extraordinary ability in the sciences, education, business, or athletics 
( commonly referred to as O-lA), 
(2) An alien ofextraordinary ability in the arts (commonly referred to as O-lB (Arts)), 
or 
(3) An alien of extraordinary achievement in motion picture and television productions 
(commonly referred to as O-lB (MPTV))

While the requirements for the O-lB (Arts) classification and the O-lB (MPTV) classification share 
some similarities, they are in fact distinct. As relating to the O-lB (Arts) classification, the petitioner 
must demonstrate that the beneficiary has extraordinary ability in the arts. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2( o )(3)(iv). The regulation defines "extraordinary ability in the arts" to mean "distinction" and 
defines "distinction" to mean "a high level of achievement in the field of arts evidenced by a degree 
of skill and recognition substantially above that ordinarily encountered to the extent that a person 
described as prominent is renowned, leading, or well-known in the field of arts." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2( o )(3)(ii). Additionally, the petitioner may offer comparable evidence to show eligibility for 
the O-lB (Arts) classification. See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C). 
On the other hand, as relating to the O-lB (MPTV) classification, the petitioner must demonstrate that 
the beneficiary has extraordinary achievement in the motion picture or television industry. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(v). The regulation states: "extraordinary achievement with respect to 
motion picture and television productions, as commonly defined in the industry, means a very high 
level of accomplishment in the motion picture or television industry evidenced by a degree of skill 
and recognition significantly above that ordinarily encountered to the extent that the person is 
recognized as outstanding, notable, or leading in the motion picture or television field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2( o )(3)(ii). Additionally, the petitioner may not present comparable evidence to show eligibility 
for the O-lB (MPTV) classification. See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2( o )(3)(v). Indeed, as discussed in the USCIS 
Policy Manual, the difference in requirements between the O-lB (Arts) classification and the O-lB 
(MPTV) classification "reflects USCIS' longstanding practice, and is consistent with the statute,

In the decision denying the petition, SCOPS discussed the evidence in the record, concluding that the 
Petitioner failed to satisfy the requirements under 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(v) and failed to establish 
eligibility to classify the Beneficiary as an alien of extraordinary achievement in motion picture and 
television productions (O-lB (MPTV)). See also 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii), (5)(iii). On appeal, the 
Petitioner does not specifically challenge or discuss SCOPS's determination on the O-lB (MPTV) 
classification. As such, she has waived this ground of ineligibility and has not established eligibility 
for O-lB (MPTV) classification. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. at 336 n.5. Instead, on appeal, 
the Petitioner argues that she has shown eligibility to classify the Beneficiary as an alien of 
extraordinary ability in the arts (O-lB (Arts)) and discusses regulatory requirements under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv). She also claimed through her counsel's November 2024 letter that she has 
established eligibility for the O-lB (Arts) classification based on comparable evidence. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C). As discussed, however, the Petitioner cannot claim eligibility to classify the 
Beneficiary as an O-lB (Arts) nonimmigrant because the Beneficiary intends to perform services for 
motion picture or television industry while in the United States. See generally 2 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at M.4(A). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Based on the reasons we have discussed above, the Petitioner has not established eligibility to classify 
the Beneficiary as an 0-1 nonimmigrant who has a record of extraordinary achievements in motion 
picture and television productions, and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. See Section 10l(a)(l5)(O)(i) of the Act; see generally 2 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at M.4(A). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
