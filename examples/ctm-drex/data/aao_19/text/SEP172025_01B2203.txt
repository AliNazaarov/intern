Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a postdoctoral fellow and researcher, seeks first preference immigrant classification as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the ten required regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as judging others, original 
contributions, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established her receipt of a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the regulatory criteria. The Petitioner claims that 
she meets three of the regulatory criteria, namely that she has participated as a judge of the work of 
others in her field, made original contributions of major significance, and has authored scholarly 
articles. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (v), (vi). 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner has authored scholarly articles, and we will not disturb that 
finding. SCOPS found that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements for judging the work ofothers 
in her field and original contributions of major significance. On appeal, she asserts that SCOPS 
overlooked evidence and applied improper standards that are not supported by the law or relevant U.S. 
Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) policy. 
1. Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, art1st1c, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) 
To meet this criterion, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, 
artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(v). USCIS should first determine whether an alien has made original contributions in their 
field. 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f-chapter-
2. If so, the Agency should then determine whether any are of "major significance to the field." Id. 
Evidence of significant contributions can include published research that has provoked widespread 
commentary on its importance from others in the field, or documentation that research generated a 
high number of citations relative to others' work in the field. Id. When determining whether original 
contributions have major significance, detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature 
and significance of the contributions may provide valuable context, especially if accompanied by 
corroborating documentation. Id. 
At the outset, we note that SCOPS did not provide particularized analysis ofthe evidence in the record, 
and summed up the Petitioner's contributions as, "has worked on projects and conducted research 
which has been published." At the time of the petition's filing, the Petitioner established her

co-authorship of 14 articles in peer-reviewed, scientific journals in the field. These articles have been 
published in international journals such as Pain, Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, and 
Cognitions and Emotion. However, even if the Petitioner established that these journals were 
prestigious, this solely would not support the conclusion that her authorship was a contribution of 
major significance. Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou, No. 1 :23CV256, 2024 WL 4008312, at *7 (M.D.N.C. 
Aug. 30, 2024), report and recommendation adopted sub nom. Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou Dir. of 
United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 1 :23-CV-256, 2024 WL 4267307 (M.D.N.C. Sept. 
23, 2024) (upholding the high-ranking status of a journal did not show an author's influence or impact 
of the petitioner's research on the field, nor did it establish that every article published in a highly 
ranked journal automatically indicates a contribution of major significance). Here, the Petitioner has 
not established that publication in a popular or highly ranked journal alone demonstrates a contribution 
of major significance in the field. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 580 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2009), aff'd in part, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (finding publications and presentations 
alone are not sufficient to demonstrate major significance). 
Additionally, the Petitioner argues that her articles have generated a significant number of citations in 
the field. For example, Google Scholar indicates that her research in experimental psychology has 
been cited 697 times. And she claims her publications have been cited by researchers from around 40 
countries across 6 continents. According to the USCIS Policy Manual, "peer-reviewed presentations 
at academic symposia or peer-reviewed articles in scholarly journals that have provoked widespread 
commentary or received notice from others working in the field, or entries (particularly a goodly 
number) in a citation index which cite the alien's work as authoritative in the field, may be probative 
of the significance of the alien's contributions to the field of endeavor." Id. However, although the 
Petitioner asserts that her research has been cited 697 times, she has not shown how the citations were 
notable and therefore amounted to a contribution of major significance. We note that none of the 
articles appear to be written solely by the Petitioner, and appear to be collaborations with several 
individuals, thus it is difficult to gauge her scholarly contribution. Additionally, the Petitioner has not 
indicated how many of these citations were either self-citations or by her co-authors. Bhanu v. 
Garland, No. CV 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *7-8 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding plaintiff 
did not corroborate the number of independent citations from Google Scholar). Generally, citations 
can serve as an indication that the field has taken interest in a petitioner's research or written work. 
However, the Petitioner has not sufficiently shown that the citations for any of her published articles 
are commensurate with contributions ofmajor significance. Nor has she demonstrated the significance 
or relevance of the citations to her articles. See Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 
WL 6931334, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) (finding the record contained little evidence that 
contextualized the number of citations the petitioner's work had received). Although the citations are 
indicative that her research has received some attention from the field, the Petitioner did not establish 
that her citation numbers for any individual article represented major significant contributions in the 
field.

2. Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work 
of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought. 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) 
At the outset we note that SCOPS acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted evidence that she served 
as a peer reviewer for several journals, but then concluded that the submitted evidence did not meet 
this criterion. SCOPS cited as an example, 'judging a national contest of professionals is of far greater 
probative value than judging a competition for novices," as a reasoning for their decision. The 
Petitioner argues that SCOPS erroneously cited the pertinent regulation as 8 C.F.R. § 205.5(h)(3)(iv) 
and conflated the standard for a final merits decision, and the requirements for the regulatory criteria. 
In this instance, we determine the erroneous citation to be a de minimus error. 
Overall, SCOPS is required to adequately address a petitioner's specific claims and evidence. 2 When 
denying a petition, SCOPS must explain in writing the specific reasons for denial. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3(a)(l)(i). This explanation should be sufficient to allow the Petitioner a fair opportunity to 
contest the decision and to allow us an opportunity for meaningful appellate review. Cf, Matter ofM­
P-, 20 I&N Dec. 786 (BIA 1994) (finding at a decision must folly explain the reasons for denying a 
motion to allow the respondent a meaningful opportunity to challenge the determination on appeal). 
Here, SCOPS's decision, which relied in significant part on templated language, did not satisfy this 
requirement. Nevertheless, even if the Petitioner were to prevail on this criterion, she would still fall 
short of the required three criteria. We therefore reserve this issue. And, as our finding is dispositive 
of this appeal, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See, e.g., INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required 
to reach issues unnecessary to the ultimate conclusion). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring them to establish 
sustained national or international acclaim and present extensive documentation to demonstrate their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. Here, the 
Petitioner's inability to meet the above regulatory criterion means she cannot satisfy the initial 
evidentiary requirement at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Therefore, we cannot conclude that she is an alien of 
extraordinary ability and need not farther analyze the merits under the Act. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-1120; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, F.Supp.3d, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) 
(finding that the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of
