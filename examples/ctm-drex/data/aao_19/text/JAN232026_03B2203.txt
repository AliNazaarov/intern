Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a metallurgical engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not satisfy at least 
three, as required, of the initial evidentiary criteria. SCOPS subsequently dismissed the Petitioner's 
motion. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 
2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

B. Translations 
The Petitioner argues that "[t]he translations accompanying both the initial petition and the RFE 
response were properly certified, and they should be afforded foll probative value." The regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 1032.(b)(3) requires that "[a]ny document containing foreign language submitted to USCIS 
shall be accompanied by a foll English language translation which the translator has certified as 
complete and accurate, and by the translator's certification that he or she is competent to translate from 
the foreign language into English."2 Here, the Petitioner did not submit credible certifications. 
At initial filing, in response to the RFE, and on motion, the Petitioner provided the same blanket 
translation certification from either I I 
I Ifor all of the foreign language documents. None of the blanket translation certifications specify 
the foreign language documents to which they purportedly pertain. Again, the Petitioner simply 
attached the same blanket certification to each translation. 
Without certifications that identify the foreign language documents, the Petitioner did not demonstrate 
that each foreign language document was actually translated by the individual/entity who completed 
the certification. Furthermore, the Petitioner did not establish that each foreign language document 
was, in fact, folly translated and competently certified as complete and accurate. Accordingly, the 
translations contain no probative value, and therefore, will not be considered to determine his 
eligibility.

C. Extraordinary Ability 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not meet any of the eight claimed categories of evidence. 
On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he fulfills the same eight criteria. 
The Petitioner entirely relies on foreign language documents without properly certified English 
language translations for the awards (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)), scholarly articles (8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi)), and salary (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix)) criteria. As discussed above, because the 
Petitioner did not provide probative, credible translations, we will not consider any of them. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not demonstrate eligibility for any of these criteria. 3 
As it relates to the membership criterion, the record contains a job confirmation letter from T-S-, 
manager of the polymer department forl Iwho indicated the Petitioner's employment as a 
quality engineer, listed some job responsibilities, and stated that "[ a ]11 his tasks ... fulfilled to our 
complete satisfaction." The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the 
alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require 
outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields."4 Here, the Petitioner did not demonstrate how his employment qualifies 
as "membership in associations." Moreover, the Petitioner did not establish that I I 
"require[s] outstanding achievements oftheir members," and that membership is judged by recognized 
national or international experts. See Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 
1708126, at *4 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025) (upholding plaintiff did not show that outstanding 
achievements are required of its members as a minimum requirement for membership); see also 
Kumar v. Dir. ofUS. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 
(D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding plaintiff's documentation did not reflect recognized national or 
international experts determine qualifications for membership). As such, the Petitioner did not 
establish he meets this criterion. 
Regarding the published material criterion, the record contains a screenshot from youtube.com. The

classification is sought. Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author of the material, and any 
necessary translation."5 Here, the Petitioner did not include the author and date of the material. See 
Kumar, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (upholding plaintiff's evidence did not include the required title, 
date, or author). Moreover, although the Petitioner provided a translation ofthe transcript for the video

The Petitioner did not establish eligibility for any of the categories of evidence discussed above. 
Although the Petitioner also claims qualification for the contributions (8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v)) and roles (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii)) criteria, we need not reach these grounds 
because the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three under 8 C.F .R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). See Kinuthia v. Eiden, 702 F. Supp. 3d 5, 11 (D. Mass. 2023) (declining to consider a 
criterion when the plaintiff cannot satisfy at least three). Accordingly, we decline to reach and hereby 
reserve these issues. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
Regardless, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
