Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the petition, concluding that the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner warranted a favorable determination in the case's final merits 
analysis. We dismissed a subsequent appeal. The matter is now before us on combined motions to 
reopen and reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motions. 
A. Motion to Reconsider 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner contests the correctness of our prior decision, stating that we "improperly 
fragmented the evidentiary record, applied an impermissibly elevated standard, and failed to evaluate 
the cumulative significance of the evidence . ..." As an initial matter, the Petitioner contends that we 
erred in our analysis of the initial evidence requirements, specifically as relating to the regulatory 
criteria. However, our decision merely acknowledges USCIS' determinations as they relate to the 
evidentiary criteria. Noting that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements of satisfying the 
minimum amount of evidentiary criteria required, our decision analyzes the evidence under a final 
merits determination. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-part 
review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination). Our appellate decision and our decision 
here on motion need not consider the Petitioner's arguments that she meets the additional evidentiary 
criteria for awards, original contributions, and display by comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4), beyond the five already granted. 1 See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The Petitioner contends that our appellate decision erred in the final merits determination, in part, 
because we acknowledged that she satisfied multiple evidentiary criteria, but then determined that she 
did not establish sustained national and international acclaim or that she is among the small percentage 
at the very top of her field. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. The Petitioner's contention relies on a misunderstanding ofKazarian 
and USCIS policy. In determining whether a petitioner has established extraordinary ability, we use 
a two-step analysis to evaluate the evidence. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.2 As described in 
the USCIS Policy Manual, the evidence evaluated in the first step analysis is also reviewed in the 
second step, where the officer must determine whether the person is one of that small percentage who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and that the person has sustained national or 
international acclaim. 3 Objectively meeting the regulatory criteria in the first step does not establish 
that the person in fact meets the requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability. 4 
Therefore, our analysis, in a final merits determination, as to whether the evidence-including 
evidence that satisfies initial regulatory criteria-shows that the Petitioner meets the requirements for 
classification as a person with extraordinary ability, is a proper application of this framework. In fact, 
the USCIS Policy Manual provides the following example that relates to the judging criterion under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv): 
Participating in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization alone, regardless of the circumstances, should satisfy the regulatory 
criteria in the first step of the analysis. However, the second step requires the officer 
to evaluate the person's participation to determine whether it was indicative of the 
person being one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor and enjoying sustained national or international acclaim. 5 
Separation between assessing the evidentiary criteria and a final merits determination reflects the 
proper analysis in which the officer, at step one, determines only if the evidence objectively meets the 
regulatory criteria and, at step two, considers whether the evidence in its totality demonstrates 
sustained national or international acclaim. Here, we properly conducted a final merits analysis, 
separate from USCIS' analysis of the evidentiary criteria to determine whether the evidence in the

The Petitioner contends that our appellate decision minimized her impact within the field, applying a 
"materially heightened standard" by "demand[ing] indicators approaching field dominance, 
exceptional bibliometric prominence, or evidence exceeding undefined comparative benchmarks." 
The Petitioner asserts that in her field of healthcare ethics and medical communication, "distinction 
[is] demonstrated through institutional adoption, integration into professional training systems, 
implementation within healthcare programs, expert invitations, and reliance by clinicians and 
healthcare organizations." Our appellate decision did not require field dominance or a high citation 
record but concluded that the record did not provide sufficient evidence in which to compare the 
Petitioner to others in her field or proper context to evaluate the evidence and determine she has 
sustained national and international acclaim and that she is among the small percentage at the very top 
of her field. See section 203(b )(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 
F.3d at 1119-20. 
For example, our decision specifically quotes a letter in the record written by Dr. G-B-, who asserts 
that the Petitioner's "contributions extend beyond academia into policy development, training, and 
direct engagement with healthcare professionals and families." However, as we stated in our decision, 
the author did not offer corroborating evidence or specific examples to support his claim that the 
Petitioner's work "has influenced national initiatives that enhance healthcare access for families with 
limited English proficiency." A petitioner must support assertions with relevant, probative, and 
credible evidence. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. Here, the Petitioner did not provide 
sufficient supporting evidence to support the claimed impacts. 
In addition, our decision quotes the letter from Dr. A-D-, in which the author describes a project that 
he worked on with the Petitioner that was "implemented by a consortium of 11 universities from 
Ukraine, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Norway, and Germany." Here too, we explained that although the 
letter summarizes the Petitioner's work and praises her skills and contributions, it does not 
"demonstrate her achievements have been widely recognized in her field or otherwise show that she 
has garnered sustained acclaim at the very top of her field." We noted that the letters in the record do 
not provide sufficient explanation nor is there additional supporting evidence to show that the 
Petitioner is "viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, to be among the upper echelon 
or that she has garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor." Mishra v. Richardson, No. 1 :20-cv-991 
(LMB/TCB), 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the plaintiff's 
letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his accomplishments to show sustained 
national or international acclaim). 
The Petitioner asserts that our decision failed to synthesize the record as a whole and contends that the 
evidence "demonstrates sustained professional recognition through scholarly dissemination, 
evaluative authority, institutional leadership, healthcare implementation, and continuing expert 
reliance" and establishes "repeated invitations to participate in professional education and healthcare 
communication initiatives, leadership roles within distinguished organizations, authorship ofscholarly 
work, independent published material about the [Petitioner], and continued selection to contribute to 
professional practice within the field." Our decision states that the evidence of her "awards,

memberships, media coverage, judging, original contributions, authorship of scholarly articles, 
presented work, and leading roles reflects that she has received some recognition for her contributions

these categories and concluded that the record as a whole did not establish sustained national and

motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit.
