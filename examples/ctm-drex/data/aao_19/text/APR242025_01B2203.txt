Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a karate coach, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-
1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. 
permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) 
of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not meet 
any of the category's initial ten evidentiary requirements - three less than needed for a final merits 
determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he satisfied the 
following nine evidentiary criteria: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Appearances in published material about him and his work in the field; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in his or an allied field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field; 
• Display of his work at artistic exhibitions or showcases; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field; and 
• Commercial successes in performing arts. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v), (vii), (viii), (ix), (x). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he submitted evidence of his participation as a judge of others' work in an 
allied field and his performance in a leading role for an organization with a distinguished reputation. 
But, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the requisite minimum number of evidentiary criteria, we 
will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )( I )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(implementing a two-step extraordinary ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards for Excellence 
This criterion requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines if a petitioner - as opposed to their 
employer, for example - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), 
Criterion 1. If so, the Agency then determines whether the awards are for excellence in the applicable 
field and nationally or internationally recognized. Id.

coach or a manager.

As previously noted, a petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish their eligibility "[i]f 
the above standards do not readily apply to the[ir] occupation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). To the extent 
SCOPS found that the Petitioner must demonstrate that all ten evidentiary requirements do not readily 
apply to his occupation, we withdraw that finding. He need only establish that the criterion at issue 
does not readily apply to his occupation. See Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 190 (finding that, to meet

The Petitioner has not demonstrated that awards for excellence do not readily apply to karate coaches. 
The record lacks a detailed and specific statement from the Petitioner or other supporting evidence. 
Even if awards do not readily apply to his field, he has not established that receipt of his coaching 
licenses and fifth Dan grade certificate favorably compares to awards. 
For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence of his receipt of nationally or 
internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding 
regarding this criterion.

6. Display of the Petitioner's Work at Artistic Exhibits or Showcases 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
USCIS first determines whether displayed material is a petitioner's work product. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2.(B)(l), Criterion 7. USCTS then determines whether the venues that 
displayed the work - including virtual venues - were artistic exhibitions or showcases. Id. 
The Petitioner contends that his coaching at national and international karate competitions should 
serve as "comparable evidence" to meet this criterion. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). He states that, at 
these events, his students use the athletic skills he has taught them and, by winning championships, 
display the skills to the public. He notes that, under USCTS policy, "[a] person who is an Olympic 
coach whose athlete wins an Olympic medal while under the person's principal tutelage would likely 
constitute evidence comparable to that in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) [Criterion 5]." See 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), Comparable evidence. 
Federal courts have affirmed our finding that the phrase "artistic exhibitions or showcases" in this 
criterion limits applicable presentations to visual arts, not dance or performance arts. See Visinscaia 
v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 135-36 (D.D.C. 2013); Negro-Plumpe v. Okin, No. 2: 07-CV-820-ECR­
RJJ, 2008 WL 10697512, at *4 (D. Nev. Sep. 9, 2008). If even an art, karate would be a performance 
art rather than a visual art. We therefore agree with the Petitioner that this criterion does not readily 
apply to his karate coaching field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
But the Petitioner has not shown that his karate coaching at national or international competitions 
compares to displaying works of art. See Xia v. Miller, No. 3: 20-cv-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, 
at **4-6 (D. Or. Nov. 18. 2021) (finding insufficient evidence that coaching athletes to medals 
compares to contributions of major significance in the field). The record indicates that karate 
competitions primarily focus on the work or performances of athletes, not their coaches. Coaches' 
contributions to athletes' performances remain largely unseen by the public. They therefore are not 
on "display." 
9 
For the foregoing reasons, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence comparable to displaying his work 
in his field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. Thus, we will affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this 
criterion. 
7. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence that [they] ha[ve] performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
USCIS first determines whether a petitioner has performed in a leading or critical role for an 
organization, establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See 
generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion 8. A leading role means that a petitioner is ( or 
was) a leader within the organization, establishment, division or department. Id. In contrast, a critical 
role means that a petitioner "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome 
ofthe organization or establishment's activities or those ofa division or department ofthe organization 
or establishment." Id. 
Next, USCIS determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8. In this context, the word "distinguished" means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, supra. Relevant factors include not only an organization's relative size 
or longevity but also other information such as the scale of its customer base or relevant media 
coverage. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8.

9. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of commercial successes in the performing arts, as shown by box 
office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x). 
"This criterion focuses on volume of sales and box office receipts as a measure of the person's 
commercial success in the performing arts." 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion l0. "The 
evidence must show that the volume of sales and box office receipts reflect the person's commercial 
success relative to others involved in similar pursuits in the performing arts." Id. 
The Petitioner maintains that evidence of his participation in national and international karate 
competitions as a national coach qualifies as "comparable evidence" under this requirement. Some of 
his students purportedly provided him with "bonuses and extra remuneration" in appreciation of his 
coaching. 
11 
Karate coaches generally do not generate sales or receipts in the performing arts. We therefore agree 
with the Petitioner that this criterion does not readily apply to his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that his coaching at national or international karate 
competitions compares to commercial successes in the performing arts. See Xia, 2021 WL 5817324, 
at **4-6 (finding insufficient evidence that coaching athletes to medals compares to contributions of 
major significance in the field). Counsel states that, in gratitude for the Petitioner's coaching, students 
have provided him with additional compensation. But counsel's unsubstantiated assertion does not 
constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("[S]tatements in a 
brief, motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary 
weight.") Also, the record lacks the additional compensation amount purportedly received by the 
Petitioner and evidence that other karate coaches receive such compensation. The omission of such 
evidence prevents comparison of the Petitioner's commercial successes with those of others in the 
field. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that his documentation favorably compares to evidence of 
commercial successes in the performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, 
compact disk, or video sales. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this criterion. 
C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met two of the nine evidentiary requirements he claims to have met - one less than 
needed for a final merits determination. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1120 ( citation omitted) ("The 
regulations regarding this preference classification are extremely restrictive.") Because he did not 
satisfy the required minimum number of criteria, we need not reach and hereby reserve consideration 
of his final merits determination arguments. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to 
their ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not met the required minimum number of evidentiary criteria for the requested 
immigrant visa category. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
