The Petitioner, a film and television director, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria for this classification. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with 
the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, a petitioner must demonstrate: 
• The alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics 
which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• Their entry will offer substantial prospective benefits to the United States. 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
individuals in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If the petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review 
process as "consistent with the governing statute and regulation").

1. Published Materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. Professional or major trade publications may be identified 
by their intended audience, while other major media and major trade publications should have elevated 
circulation or readership relative to other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claimed eligibility under this criterion based on his submission of published material 
about him and his work from nine French print and online publications. SCOPS determined that none 
of the materials satisfied the plain language of the regulation. We disagree with SCOPS' assessment 
of the evidence and conclude that some articles meet the requirements of this criterion. For instance, 
the Petitioner submitted articles about him and his work in the field that were published in the French 
newspaper! Iand the online news publicationI I~ I). These articles 
include the title, date and author ofthe articles. In addition, the Petitioner submitted relevant, probative 
documentation about the publications and their relative readership and circulation. The evidence 
indicates that these published articles are about the Petitioner and his work in his field, and that they 
were published in major media in France. 
The Petitioner has demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that he meets this criterion. We 
will therefore withdraw SCOPS' findings to the contrary. 
2. Commercial Success 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) calls for "evidence of commercial successes in the 
performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." The 
evidence must show that the volume of sales or box office receipts reflects the alien's commercial 
success in the performing arts relative to others involved in similar pursuits. Recording and releasing 
musical compilations or performing in theatrical, motion picture, or television productions would be 
insufficient, in and of itself, to satisfy this criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner claims eligibility based on the commercial success of his animated feature filml I 
for which he served as both screenwriter and director. The record shows thatl Iwas initially 
released internationally in 2016 and released in the U.S. market in 2017. SCOPS concluded that the 
Petitioner did not satisfy this criterion because he did not provide evidence to "verify the volume of 
sales and box office receipts of others" involved in similar pursuits. 
The Petitioner provided evidence of box office receipts from credible industry sources, showing that 
his film, which had a $30 million budget, achieved gross receipts of approximately $109 million and 
was shown in over 2500 theaters worldwide. Contrary to SCOPS' determination, these industry 
sources also reported rankings sufficient to sho~ I's relative domestic and international box office 
performance across several categories, including among animated films and among films ofall genres. 
This evidence also showed the film's performance relative to other movies released the same year and

B. Basis for Remand 
Based on the foregoing discussion, the Petitioner has satisfied at least three of the ten criteria at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 3 We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' decision, because that decision was 
based on a determination that the Petitioner did not satisfy the initial evidence requirements for this 
classification. 
However, granting the third initial criterion does not suffice to establish eligibility for classification as 
an alien of extraordinary ability. The next step is to undertake a final merits determination to analyze 
whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national 
or international acclaim; that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor; 
and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See 
section 203(b )(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
at 1119-20; see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2). A final merits determination 
involves analyzing an individual's accomplishments and weighing the totality of the evidence to 
determine if their achievements are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(2). 
Rather than make such a determination in the first instance, we will remand the matter. SCOPS should 
consider any potentially relevant evidence of record, even it does not fit one of the regulatory criteria 
or is not comparable evidence as contemplated under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2). The Petitioner bears the burden of explaining the evidence's 
significance, and how it demonstrates his possession of sustained national or international acclaim and 
recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval or denial should depend on the evidence's type 
and quality. Id. 
ORDER: SCOPS' decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision
