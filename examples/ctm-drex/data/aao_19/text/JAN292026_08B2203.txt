Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software engineer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not 
establish his satisfaction of at least three initial evidentiary criteria. Specifically, the Director found 
the Petitioner met only two (judging under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and roles under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii)) of the four claimed categories of evidence. We dismissed a subsequent appeal, 
determining that the Petitioner did not meet any other contested criteria (awards under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) and salary under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix)). The matter is now before us on combined 
motions to reopen and reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motions. 
I. Analysis 
A motion to reopen must state new facts and be supported by documentary evidence. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.5(a)(2). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, we will only review the 
latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(l )(i), (ii). We may grant motions that satisfy 
these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. See Matter of Coelho, 20 
l&N Dec. 464, 4 73 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence has the potential to change the outcome). 
On motion, for the first time, the Petitioner claims eligibility for three new criteria - memberships (8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)), contributions (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v)), and scholarly articles (8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vi)). The Petitioner had the opportunity to make these eligibility claims when he initially 
filed his petition or in response to SCOPS' request evidence. Because the Petitioner did not make 
these claims before SCOPS, we will not consider them for the first time in this appellate proceeding, 
including on motion. Generally, appellate bodies will not decide issues in the first instance or consider 
claims raised for the first time in an appeal. See National R[fle Association ofAmerica v. Vullo, 144 
S.Ct. 1316, 1331 n.5 (2024). Furthermore, regarding the awards and salary criteria, the Petitioner 
provides previously submitted documentation. As we have already reviewed and addressed the 
evidence, we will not re-adjudicate the petition anew. 
In the appellate decision discussing the awards criterion, we indicated, in part, that the Petitioner 
provided recommendation letters. However, the letters indicated that the awards were only available 
to employees of the granting organizations and announced in internal publications; therefore, the 
Petitioner did not demonstrate recognition of the awards beyond the awarding entities. On motion, 
the Petitioner submits additional letters from various individuals claiming they are personally aware 
of the awards. However, the Petitioner's submission ofletters from five individuals who have heard 
about the awards does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's awards qualify as nationally or 
internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field consistent with the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). The letters do not provide specific, detailed information explaining the national or 
international recognition of the awards in the field. Besides personally hearing about the awards, the 
letters do not reference any other indicators of national or international recognition for excellence or 
offer any persuasive evidence of them. 
In discussing the salary criterion, we indicated, in part, that the Petitioner offered comparative 
evidence of average salaries rather than showing he commanded a high salary. Furthermore, the 
Petitioner claimed his cumulative earnings of salary, bonuses, and restricted stock and compared them 
to only the wages of others without factoring in bonuses and restricted stock; the Petitioner did not 
provide an "apples to apples" comparison rather than an "apples to oranges" one. 
On motion, the Petitioner asserts: 
In its September 3, 2025 decision, the AAO acknowledged that [the Petitioner] 
submitted salary records, T4 forms, and comparative wage surveys. However, it 
dismissed the evidence, reasoning that: 
• "Average, local or national salary levels do not allow for an appropriate 
basis of comparison under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix)." 
• The record "lacked evidence showing that the Petitioner's compensation 
was consistently higher than that of others in the field, such as percentile­
based wage data, longitudinal comparisons, or authoritative industry 
benchmarks." 
Accordingly, the AAO concluded that [the Petitioner] had "not demonstrated that his 
remuneration is significantly higher than that of others in his occupation." 
Although the Petitioner claims to quote from our appellate decision, our decision does not contain any 
of those statements. Moreover, the Petitioner argues that he "now provides ... records from 2022-
2025." However, the Petitioner filed his petition in December 2024, and the evidence relating to 
events in 2025 occurred after the filing of his petition and will not be considered. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.2(b)(l) (indicating eligibility must be established at filing). Furthermore, the Petitioner claims

that "[t]his cures AAO's concern that the evidence was limited to one year." However, our decision 
made no mention ofthe Petitioner's evidence being limited to only one year, nor was it an issue raised 
in our decision. 
Again, the scope of a motion is limited to the prior decision, which in this case, is our decision 
dismissing his appeal. On motion, we will not address issues raised in prior proceedings, including 
purported determinations or statements we never made. 
The Petitioner also submits evidence with self-serving captions embedded on the purported original 
material describing the content, year, source, and other information, including comparative salary 
information for a 20221 ~ob offer inl !Massachusetts. The Petitioner's altered evidence 
compromises the integrity of the material and does not represent credible and probative comparable 
salary or other remuneration information. Although the Petitioner offers his wage history from 2022 
- 2024, including other compensation such as bonuses and restricted stock options, the Petitioner did 
not present reliable comparable documentation establishing that he commanded a high salary or other 
compensation. 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the 
prior decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), 
(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. 
On motion, the Petitioner makes broad assertions without further explaining how we erred as a matter 
oflaw or policy or makes claims not supported by the record. For instance, the Petitioner claims under 
the salary criterion that "[i]mposing a 'percentile-only' requirement is impermissible under Kazarian," 
and "[t]he AAO faulted Petitioner's evidence for lacking 'percentile-based" wage data and 
'longitudinal comparisons."' Our decision does not reflect we "imposed a percentile-only 
requirement," nor is there any mention of"longitudinal comparisons." Likewise, the Petitioner asserts 
that "[t]he AAO also faulted Petitioner's award for lacking external publicity and published award 
criteria, again not raised in the RFE." Our decision does not "fault" the Petitioner for not submitting 
external publicity and published award criteria, let alone mention anything about these issues. 
In another example, the Petitioner also asserts that we overlooked his submission of nine affidavits 
under the awards criterion. On the contrary, our decision specifically addressed his recommendation 
letters, including a discussion of a sample letter. In addition, the Petitioner argues that "[t]he AAO 
explicitly refused to consider new evidence submitted on appeal, citing procedural timing." However, 
the Petitioner did not demonstrate that we erred in not considering new evidence on appeal. In fact, 
in our appellate decision, we cited to 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l 1) and Matter of Soriano, 19 I&N Dec. 
764, 766 (BIA 1988) to support our determination rather than simply "procedural timing." The 
Petitioner's unsubstantiated assertions and broad claims of various errors without further elaboration 
do not show we incorrectly applied law or policy.

II. Conclusion 
Although the Petitioner has submitted additional evidence in support of the motion to reopen, the 
Petitioner has not established eligibility. On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not demonstrated 
that our previous decision was based on an incorrect application of law or policy at the time we issued 
our decision. Therefore, the motion will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed. 
FURTHER ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
