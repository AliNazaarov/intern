Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a petroleum and industrial engineer who seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers 
(petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, internationally 
recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. We 
dismissed his appeal. The matter returns to us on the Petitioner's combined motion to reopen and 
reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter of Chawathe, 
25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we affirm our prior conclusion that the Petitioner 
has not met the required number of evidentiary criteria. We will dismiss the motion to reopen and 
reconsider. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b) of the Act states, in pertinent part, that: 
(1) Priority workers. -- Visas shall first be made available ... to qualified immigrants who 
are aliens described in any of the following subparagraphs (A) through (C): 
(A) Aliens with extraordinary ability. -- An alien is described in this subparagraph if--
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim 
and recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award). If a petitioner does not have such an award, then he must 
provide sufficient evidence material that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). 
Satisfaction of at least three criteria, however, does not, in and of itself, establish eligibility for this 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the evidence is first counted and then, if the required number of criteria are met, considered in 
the context ofa final merits determination). See also Rijalv. USCIS, 772 F.Supp.2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 
2011) (affirming our proper application ofKazarian), aff'd, 683 F.3d. 1030 (9th Cir. 2012); Visinscaia 
v. Beers, 4 F.Supp.3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013) (finding that U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) appropriately applied the two-step review); Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 
376 (AAO 2010) (holding that the "truth is to be determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but 
by its quality" and that USCIS examines "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and 
credibility, both individually and within the context of the totality of the evidence, to determine 
whether the fact to be proven is probably true"). 
A motion to reopen must state the new facts to be provided and to be supported by affidavits or other 
documentation. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). However, any new facts must relate to eligibility at the time 
the Petitioner filed the petition. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l), (12); see also Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N 
Dec. 45, 49 (Reg'l Comm'r 1971). 
A motion to reconsider must state the reasons for reconsideration and be supported by any pertinent 
precedent decisions to establish that the decision was based on an incorrect application of law or 
USCIS policy. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). A motion to reconsider is based on the existing record and the 
Petitioner may not introduce new facts or evidence relative to his or her arguments. A motion to 
reconsider contests the correctness of the original decision based on the previous factual record, as 
opposed to a motion to reopen which seeks a new hearing based on fresh documents. Compare 8 
C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3) and 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(2). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior 
decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). 
We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and demonstrate eligibility for the requested 
benefit. See Matter ofCoelho, 20 I&N Dec. 464, 473 (BIA 1992) (requiring that new evidence have 
the potential to change the outcome).

II. ANALYSIS 
1. Regulatory Criteria 
A. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the 
field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or 
international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
At the outset, we note that SCOPS discussed the evidence submitted for this criterion and found that 
the Petitioner did not establish his eligibility. On appeal, the Petitioner did not contest SCOPS's 
findings for this criterion or offer additional arguments. We therefore concluded that the Petitioner 
abandoned or forfeited his eligibility claims under this criterion. Any argument a filing party does not 
raise on appeal is deemed waived. Matter ofC-A-R-R, 29 I&N Dec. 13, 18 (BIA 2025). On motion, 
the Petitioner has not resurrected this argument. 
B. "Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, 
in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
In the initial filing, the Petitioner indicated he was engaged in scientific research, and he submitted "a 
copy of [his] mono graphic work" as evidence under this criterion. The Petitioner is listed as a co­
author. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the requirements here. However, we determined 
that the evidence the Petitioner submitted for the record did not demonstrate that he satisfied these 
requirements. The Petitioner did not submit sufficient information about the publication to 
demonstrate it was one of the required publication types, or evidence establishing the journal's 
existence. Although the Petitioner presented the title of his work, he did not identify the publication's 
name, and it was not apparent from the English language translation. Because we determined that the 
record lacked such evidence, we withdrew SCOPS's favorable determination under this criterion. 
On motion, the Petitioner argues that we improperly disregarded evidence of his scholarly authorship 
and that our conclusion "reflects both a factual misreading of the record and a legal misapplication of 
the plain language of the regulation." He argues that he only needs to demonstrate authorship of 
scholarly articles in the field for which classification is sought and that these articles were published 
in professional or major trade publications or other major media. He asserts that the publication 
addressed a highly specialized area within industrial and petroleum engineering; it was published 
under the auspices ofl Iand it included an English language 
translation, a cover page identifying the institutional publisher, and a publisher's letter confirming its 
appearance in the university's Technical Research Series. He farther argues that this series is peer­
reviewed, curated by a faculty editorial board, and distributed through both print and digital university 
archives. In addition, he states that he submitted institutional evidence verifying the circulation and 
scholarly nature of the series. 
3 
On motion, the Petitioner has not overcome our determination that the monograph does not constitute 
a major publication. Although the Petitioner references the circulation of the monograph, it appears 
that the number was capped at a modest 500. Moreover, on motion, the Petitioner has not provided 
evidence that the journal in which he published his article was a major trade publication. Matter of 
Chawathe. To be a major trade publication, "the trade journal must be one of the major publications 
in the field." Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06-5105 SJO (FMOx), 2007 WL 9229758, *6 (C.D. Cal. July 
6, 2007). Thus, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his claimed authorship of scholarly articles in his 
field in a major trade publication. We further determine that the publication does not meet the 
requirements of a major trade publication. Upon further consideration however, the Petitioner has 
established that his 1993 monograph titled, "Mathematical models of control system functioning/Gas 
production and transportation," is a professional publication. The term "profession" includes but is 
not limited to architects, engineers, lawyers, physicians, surgeons, and teachers in elementary or 
secondary schools, colleges, academics, or seminaries. See section 10l(a)(32) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 10l(a)(32). 
In evaluating whether a submitted publication is a professional publication, a relevant factor includes 
the intended audience. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Here, the intended audience includes engineers, a profession. 
Because the Petitioner demonstrated the publication's intended audience meets the definition of 
"profession" under the statute and regulations, the Petitioner has established the publication qualifies 
as a professional one. Therefore, upon further consideration, we determine that the Petitioner meets 
this criterion. 
C. "Documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of 
endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; and 
(3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner claimed two honorarium certificates under this criterion and SCOPS determined that 
he did not meet the regulatory requirements. SCOPS's request for evidence (RFE) conveyed that the 
certificates appeared to represent local work-related honors instead of accolades recognized by the 
field. SCOPS also concluded the RFE response materials were inadequate to satisfy the criterion's 
requirements as two photographs ofthe evidence coupled with counsel's assertions were not sufficient 
to meet his burden of proof under this requirement. On appeal, the Petitioner offered additional 
assertions about his evidence similar to the claims he presented to SCOPS. He did not offer any 
additional evidence with the appeal. 
We agreed with SCOPS that the Petitioner's assertions, coupled with the assertions of his attorney, 
and his evidence consisting of two photographs fell short of meeting his burden of proof A 
petitioner's burden of proof comprises both the initial burden of production, as well as the ultimate 
burden of persuasion. Matter of Y-B-, 21 I&N Dec. 1136, 1142 n.3 (BIA 1998). First, a petitioner 
must satisfy the burden of production. As the term suggests, this burden requires a filing party to 
4 
produce evidence in the form of documents, testimony, etc. that adheres to the governing statutory, 
regulatory, and policy provisions sufficient to have the issue decided on the merits. 
Second, a petitioner must satisfy the burden of persuasion, meaning they must establish the degree to 
which their arguments and evidence should persuade or convince USCIS that the requisite eligibility 
parameters have been met (i.e., the obligation to persuade the trier of fact ofthe truth of a proposition). 
Dir., Office ofWorkers' Comp. Programs, Dep'tofLaborv. Greenwich Collieries, 512 U.S. 267,274 
(1994). The level at which petitioners must persuade in the present context is the preponderance of 
the evidence. Whether a petitioner can show that a particular fact or event is more likely than not true 
is the determinant of whether they have met the preponderance of the evidence standard of proof. 
On motion, the Petitioner again points to the honorarium certificates and repeats his arguments on 
appeal. He asserts that the Petitioner received honorarium certificates from I land the 
in acknowledgment of his leadership in large-scale~----------------~ modernization projects that directly improved Uzbekistan's national gas infrastructure, and that these 
entities are not minor or local employers but are principal operators of Uzbekistan's energy grid. We 
determine that the honorarium certificates do not meet this criterion. The honorarium certificates on 
their faces explain why they were given to the Petitioner. The honorarium certificate froml I 
states that it was presented to the Petitioner "on the occasion of the employee's professional holiday 
effective work, outstanding performance among his peers and for actively participating in public 
events." While the honorarium certificate from I ]states that 
is being presented to the Petitioner "Successfully [sic] and for showing a special example at work with 
selfless attitude." The honorarium certificates represent the Petitioner's recognition and appreciation 
for his work rather than a nationally recognized award for excellence. 
In addition, the Petitioner asserts that he was presented with a medal from the President of Uzbekistan 
in celebration of the country's tenth anniversary of its independence. He proffers that this medal 
"honors individuals who have made significant contributions to national infrastructure and 
development" and the "award is granted selectively at the national level, and its issuance is a matter 
of public record, not internal employment recognition." While the record contains a photograph of the 
front of the purported medal, the photograph does not establish that it was presented to the Petitioner. 
No name is included on the face of the medal, and the Petitioner has not provided a photograph of the 
back of the medal. Moreover, the medal is not accompanied by a certificate identifying the Petitioner 
as the recipient. Furthermore, there is insufficient documentation in the record explaining under what 
circumstances the medal is awarded. The Petitioner states that the medal was "conferred by 
presidential decree," however the Petitioner did not submit a copy ofthe decree issued by the President 
of Uzbekistan explaining the purpose of the medal and on whom it may bestowed. Even ifwe were 
to consider this medal, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the medal was awarded to honor 
"individuals who have made significant contributions to national infrastructure and development" and 
that it is "granted selectively at the national level." The language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) specifically requires that the Petitioner's awards be nationally or internationally 
recognized in the field of endeavor and it is his burden to establish every element ofthis criterion. The 
record is insufficient to confirm that the Petitioner's medal constitutes his receipt of a nationally or 
internationally recognized prize or award for excellence in the field of petroleum and industrial 
engineering. Consequently, on motion, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain 
language requirements of this criterion.

D. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role 
for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished 
reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
On appeal we reserved the above criterion because the Petitioner's arguments failed on other criteria. 
Consequently, he could not numerically meet the required number of criteria required to establish 
eligibility. Therefore, it was unnecessary for us to consider these additional grounds under the 
regulation. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. For a critical role, evidence should 
show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the 
organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or 
establishment. In determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division 
thereof: has a distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale ofits customer 
base, and relevant media coverage. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
On motion, the Petitioner submits an April 2025 recommendation letter from V-K-, 1 the First Deputy 
Director of.__ _________. In the letter, V-K- opines that the Petitioner "was a very honest 
and professional individual, who trained many highly skilled professionals. All tasks assigned to him 
were completed conscientiously and within the established deadlines." V-K- describes the work the 
Petitioner did and otherwise repeats the information already in the record including that from 1998 to 
2001, the Petitioner was the Head of the administration and that the administration employed about 
3,500 people, consisting of 20 subordinate divisions. However, this letter is not from an employer as 
required by regulation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(g)(l) states that the evidence of experience shall consist of 
letters from employers. Here, V-K- does not indicate that he was at any time the Petitioner's employer. 
This recommendation letter is therefore insufficient to meet the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
Moreover, the Petitioner has not indicated that evidence relating to his qualifying experience or 
training was unavailable. Furthermore, because the Petitioner was put on notice and given a 
reasonable opportunity to provide this evidence, we will not consider it for the first time on motion. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(l 1) (requiring all requested evidence be submitted together at one time); 
Matter ofSoriano, 19 I&N Dec. 764, 766 (BIA 1988) ( declining to consider new evidence submitted 
on appeal because "petitioner was put on notice of the required evidence and given a reasonable 
opportunity to provide it for the record before the denial"). We therefore determine that the Petitioner 
has not met this criterion.

3. Final Merits Determination 
On appeal, we reserved a final merits decision. We determined that there was insufficient evidence 
that the Petitioner has the acclaim and recognition needed for the requested classification. On motion, 
the Petitioner disagrees with our findings. We have reversed one ofour findings, but it is not sufficient 
for the Petitioner to establish eligibility for the benefit. Because he has not been determined to have 
garnered acclaim and recognition and he has not met the requisite number of evidentiary criteria, we 
need not reach final merits issues and hereby reserve their consideration. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility for the requested classification. Although the Petitioner 
has submitted additional evidence in support ofthe motion to reopen, the Petitioner has not established 
eligibility. On motion to reconsider, the Petitioner has not established that our previous decision was 
based on an incorrect application of law or policy at the time we issued our decision. Therefore, the 
motion will be dismissed. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(4). See also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th 
Cir. 2022). 
ORDER: The motion to reopen is dismissed.
