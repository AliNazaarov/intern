Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a postdoctoral researcher who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant 
classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a postdoctoral research engineer specializing in scientific machine learning, 
uncertainty quantification, and numerical modeling, with experience integrating data-driven and 
mechanistic models for stochastic energy systems. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met four of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and the authorship 
of scholarly articles but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with original contributions or 
performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner presents several arguments relating 
to the request for evidence (RFE) SCOPS issued, but this appeal is only a means to contest their final 
decision so we will not address any issues associated with the RFE. Here, the Petitioner maintains 
that he meets the two denied evidentiary criteria. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we 
conclude that through his appeal, he has not demonstrated SCOPS erred in a manner that would 
warrant a different result from the denial. 
l. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect 
and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or 
to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion.

The Petitioner provided support letters, some of his published work and other researchers citing to it, 
and material relating to his citation record. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the 
requirements of this criterion. 
On appeal, the contest four elements in the denial. First, he took issue with SCOPS' statement that 
the phrase contributions of major significance in the field requires substantial influence beyond one's 
employer, clients, or customers. But that statement was made in the context of general requirements 
a petitioner must show and SCOPS did not directly apply that to the facts of this case. As a result, this 
is not an error on SCOPS' part and it is unnecessary that we address it further. 
His second argument relates to SCOPS' discussion of support letters and their statement that 
correspondence "written by experts may be helpful; however, the major significance of the 
beneficiary's work must be demonstrated by preexisting, independent, and objective evidence." In 
support here, he notes his contributions to the field have been adopted internationally by individual 
researchers. But simple adoption of his original work does not illustrate how that has made an impact 
in the field necessary to meet this criterion's mandates that it be of major significance. This does not 
demonstrate a fundamental shift in the field's philosophy or a transformative advancement in research, 
rather than the routine academic acknowledgement typical in the research oriented fields. Agency 
policy requires one's published research to have "provoked widespread commentary on its importance 
from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' 
work in that field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), https://www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. The acknowledgement of his findings internationally falls far short of this standard. 
Third, he notes "[t]he denial notice also seeks 'specific examples of contributions that are indicative 
of major significance"' and he identifies evidence that he provides on appeal that he did not submit to 
SCOPS (a Department of Energy report), or that postdates the petition filing date (a journal article in 
Elsevier). Most importantly, the portion of the denial the Petitioner quotes was discussing the 
shortcomings associated with his recommendation letters and not general evidence, but he contests 
that portion of the denial without mentioning the letters. So, his argument here does not appear to be 
directly related to the portion of the denial that he cites. 
What's more, because the Petitioner was provided proper notice and a reasonable opportunity to 
respond to an RFE about this issue, we decline to consider new evidence submitted for the first time 
on appeal, as the appellate process is not a venue for initial filings. Under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .2(b )(11 ), all 
requested evidence must be submitted together, and administrative bodies consistently refuse to review 
new evidence first introduced on appeal. See Matter of Furtado, 28 I&N Dec. 794, 801-02 (BIA 
2024); Matter ofIzaguirre, 27 I&N Dec. 67, 71 (BIA 2017); Matter ofJin, 29 I&N Dec. 441, 449 
(BIA 2026). Additional precedent strongly reinforces this restriction. See Matter ofN-P-A-, 29 I&N 
Dec. 347,349 (BIA 2025); Matter ofD-J-L-, 29 I&N Dec. 485,489 (BIA 2026); Matter ofD-G-E-A­
& N-G-G-E-, 29 I&N Dec. 570, 577 n.5 (BIA 2026). 
And beyond that, a petitioner must establish eligibility at the time they file the visa petition. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103 .2(b)(1 ), (12). USCIS may not approve a visa petition ifa petitioner was not qualified at the priority 
date but expects to become eligible at a subsequent time. See Matter oflzummi, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 175-76 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1998); MatterofKatigbak, 14 I&NDec. 45, 49 (Reg'! Comm'r 1971).

For the final element under this criterion, the Petitioner quotes from the denial and he states: 
For the citation records, the denial notice required "to compare the petitioner's citations 
to other similarly, highly cited articles that the field views as having been of major 
significance". I believe this requirement has been met by the analysis of the caliber of 
journals in which my articles have been published [], and consequently the relative

Within SCOPS' decision, the quoted portion was evaluating the evidence the Petitioner provided from 
Clarivate Analytics in which they stated that instead of comparing his citation rates to the average in 
a field, "[a] more appropriate analysis [] would be to compare the petitioner's citations to other 
similarly, highly cited articles that the field views as having been of major significance ...." Instead 
of adhering to that, the Petitioner relies on the stature ofjournals in which he has published. 
But the Petitioner doesn't go on to explain how publishing in high caliber journals demonstrates the 
papers he published in them amounted to a contribution in the field that rose to the level of being of 
major significance. See Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou, No. 1 :23CV256, 2024 WL 4008312, at *7 
(M.D.N.C. Aug. 30, 2024), report and recommendation adopted sub nom. Venkatnarayan v. Jaddou 
Dir. o_f United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. l:23-CV-256, 2024 WL 4267307 (M.D.N.C. 
Sept. 23, 2024) (upholding the high-ranking status of a journal did not show an author's influence or 
impact of the petitioner's research on the field, nor did it establish that every article published in a 
highly ranked journal automatically indicates a contribution ofmajor significance); Agrawal v. Miller, 
No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 2879217, at *20 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025) (upholding that it cannot be 
automatically presumed that all articles published by respected journals and organizations are 
considered majorly significant). 
The Petitioner closes his arguments under this criterion referring to unpublished decisions from this 
office dating back to 2014. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(c) provides that this office's precedent 
decisions are binding on all USCIS employees in the administration of the Act. But for much the same 
reasons federal courts recognize the Board of Immigration Appeals' unpublished decisions carry no 
precedential value (see, e.g., Charles v. Garland, 113 F.4th 20, 24 (1st Cir. 2024), our unpublished 
decisions are not binding on us or on agency officers. Deciding in one manner in one case-or in a 
presented set of cases-does not limit our discretion to decide in an alternative manner in a similar case. 
Another factor is each eligibility determination on a benefit request will be based on information 
contained in the record of proceeding before us. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(10), (11), (14), (16)(i)-(ii). Each 
case must be decided on its own facts with regard to the sufficiency ofthe claims and evidence presented. 
See, e.g., Matter o_fH-C-R-C-, 28 I&N Dec. 809,812 (BIA 2024). 
We conclude that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that his contributions have had a 
sufficiently appreciable impact in the field, and as a result he has not met this criterion's requirements.

2. "Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "evidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations 
or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." A 
qualifying role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization 
or establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Evidence of a leading role should show that 
the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help to 
establish that a role was leading. A leading role should be apparent by its position in the overall 
hierarchy and the role's matching duties. Aravamuthan v. Jaddou, No. 3:23-CV-01412-N, 2025 WL 
1708126, at *7 (N.D. Tex. June 17, 2025). 
For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l ). In determining whether the organization, establishment, department, or division has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. Id. Organizations, establishments, divisions, or departments that enjoy a 
distinguished reputation are "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence." Id. The Petitioner 
must submit evidence satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this 
criterion. Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *7 (E.D. Wis. Nov. 4, 2024). 
The Petitioner claims he performed in a critical role in the Department of Chemical Engineering at a 
U.S. university. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements ofthis criterion. 
While there appears to be some confusion about which entity the Petitioner must have performed in a 
critical role for, here we clarify that ifhe claims he performed in a critical role for a department within 
the larger university, he must demonstrate that department enjoys a distinguished reputation, and that 
he has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the department's 
activities. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). In other words, the Petitioner 
must show the manner in which his work on behalf of the department resulted in a measurable level 
of success for that entity. 
We agree the relevant department enjoys a distinguished reputation. However, the Petitioner's appeal 
lacks specificity in identifying what particular evidence supports his claims that he performed in a 
critical role that contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the 
department's activities. Instead of listing and discussing any evidence then explaining how that 
supports his assertions, he only listed page numbers from his initial filing and RFE response. We 
reviewed the relevant pages and we were unable to locate the type of support the Petitioner presents 
for his role under this criterion. The Petitioner stated correspondence from leadership of a project he 
worked on would reflect the critical role he played "for the success of the project [] and how this has 
been 'critical to our [university's] success."' As we noted, the correspondence the Petitioner alluded 
to contained no such language and did not convey anything similar. 
Instead, the author explained the Petitioner was a standout researcher who graduated with a Ph.D. from 
the university, discussed the focus of his research, and noted that he made "impressive achievements"

on research projects in general. And when discussing a Department ofEnergy related project he stated 
the Petitioner's research formed a key part ofthe project and his "contribution to this research ensures 
that the health monitoring tool is highly robust to uncharacterized noise associated with plant data and 
this has been validated with data from both coal and gas fired power plants." This better addresses his 
work on a project than it does the department and it will not aid the Petitioner in demonstrating his 
contributions to the department in question were pivotal to its overarching success. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets this criterion's requirements. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 810; Agrawal, 2025 WL 
2879217, at* 12; Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 
2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an 
exhaustive historical review of the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the 
comprehensive regulatory scheme intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive 
this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 
WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026) (concluding the two-step framework was adopted without 
required notice-and-comment rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report­
which serves solely to explain how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two 
versions of the bill-as being more authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the 
purpose of which includes guiding the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the 
judicial branch in its interpretation. That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before 
and since the Mukherji court made it. Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final 
merits determination in this matter. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely advisory 
findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)).
