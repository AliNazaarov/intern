Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability in physics. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish he met the initial evidence 
requirements for the classification by establishing his receipt ofa major, internationally recognized award 
or by meeting three ofthe ten evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us 
on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with 
the following analysis. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; who seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and whose entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the United 
States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met only two criteria,judging the work of others under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and scholarly articles under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he fulfills one additional criterion, original contributions ofmajor 
significance in the field. The Petitioner contends that SCOPS made several errors in this matter, 
including errors of fact, law, and policy. 
For the criterion of original contributions, the regulations state a petitioner must submit "[ e ]vidence 
of [the petitioner's] original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of 
major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). A petitioner must first demonstrate making 
original contributions in the field. In addition, the petitioner must show the original contributions are 
of major significance to the field. The criterion focuses on whether a petitioner's original work 
constitutes major, significant contributions to the field. A petitioner may show that his or her 
contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, have remarkably impacted or 
influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance. Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. 
Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an original contribution may include, 
but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
Upon review, we agree with the Petitioner that SCOPS' decision contains sufficient errors to warrant 
a remand. When considering the evidence for the criterion of original contributions under 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(v), SCOPS' decision uses language relating to the analytical framework for adjudicating 
national interest waivers in the precedent decision Matter ofDhanasar, 26 l&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016). 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.5(D). 
For instance, SCOPS' decision analyzes evidence of the Petitioner's research citation history, stating 
the record lacks independent evidence to "prove that the [P]etitioner's citation record is indicative of 
a record of success or otherwise demonstrate[s] he is well positioned to advance his proposed 
endeavor." ( emphasis added). Similarly, when considering evidence of journal rankings, SCOPS 
2 
states, "USCIS looks to information about the individual articles which appeared in those journals to 
prove that the [P]etitioner' s research is influential or considered a record ofsuccess that demonstrates 
he is well positioned to advance his proposed endeavor." (emphasis added). SCOPS' consideration 
of the evidence using national interest waiver criteria calls into question whether the evidence meets 
the requirements for the criterion of original contributions under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) and the 
requested classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
In addition, SCOPS' decision analyzes "review articles" summarizing the Petitioner's work and states 
the "review articles cannot establish that the [P]etitioner is well positioned to advance his proposed 
endeavor." (emphasis added). Again, SCOPS misapplies the use ofnational interest waiver criteria to 
the Petitioner's request for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. Moreover, SCOPS' 
analysis of "review articles" is not clear because the record does not include review articles. 
To meet the criterion for original contributions, the Petitioner submitted letters of support from 
professionals in the field, independent advisory opinions from researchers who have relied on his 
work, the Petitioner's research articles, and his research publication citation history. SCOPS' decision 
quotes from the letters of support but does not explain with any specificity why the letters were not 
considered for the criterion of original contributions. An officer must fully explain the reasons for 
denying a visa petition. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(i). Although SCOPS briefly mentioned and discussed 
the letters of support, and concluded that they were insufficient, the decision does not adequately 
explain why the Petitioner's evidence was deficient to support his claims that he meets the criterion 
for original contributions and is an individual of extraordinary ability. 
On remand, SCOPS is instructed to re-evaluate the evidence submitted in support of the petition to 
determine whether the Petitioner satisfied the plain language of at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), and to issue a new decision. In doing so, SCOPS should also review the Petitioner's 
appellate brief and documentation, which further addresses the previously submitted evidence. 
As SCOPS did not conclude that the Petitioner met the initial evidence requirements, the decision did 
not include a final merits determination. If after review SCOPS dete1mines that the Petitioner received 
a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3), the 
new decision should include an analysis of the totality of the record evaluating whether the Petitioner 
has demonstrated, by a preponderance ofthe evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim, 
that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. 
ORDER: SCOPS' decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
