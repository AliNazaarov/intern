Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a conceptual artist, requests classification under the employment-based, first 
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner met two of ten 
initial evidentiary requirements - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, 
the Petitioner contends that he also satisfied the following four evidentiary criteria: 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Published material about him and his work in the field; 
• Original contributions of major significance in his field; and 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in his field. 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (iii), (v), (vi). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he met a third evidentiary requirement by submitting evidence of published 
material about himself and his work in his field. We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' decision and 
remand the matter for a final merits determination and entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, 
business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

States. He stated: "By showcasing work that is both globally relevant and distinctly rooted in the 
values of freedom and innovation, I can amplify the country's reputation as a hub for artistic

1. Membership in Associations 
This criterion requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for 
which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in three art associations and documentation 
about them. The record, however, supports SCOPS' finding of insufficient evidence that the 
associations require outstanding achievements of their members. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that membership requirements for one international association to 
which he belongs are "exceptionally rigorous, designed to include only artists who have demonstrated 
significant public engagement and recognition in the art world." But a copy of online information 
about the association does not support his argument. The online information states that artists qualify 
for association membership "not only if they earn a living by the practice of art but also if any one of 
the following or similar criteria can be applied." The criteria include: a diploma in fine or applied 
arts; teaching at a fine arts school; frequent exhibition of artwork or work that the public at large 
otherwise sees; or receipt of "important national or international prizes." 
The online information does not demonstrate that the international association "require[s}" 
outstanding achievements of its members. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) (emphasis added). For 
example, the information indicates that artists can gain association membership based on receipt of 
fine or applied arts diplomas. But the record does not establish that receipt of such diplomas 
constitutes an outstanding achievement. Bylaws of another international association to which the 
Petitioner belongs also identify receipt of a fine arts diploma as a basis for membership. 
Online information about the third association to which the Petitioner belonged states: "Membership 
Eligibility Portfolios are needed to apply. According to the artworks' relevance, noticeable 
achievements in the art world and being exhibited in various exhibitions is essential." The online 
information does not clearly state the criteria for association membership. The record does not indicate 
whether membership requires, in addition to display of artwork in various exhibitions, "noticeable 
achievements in the art world." See Marllantas, Inc. v. Rodriguez, 806 F. App'x 864, 869 (11th Cir. 
3 
2020) ( quoting Matter of Ho, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591 (BIA 1988)) ( finding that USCIS properly 
requires petitioners "to resolve any inconsistencies in the record by independent objective evidence"). 
Also, even ifthe third association requires noticeable achievements of its members, the record would 
not demonstrate that such achievements are "outstanding." Compare Noticeable, Merriam-Webster 
Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com (meaning "worthy ofnotice" or "likely to be noticed") 
with Outstanding, Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary, supra (meaning, in this context, "marked by 
eminence or distinction"). The online information therefore does not demonstrate that the association 
requires outstanding achievements of its members. See Kinuthia v. Rosenberg, No. CV 17-10255-
LTS, 2018 WL 1243955, at *5 (D. Mass. Mar. 8, 2018), aff'd sub nom. Kinuthia v. Velarde, No. 18-
1244, 2019 WL 11671989 (1st Cir. July 3, 2019) (stating that this criterion "requires 'outstanding 
achievement' and not merely membership in an association in the field"). 
The Petitioner argues that "outstanding achievement in the arts is inherently subjective." He asserts: 
"Given the subjective nature of the arts, the review and acceptance of an artist's work by respected 
associations should be acknowledged as a legitimate marker of outstanding achievement." 
But this criterion does not require evidence of the Petitioner's outstanding achievements. Rather, it 
requires evidence of his membership in "associations ... which require outstanding achievements." 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). Thus, the Petitioner must have had outstanding achievements before 
obtaining membership in the associations. Contrary to his argument, association membership itself 
cannot constitute an outstanding achievement under the criterion's parameters. Also, to the extent he 
seeks to submit "comparable evidence" to meet this requirement, the record lacks proof that this 
criterion does not "readily apply" to his occupation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4); see also 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.(2)(B)(l), Comparable Evidence ("A general unsupported assertion that the listed 
evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's occupation is not probative.") 
The Petitioner also contends that SCOPS' consideration of his "extensively detailed" association 
memberships was "condensed into just one paragraph in the denial decision." He states: "This 
paragraph appears to have been directly copied from [SCOPS'] RFE [request for additional evidence] 
letter, without considering our explanations." 
SCOPS, however, was not required to "write an exegesis" on the Petitioner's evidence under this 
criterion. See Farah, 12 F.4th at 1329 ( citation omitted). The Agency need only "explain in writing 
the specific reasons for denial." 8 C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(l)(i). SCOPS stated: "While th[ese] associations 
[to which the Petitioner belongs] require professional achievements of [their] members, association 
bylaws do not state [that] they require outstanding achievements as a condition of membership." This 
sufficiently specified SCOPS' reason for finding this criterion unmet. See Farah, 12 F.4th at 1328 
(citation omitted) (stating that an agency need not specifically address each piece of evidence 
submitted). 
Moreover, contrary to this criterion's plain language, the record lacks evidence that recognized experts 
judged members of the associations to which the Petitioner belongs. The international association 
bylaws state that a "membership acceptance committee" and the association's board of directors 
review membership applications. But the Petitioner's evidence does not establish committee or board 
members as recognized experts in their fields. Regarding the other associations to which he belongs,

the record does not demonstrate the recognized expertise or even the identity of those who determine 
the organizations' memberships. See Nelson v. USCIS, No. CV 21-10334-LTS, 2022 WL 20583561,

The Petitioner's evidence did not demonstrate that the associations to which he belongs require

2. Published Material About the Petitioner 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a petitioner must submit "[p ]ublished material about the[ mselt] 
in professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the[ir] work in the field 
for which classification is sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, 
date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." Id. 
When adjudicating this criterion, users first determines whether published material relates to a 
petitioner and their specific work in the field. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), 
Criterion 3. Material covering a broader topic meets the criterion ifthe material includes "a substantial 
discussion of the person's work in the field and mentions the person in connection to the work." Id. 
users then considers whether a publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication. Id. Relevant factors include, for professional and major trade 
publications, their intended audiences and, for major trade publications and other major media, their 
relative circulations, readerships, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of several published articles about himself and his artwork. SCOPS 
found that a 2021 online article about him in a major U.S. newspaper lacked "a substantive discussion" 
of him and his work. We acknowledge that the article briefly discusses the Petitioner as one of several 
Middle Eastern artists included in an exhibition. But the article specifically mentions him and 
discusses his work in the particular collection. Thus, the article discusses his work substantially 
enough to satisfy this evidentiary criterion. See 6 USCIS Pol icy Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), Criterion 3 ( stating 
that published material covering a broader topic meets the criterion if the material includes "a 
substantial discussion of the person's work in the field and mentions the person in connection to the 
work"). 
Also, the Petitioner submitted articles solely about himself and his artwork that appeared in Turkish 
newspapers. SCOPS found "insufficient evidence to establish the circulation statistics for the 
publications." But the Petitioner's RFE response included circulation information about Turkish 
newspapers from 2019 through 2024. He therefore established the publication of some of these 
newspaper articles in major Turkish media. 
The Petitioner submitted published material in major media about himself and his work in his field. 
We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding.

C. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met the requisite minimum number of evidentiary requirements. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not consider his arguments regarding the other claimed criteria. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely 
advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). 
USCIS must now conduct a final merits determination. SCOPS did not make such a determination 
and, rather than issue one in the first instance, we will remand the matter. 
On remand, SCOPS must determine whether the Petitioner has sustained national or international 
acclaim and recognition ofhis achievements sufficient to establish him as one of that small percentage 
who has risen to his field's very top. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)(2). SCOPS should 
consider any potentially relevant evidence ofrecord, even ifit does not fit one ofthe regulatory criteria 
or was not presented as comparable evidence. Id. The Petitioner's burden ofproof includes explaining 
the significance of his evidence and how it demonstrates sustained national or international acclaim 
and recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval or denial should depend on the evidence's 
type and quality. Id.
