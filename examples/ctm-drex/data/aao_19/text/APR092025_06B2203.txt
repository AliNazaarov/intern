Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a sales and marketing specialist, seeks classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition, concluding that the record did not 
establish that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. 
The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

2. Published Material 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) calls for published material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought. The evidence must include the title, date, and author of the material, and any 
necessary translation. 
Published material should be about the person, relating to the person's work in the field, and not just about 
the person's employer and its work. Marketing materials created for the purpose of selling the person's 
products or promoting the person's services are not generally considered to be published material about 
the person. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
3 
The person and their work need not be the only subject of the material, provided that it mentions the 
person in a substantial discussion ofthe person's work in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner claimed to have "been profiled by some of the biggest and most widely read newspapers 
in the Arab media landscape." The Petitioner submitted translated copies of articles from newspapers in 
Kuwait, Jordan, Turkey, and the United Arab Emirates, published between 2008 and 2019. 
The Director concluded that most of the articles lack the author credit that the regulation requires, and 
other articles are not about the Petitioner, relating to his work in the field. On appeal, the Petitioner claims 
that Kuwaiti law does not require author credits on newspaper articles, and he maintains that the submitted 
articles quote him, name him, and relate to his work. 
Most of the submitted articles mention the Petitioner only briefly, providing his name and title but not 
including substantial discussion of his work in the field. Instead, the articles describe activities by the 
Petitioner's employers or organizations with which he has been involved. In the case of coverage of a 
2019 real estate fair, the Petitioner is one of several individuals shown in a published group photograph 
but his name does not appear in the articles. 
The Petitioner also submitted a printout from consisting ofa three-sentence biographical profile 
of the Petitioner. The Petitioner asserted that ._______. 'summarizes . . . well-known persons, 
businessm[ e ]n and dignitaries in [the] Arab world." The Petitioner did not establish that I I 
constitutes a professional or major trade publication or other major media. The submitted printout appears 
to be an entry from a directory or database with capsule information about listed individuals. The 
Petitioner also did not submit evidence of the inclusion criteria to show thaU Ionly lists "well­
known persons" as claimed. 
The article that devotes the most attention to the Petitioner is a three-paragraph story from the Kuwaiti 
news websiteO published without an author credit, stating that a real estate company "announced the 
appointment of [ the Petitioner] as a manager of one of the group's departments." Even then, the article 
focuses on assertions about the company's reputation and the services it offers, and provides few details 
about the Petitioner other than his name and new title. The promotional tone of the anonymous article is 
consistent with a press release from the company. Content that the person or the person's employer paid 
for does not generally meet the regulatory requirements. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l). The absence of an author credit is particularly significant in light of the apparently 
promotional nature of the article, because the article lacks a potential means to distinguish between 
journalistic coverage and what amounts to an advertisement for the Petitioner's then-employer. 
We agree with the Director that the Petitioner's evidence does not meet the regulatory requirements.

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten lesser criteria. As a result, we need not provide the type 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
1 See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (stating that, like courts, federal agencies are not generally required 
to make findings and decisions unnecessary to the results they reach); see also Matter ofL-A-C-, 26 l&N Dec. 516, 526 
n. 7 (BIA 2015) ( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible).

The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec. (Hamal II), No. 
19-cv-2534, 2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 
31, 2023) ( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective 
immigrants"); see also Hamal v. Dep 't of Homeland Sec. (Hamal I), No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 
2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial of petition of a 
published theoretical physicist specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that 
"[c]ourts have found that even highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. 
Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one ofthe most famous baseball 
players in Korean history" did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown a degree ofrecognition ofhis work that rises to the level ofsustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as 
contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b )(l)(A) 
of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one ofthe small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has submitted letters that indicate, usually in general terms, that he has had a significant 
and wide-ranging impact on his field. The record, however, does not corroborate these claims. Serious 
credibility issues arise from factors discussed further above, which further diminish the weight of the 
unsupported claims in the submitted letters. The Petitioner has provided evidence of several years of 
experience in various occupations, but that evidence does not consistently reflect sustained national or 
international acclaim at the top of the field as the statute and regulations demand. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
