Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a scientific researcher who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant 
classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a Ph.D. candidate seeking his doctoral degree in computer science. In the request for 
evidence (RFE) response, the Petitioner stated his "field of endeavor is the development of reliable 
and trustworthy large multimodal [artificial intelligence (AI)] models for e-commerce." 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met four of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and the authorship 
of scholarly articles but that he had not satisfied the criteria associated with published material or 
original contributions. On appeal, the Petitioner claims the same criteria that SCOPS denied. After 
reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude he has not demonstrated eligibility here. 
1. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii).). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, the 
Petitioner must establish the circulation statistics are high relative to other similar forms of media. 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion.

The Petitioner provided multiple articles and postings. SCOPS determined that he didn't meet the 
requirements of this criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner contests SCOPS' determination stating: 
The Director erred in concluding that the material was not "about" the Petitioner merely 
because the Petitioner's name did not appear in the headline. The regulation requires 
published material about the alien relating to the alien's work in the field; it does not 
require that the article be titled with the Petitioner's name. 
We note that SCOPS did not require that the Petitioner's name appear in the headline. USCIS policy 
indicates that "published material that covers a broader topic but includes a substantial discussion of 
the person's work in the field and mentions the person in connection to the work may be considered 
material about the person relating to the person's work." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8)(1 ), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. He further states in the appeal: 
[T]he MarkTechPost article discusses! Ia framework for which the Petitioner 
is the first author, and provides a direct connection to the underlying project and 
research identifying the Petitioner's role. When considered together with the authorship 
evidence in the record, the published materials clearly relate to the Petitioner and his 
work. 
We will not follow the Petitioner down this "winding path of connect-the-dots" to arrive at his 
preferred conclusion simply because his evidence cannot adhere to the clear regulatory text. See King 
v. Burwell, 576 U.S. 473, 497 (2015). Ultimately, the Petitioner hasn't demonstrated how articles 
about a project he worked on qualify as published material about him. While the articles may relate 
to his work, they do not identify or discuss the Petitioner personally and therefore do not meet the 
requirement that the published material be about the alien under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
If an article or report discusses a project, discovery, or creation without naming the individual, the 
material is about the work, not the person, and fails to meet the criterion's plain language requirements. 
Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 6931334, at *4 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) 
(upholding articles are primarily about scientists who headed the beneficiary's research team and about 
the research of the team as a whole, not about the beneficiary and his individual work); Kinuthia v. 
Rosenberg, No. CV 17-10255-LTS, 2018 WL 1243955, at *5 (D. Mass. Mar. 8, 2018), aff'd sub nom., 
Kinuthia v. Velarde, No. 18-1244, 2019 WL 11671989 (1st Cir. July 3, 2019), cert. denied, 140 S. Ct. 
2643, 206 L. Ed. 2d 714 (2020) (finding it is not enough that the material merely relates to the 
applicant's work). 
Our analysis aligns with SCOPS' ultimate determination here that the Petitioner has not submitted 
evidence that meets the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
2. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The

regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect

realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion.

In the appeal, the Petitioner also claims researchers have adopted his work and it has spurred follow

He also raises two best paper awards recognizing his work, notes they were awarded through 
competitive peer review, and they reflect that his research is regarded as outstanding by experts in the 
field. Absent is any indication of how this translates into a contribution in the field that is of major 
significance. 
The Petitioner contends that I Iconstitutes an original contribution of major significance 
because it addressed an unmet need in evaluating artificial intelligence systems within a specialized 
domain. The record supports part of that proposition. I !represents an original piece of 
technical work, and the evidence suggests that it offered a distinct methodology for assessing 
performance in an area where standardized evaluation had been limited. But originality is only the 
beginning of the inquiry and the regulation requires more when it asks whether the contribution has 
achieved major significance in the field. Based on the current record, the evidence does not carry that 
burden. 
The Petitioner's principal evidence of impact is thatl lusedl lin evaluating one of its 
reasoning models, but none of its other models before or since. That is no small accomplishment as
I lis among the world's leading artificial intelligence research organizations, and its decision to 
use a benchmark reflects a degree of confidence in the tool's technical soundness. But significance 
cannot be inferred solely from one company's single use of the tool, however prominent that company 
might be. The relevant question is not whether a distinguished organization found the benchmark 
useful on one occasion; it's whether it has materially shaped the field. 
The record does not demonstrate the requisite broader influence as it contains little evidence that
I lhas been independently adopted by multiple research laboratories, integrated into widely 
used evaluation pipelines, or relied upon as a standard benchmark by the broader artificial intelligence 
community. Nor has the Petitioner established on appeal that subsequent researchers routinely

evaluate their systems agains~ lor that it has become an accepted point of reference within its 
asserted specialty area of data science. The evidence instead reflects a contribution that has been 
recognized, but in a more limited way that falls short of this criterion's requirements. 
We base our conclusion on the absence of objective evidence demonstrating substantial influence. 
Technical merit and practical usefulness are important attributes, but without more they are not 
adequate evidence of major significance. And that distinction is an important one as many valuable 
research contributions advance their fields in incremental ways. This criterion is reserved for a 
narrower class of contributions to those whose significance has already been demonstrated through 
their effect on the field itself. Here,I I appears to be a capable and original benchmark that has 
attracted meaningful, though limited attention, and the evidence does not establish that it has met the 
higher standard the regulation contemplates. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that his contributions have had a sufficiently appreciable 
impact in the field, and as a result he has not met this criterion's requirements. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 O; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis ofthe legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. Patel 
v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) ( citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision)).
