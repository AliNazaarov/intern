Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, who intends to work as a cultural diplomacy expert, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)( 4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

When she filed the petition, the Petitioner submitted a brief statement and copies of identity and

In May 2025, USCIS issued a request for evidence (RFE), describing the eligibility criteria at 8 C.F.R.

USCIS denied the petition in August 2025, acknowledging the Petitioner's assertions but concluding that

On appeal, the Petitioner disputes the determinations in the denial notice, but does not establish that any 
ofUSCIS' conclusions were in error. We agree with SCOPS that the Petitioner did not submit adequate 
evidence to support her claims. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that she received a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x).

2. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 
The Petitioner submitted copies of certificates identifying her as an associate member of the Society for 
Peace Studies and Practice and the Institute of Chartered Mediators and Conciliators of Nigeria. The 
Petitioner did not submit any documentation ofthe associations' membership requirements or the process 
by which the associations judge candidates for membership. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that her "memberships . . . are selective and require significant 
academic and professional qualifications. USCIS overlooked the credentialing standards of these 
associations, which demonstrate that membership is based on outstanding achievements evaluated by 
recognized experts." But the Petitioner has not submitted any documentary evidence to establish that the 
associations have the "credentialing standards" that she claims. 
The burden of proving eligibility for the benefit sought remains entirely with the applicant. Section 291 
of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1361. The Petitioner's own unsupported claims do not meet this burden. 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the requirements of this criterion.

3. Published Material 
The regulation at 8 e.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) calls for published material about the alien in professional 
or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which 
classification is sought. The evidence must include the title, date, and author of the material, and any 
necessary translation. 
The Petitioner did not include published materials in her point-by-point discussion of the regulatory 
criteria. But elsewhere in her RFE response statement, the Petitioner asserted that she had "earned ... 
feature coverage in national media." users determined that the Petitioner had not submitted evidence 
to support this claim. 
On appeal, the Petitioner states: "Users improperly found no qualifying publications. Petitioner has 
been the subject of media features and professional coverage highlighting her cultural diplomacy 
initiatives. Regulatory language does not limit evidence to traditional press; trade and professional 
publications also qualify." 
The issue is not whether "trade and professional publications also qualify." The Petitioner has not 
submitted copies of any published materials, or even identified any publications that featured her. The 
Petitioner has offered only the bare assertion that she received "feature coverage in national media." We 
agree with users that this vague and unsubstantiated claim cannot suffice to meet her burden ofproof 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the requirements of this criterion.

In light of the above conclusions, the Petitioner does not meet the initial evidentiary requirement of

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
5 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a pet1t10ner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
The minimal evidence in the record does not meet the requirements of any of the regulatory criteria, 
and we will therefore dismiss the appeal.
