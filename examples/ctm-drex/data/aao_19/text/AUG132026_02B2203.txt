Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a health technology platform company, seeks classification for the Beneficiary as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that while the Petitioner showed the Beneficiary satisfied at 
least three of the initial evidentiary criteria, the record did not establish that he has garnered sustained 
national or international acclaim for his achievements and is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the technology entrepreneurship field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry into the United States 
will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D .D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established the Beneficiary's receipt of a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must show the Beneficiary satisfies at least three of the alternate 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed that the Beneficiary 
satisfied eight of the ten criteria pertaining to receipt of lesser recognized awards or prizes, 
membership, published materials, judging, authorship, artistic display, leading or critical role, and 
salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(iv), (vi)-(viii), and (ix). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) determined that the Beneficiary met six of the eight claimed 
criteria for published materials,judging, authorship, artistic display, leading or critical role, and salary. 
However, they found that the Beneficiary did not meet the criteria for receipt of lesser recognized 
awards or prizes and membership. Because they found the Beneficiary met at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria, they conducted a final merits determination. In doing so, SCOPS concluded that 
the record did not demonstrate the Beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim and that 
his achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating he is one ofthat small 
percentage who bas risen to the very top of the field. Therefore, they denied the petition.

B. Final Merits Determination 
As indicated above, we will evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, the Beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim, that he is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in 
the field through extensive documentation. In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's 
accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if his successes are sufficient to 
demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of 
the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that SCOPS misapplied the law, applied a heightened standard of 
review, and did not adequately consider the evidence. It argues that SCOPS' findings were conclusory 
and unsupported by the record. 
Upon de novo review, we agree with SCOPS' conclusion that the Petitioner has not established the 
Beneficiary's eligibility. While the evidence may demonstrate the Beneficiary's noteworthy 
accomplishments, it does not establish that he is an individual of extraordinary ability who has 
sustained national or international acclaim, and demonstrated that he is among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field of endeavor. 
As an initial matter, the Petitioner argues that, because SCOPS found the Beneficiary met at least three 
of the ten criteria, it "create[ d] a rebuttable presumption: the beneficiary meets the legal standard for 
eligibility and shifts the burden of proof to then obligate users to raise material challenges to 
specifically-identified requisite facts, or approve this petition." This is incorrect. Although the 
evidentiary standard in immigration proceedings is the lowest preponderance ofthe evidence standard, 
the burden is on the Petitioner alone to provide material, relevant, and probative evidence to meet that 
standard. Section 291 of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1361. Further, the regulations are clear that the filing 
party bears the burden ofproving eligibility by submitting evidence for consideration and adjudication. 
8 C.F .R. § 103 .2(b )(8)(i)-(iii). 
In petitions seeking classification as an individual of extraordinary ability, as referenced above, once 
a petitioner demonstrates the beneficiary satisfies at least three of the ten regulatory criteria, it must 
then also demonstrate to users that a favorable decision is warranted when considering all of the

C. The Petitioner's O-lA Visa 
We acknowledge the Petitioner's appellate assertion that the Beneficiary has an approved O-lA 
nonimmigrant visa petition, and that this classification is reserved for nonimmigrants of extraordinary 
ability. Under USCIS policy, the Beneficiary's receipt of 0-1 nonimmigrant classification is a 
relevant consideration and may be an indicator of eligibility for EB-1 classification but, as the 
Petitioner acknowledges, it is not determinative. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Prior approval of an 0-1 nonimmigrant visa does not preclude 
USCIS from denying an immigrant visa petition which is adjudicated based on a different statute, 
regulations, and case law. Each petition is separate and independent and must be adjudicated on its 
own merits, under the corresponding statutory and regulatory provisions. As discussed above and in 
SCOPS' denial, the evidentiary shortcomings in the record do not support a grant of this petition. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
