Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification on behalf of the Beneficiary as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that the Beneficiary had a major, 
internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that she met at least three ofthe ten regulatory 
criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 
C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition ofachievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that the Beneficiary has received a major, 
internationally recognized award, it must demonstrate that the Beneficiary satisfies at least three ofthe 
alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed the 
Beneficiary satisfied seven criteria. The Director concluded that the Beneficiary satisfied two of the 
claimed criteria related to judging and performing in a leading or critical role and therefore did not 
establish her eligibility for the classification. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(iv) and (viii). On appeal, the 
Petitioner claims the Beneficiary meets the remaining claimed criteria related to awards, membership, 
authorship of scholarly articles, display, and high salary. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)(i)-(ii), (vi)-(vii), 
and (ix). After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not established 
that the Beneficiary meets at least three categories of evidence. Therefore, she is not eligible for the 
extraordinary ability classification in the field of technology entrepreneurship for the reasons we will 
discuss below. 
A. Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 
USCIS determines if the person was the recipient of prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The description of this type of evidence in 
the regulation indicates that the focus should be on the person's receipt of the awards or prizes, as 
opposed to the employer's receipt of the awards or prizes. Id. USCIS determines whether the award 
is a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize or award which the person received for 
excellence in the field of endeavor. Id. Relevant considerations regarding whether the basis for 
granting the prizes or awards was excellence in the field include but are not limited to the criteria used 
to grant the awards or prizes, the national or international significance of the awards or prizes in the 
field, the number of awardees or prize recipients, and limitations on competitors. Id.

in this proceeding. However, even if we were to consider that venture capital could be considered a 
prize or award, this would not mean that any and all venture capital, from whatever source, is a 
nationally or internationally recognized prize or award.

Petitioner did not show that the venture capital funding resulted in prizes or awards from the investors 
rather than business investments and transactions between the two parties. Accordingly, the evidence 
does not demonstrate that the Beneficiary meets this criterion.

C. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly

manual. To determine whether the publication qualifies as a professional or major trade publication 
or other major media, we consider such relevant factors as the circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field and the intended audience (for professional journals). Id.

E. High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner contends the Beneficiary meets this criterion based on her position of "Full Stack 
Software Engineer, Trust (Level 4)" forl IThe offer letter confirms that the Beneficiary

Petitioner has not demonstrated that the Beneficiary meets this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(3 ), we cannot conclude that the Beneficiary is an alien of extraordinary ability and 
need not further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also 
Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the 
regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks 
an extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (stating 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an individual of extraordinary ability.
