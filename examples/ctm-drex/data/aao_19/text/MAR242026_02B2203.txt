Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner was not the 
recipient of a major, internationally recognized award, and, did not satisfy at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria to meet the initial evidentiary requirements of the classification. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, In c., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204. 5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204. 5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir.2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation 
is frrst counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally recognized 
award under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). He must therefore satisfy at least three of the regulatory criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Based on our de novo review, the Petitioner has not met this showing. 
As an initial matter, we note that the Petitioner's original filing did not identify his field of 
extraordinary ability. On his Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers, the Petitioner did not 
provide his occupation but claimed his job title is that of a chief executive with the Standard 
Occupational Code (SOC Code) 11-1011, which is for top executives. 1 In his undated personal 
statement, submitted in response to SCOPS' first request for evidence, the Petitioner claimed he is an 
individual of extraordinary ability in the field of civil engineering with a specialization in water 
infrastructure and environmental sustainability. In its decision, SCOPS determined the Petitioner 
materially changed his field and reviewed the evidence using his initial field of water supply. On 
appeal, the Petitioner does not address this finding. Rather he proceeds to argue his eligibility in the area 
of civil engineering. As the Petitioner specifically indicated he is a top executive, we interpret his area of 
exceptional ability as that of an executive with a specialization in water infrastructure.2

B. Evidentiary Criterion Withdrawn 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner met the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), which requires 
evidence of his participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the 
same or an allied field of specification for which classification is sought. In support of this criterion, 
the Petitioner provided certificates that he served on the State Attestation Commission for the 
protection of final qualifying works and diploma projects of students completing their bachelor's 
degree for the 2016-2017 and 2017-2018 academic years. The certificates also list him as a reviewer 
of two books that were subsequently published. According to one of the Petitioner's personal 
statements, his role at the commission involved "evaluating final qualifying works and diploma 
projects of students specializing in water supply and sewerage systems, ensuring that future 
professionals in the field met the highest academic and technical standards." He added that each 
evaluation required "a thorough review of innovative approaches and methodologies, emphasizing 
sustainable water infrastructure, operational efficiency, and environmental considerations." He also 
explained his role as a reviewer involved "ensuring" that educational materials, including textbooks, 
reflected the latest industry standards and were aligned with the evolving challenges of water 
management. While we acknowledge his statements, he did not provide independent corroborating 
information on his role at the State Attestation Commission and as a reviewer to demonstrate that 
"evaluating final works," and "ensuring educational materials reflect the latest industry standards," for 
example, meet the plain language of the criterion requiring judging the work of others. We therefore 
withdraw SCOPS' determination to the contrary.

SCOPS also determined the Petitioner did not meet the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), which 
requires evidence of performing in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that 
have a distinguished reputation. For a leading role, officers look at whether the evidence establishes 
that the person is ( or was) a leader within the organization or establishment or a division or department 
thereof. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). For a critical role, officers look 
at whether the evidence establishes that the person has contributed in a way that is of significant 
importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or 
department of the organization or establishment. Id. Letters from persons with personal knowledge 
of the significance of the person's leading or critical role can be paiiicularly helpful in making this 
determination, so long as the letters contain detailed and probative information that specifically 
addresses how the person's role for the organization, establishment, division, or department was 
leading or critical. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(g)(l) (Evidence relating to qualifying experience or training 
shall be in the form of letter(s) from current or former employer(s) ... and shall include a specific 
description of the duties performed by the alien); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l).

2. Issues Reserved 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not establish he meets the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), 
requiring evidence of published material about the Petitioner in professional or major trade 
publications or other major media, relating to the Petitioner's work in the field for which classification 
is sought; and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), requiring evidence of the Petitioner's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field. As the 
Petitioner would be unable to establish three of the ten threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude he is an alien of extraordinary ability and reserve our 
analysis of the remaining two criteria as well as a final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20 ( discussing a two-step review, where if the required number of criteria are met 
by the documentation, then it is considered in the context of a final merits determination).
