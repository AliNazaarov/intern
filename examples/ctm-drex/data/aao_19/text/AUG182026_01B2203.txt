Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software developer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
the initial evidentiary requirements, it did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner has sustained 
national or international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. 
The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

The Petitioner argues on appeal that the denial decision identified her field inconsistently, referring 
variously to "information technology," "software engineering," and "computer engineering." The 
Petitioner asserts that the terms "are not interchangeable," and that "[a] final-merits determination cannot 
be reasoned if the field itself changes during the analysis." 
But the Petitioner, on appeal, does not establish that the differing terminology changed the outcome of 
the decision. In this regard, we note that, to establish that she earns a high salary under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix), the Petitioner submitted Department of Labor data for "Software Developers" and 
Glassdoor data for a different position title, "Senior Machine Leaming Engineers." 
USCIS determined: 'The evidence indicates that the petitioner has some recognition but is not persuasive 
that the petitioner's achievements set her significantly above almost all others in her field." USCIS 
concluded that, while the Petitioner had submitted enough evidence to satisfy the facial requirements of 
three or more of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), the evidence ofrecord does not show 
the required level of acclaim and recognition. USCIS also observed that some activities that nominally 
satisfy particular criteria, such as judging and authorship of scholarly articles, are essentially routine in 
certain occupations and the Petitioner had not shown that her work in those areas distinguished her from 
others in her field. 
The Petitioner contends that, in the final merits determination, USCIS did not consider the record as a 
whole, instead re-evaluating each of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner 
asserts that the evidence under each claimed criterion need not independently establish sustained national 
or international acclaim. 
We agree with the general point that not every piece of evidence must establish acclaim. But it is still 
necessary to look at the various elements ofthe record to determine whether, as a whole, they demonstrate 
that the Petitioner has submitted extensive documentation of recognition, achieved sustained national or 
international acclaim, and reached the very top ofthe field as the statute and regulations require. 
Before denying the petition, USCIS issued a request for evidence (RFE). In that RFE, relating to the 
'judging" criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), USCIS stated: "The weight given to evidence submitted 
to fulfill the criterion . . . depends on the extent to which such evidence demonstrates, reflects, or is 
consistent with sustained national or international acclaim at the very top ofthe alien's field ofendeavor." 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that this standard blurs the line between the first step of adjudication, 
which should focus only on the facial requirements of the initial criteria, and the second step, the final 
merits determination, which concerns the larger question of whether the record as a whole establishes 
sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner contends that the language in the RFE 
3 
"demonstrates that the adjudication was infected from the outset by a conflated legal standard. Even 
though USCIS later accepted the judging criterion, the RFE's framework shows that the agency was 
evaluating threshold evidence through an improper merits lens from the beginning. That same legal error 
carried into the denial." 
The subject of the appeal before us, however, is not the RFE, but the subsequent denial decision. In that 
denial decision, USCIS concluded that the Petitioner had met the requirements of the judging criterion. 
The Petitioner does not identify any language in the denial decision that improperly called for evidence 
ofacclaim in the context ofthe initial criteria. The claim that the "same legal error carried into the denial" 
is a general assertion that identifies no specific error in the denial decision. 
In the denial decision, when discussing the initial criteria, USCIS raised the subject of"recognition" only 
in the context of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), which requires receipt of "nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards." USCIS discussed "impact" or "influence" only when discussing 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v), which requires evidence of"original ... contributions ofmajor significance in the field." 
In doing so, USCIS remained within the confines ofthe respective criteria. 
The Petitioner protests: "USCIS discounted already-granted criteria at final merits by adding extra­
regulatory demands." For example, the Petitioner states: 
USCIS accepted the judging criterion. The Policy Manual specifically recognizes peer 
review and similar gatekeeping activities as qualifying judging evidence where the record 
shows the reviews were requested and completed. Yet at final merits USCIS discounted 
this evidence because Petitioner had not shown a substantial history of review requests, 
editorial-board service, or chairing technical committees. The regulation does not require 
any of those things. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
USCIS was entitled to decide what weight the judging evidence deserved in the totality, 
but it was not entitled to transform an accepted criterion into a more demanding, different 
evidentiary category. 
The final merits determination considers the totality ofthe evidence in the broader context ofsustained 
national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to the 
antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence" 
"might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very top of his or 
her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
The final merits determination hinges on whether the Petitioner has established sustained national or 
international acclaim and has reached the small percentage at the very top of the field. This 
determination is related to, but separate from, the antecedent question of whether the Petitioner has 
satisfied at least three of the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). It is within this broader 
context that USCIS acknowledged the Petitioner's judging activity while also concluding that the 
Petitioner had not shown that such activity placed her at the very top of the field.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), ajf'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at * 1 (D.D.C. June 
3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial ofpetition ofa published theoretical physicist 
specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even 
highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" 
did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, while the Petitioner has established a successful career with major companies, she has not shown 
a degree ofrecognition of her work that rises to the level of sustained national or international acclaim 
and demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. 
No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act. Moreover, the record does 
not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
