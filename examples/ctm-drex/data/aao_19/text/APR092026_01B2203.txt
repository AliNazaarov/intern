I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in "that small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a 
beneficiary's sustained acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not 
submit this evidence, then the petitioner must provide sufficient qualifying documentation showing 
that the beneficiary meets at least three of the ten categories of evidence listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
authorship of scholarly articles). The regulation provides that if the criteria listed under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows 
the submission of comparable evidence. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidentiary requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows the beneficiary's 
sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that he or she is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 
2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, iffolfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not establish the Beneficiary had received a major, 
internationally recognized award. On appeal, the Petitioner does not specifically challenge this 
finding, it has therefore waived this claim. See, e.g., Matter ofM-A-S-, 24 I&N Dec. 762, 767 n.2 
(BIA 2009). As such, to meet the initial evidentiary requirements, the Petitioner must present evidence 
showing that the Beneficiary satisfies at least three of the criteria listed under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). In the decision denying the petition, SCOPS determined that the 
Petitioner had satisfied two criteria by submitting "[p ]ublished material about [the Beneficiary] in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to [his] work in the field for 
which classification is sought," as well as"[e ]vidence that the [Beneficiary] has performed in a leading

B. Final Merits Determination 
Upon remand, if SCOPS concludes that the Petitioner has presented evidence satisfying the initial 
evidentiary requirements, SCOPS should consider the totality ofthe material provided in a final merits 
determination and assess whether the record shows the Beneficiary's sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrates that he is among the small percentage at the very top of "the 
field oftechnology entrepreneurship." See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115; see also Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 
3d at 131-32; Rijal, 772 F. Supp. 2d at 1339. 
According to the Beneficiary's resume, he founded the pet1t10ning entity in January 2024, 
approximately a year and a half before filing the petition. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(2) (providing that 
a "petitioner must establish that he or she [ or it] is eligible for the requested benefit at the time of filing 
the benefit request and must continue to be eligible through adjudication"). The evidence the 
Petitioner has offered in support ofthe petition all relates to the Beneficiary's role and work associated 
with the petitioning entity, and all the relevant events that the Petitioner claims confirm the 
Beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim occurred within the year and a half time 
frame. Prior to his founding of the petitioning entity, the Beneficiary was an "intern," "research 
assistant," "coding instructor" and was in a "STEM fellowship." The Petitioner has not alleged, and

the record does not demonstrate, that these positions are in "the field of technology entrepreneurship." 
The Beneficiary's resume further reveals that he began undergraduate studies in computer science in 
September 2022, approximately a year before he founded the petitioning entity, and that he "dropped 
out" of school before completing his studies. 
Given the structure of the regulation and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, the satisfaction 
of the initial evidentiary requirements alone does not automatically or presumptively establish 
eligibility. In a final merits determination, SCOPS should evaluate whether the Petitioner has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, the Beneficiary's sustained national or 
international acclaim, that he is among the small percentage at the very top of "the field of technology 
entrepreneurship," and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. Significantly, USCIS Policy Manual provides: "According to Black's Law 
Dictionary (11th ed. 2019), the definition of sustain is 'to support or maintain, especially over a long 
period of time' ... [ and to] 'persist in making ( an effort) over a long period of time."' See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(l) n.5, https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f­
chapter-2; see also Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); Kazarian, 596 F.3d 
at 1119-20. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Based on the issues we have discussed, including SCOPS did not fully address evidence the Petitioner 
had presented concerning the Beneficiary's "participation ... as a judge," referenced under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv), we are remanding the matter to allow SCOPS to consider all materials in the record 
and for the entry of a new decision. Upon remand, if SCOPS concludes that the Petitioner has 
presented evidence satisfying the initial evidentiary requirements, SCOPS should consider the totality 
of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record confirms the 
Beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that he is among the small 
percentage at the very top of "the field of technology entrepreneurship." See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1115; see also Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131-32; Rijal, 772 F. Supp. 2d at 1339. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
