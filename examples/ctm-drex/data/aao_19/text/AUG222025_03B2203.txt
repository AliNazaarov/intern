Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a researcher/beamline scientist, requests classification under the employment-based, 
first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). 
Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their 
fields. Section 203(b )( I )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
the Petitioner received a one-time achievement of a major, internationally recognized award, or, in the 
alternative, that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is 
now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the filed of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met only two (judging either individually or on a panel the 
work of others in the same or an allied field under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and authorship of 
scholarly articles in the field in professional or major trade publication or other major media under 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi)) of the five claimed categories of evidence. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts 
that she fulfills the three remaining claimed criteria. Any ground of ineligibility that is not raised on 
appeal is waived. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 l&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) ( citing Matter ofR­
A-M-, 25 l&N Dec. 657, 658 n.2 (BIA 2012)). 
We do not disturb SCOPS' determination that the record satisfies the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (vi). Furthermore, for the reasons discussed below, we conclude that the record 
satisfies the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). Therefore, the record satisfies at least three of 
the 10 initial evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Because the record satisfies at 
least three initial evidentiary criteria, we reserve our opinion regarding whether it satisfies the 
remaining claimed criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) and (v). See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 
25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). However, for the reasons discussed below, the record does not 
show sustained national or international acclaim or that the Petitioner is among the small percentage 
at the top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1110-20 ( citing section 203(b)(l)(A) of 
the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) and (3)).

Finally, the record reflects that the Petitioner provided recommendation and reference letters. 
However, the letters are not sufficiently persuasive. The letters generally highlight the Petitioner's 
work and indicate the authors' personal implementation of the Petitioner's work into their own work. 
Letters that make general and expansive statements regarding the beneficiary and the beneficiary's 
accomplishments are generally not persuasive. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(3). The letters do not claim, nor does the content show, that the Petitioner achieved national 
or international acclaim from the field. Moreover, the letters do not demonstrate that the Petitioner 
has attracted a level of attention placing her the very top of her field. In addition, the letters do not 
show that she has enjoyed a career of acclaimed work. See Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & 
Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (finding testimonial letters were 
insufficient to establish his relative status in the field and do not compare him to others). 
Here, the letters do not contain sufficient information and explanation to show that the Petitioner is 
viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper echelon or that she has 
garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 
2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that none of the plaintiff's letters point to 
evidence of extensive documentation of his accomplishments to show sustained national or 
international acclaim). Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's work, they do not reflect a career 
of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international acclaim. See 
Bodhankar v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 
777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the submitted letters failed to establish the plaintiff's 
contributions resulted in significant acclaim). 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2) (providing 
that some evidence weighs more favorably on its own, while other evidence is more persuasive when 
viewed with other evidence). Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
6 
individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary 
ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an 
exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has 
not shown the significance of her work is indicative of the required sustained national or international 
acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b)(l)(A) of 
the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for 
the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that she is among the small 
percentage at the top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Because the Petitioner does not establish on appeal that the totality of the material provided 
demonstrates both sustained national or international acclaim and that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, the Petitioner is not eligible for the requested 
classification. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 (citing section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(2) and (3)). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The evidence meets at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). However, the 
totality of the record does not support a conclusion that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and 
recognition required for the classification sought. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. U.S. 
Citizenship and Immigration Services has long held that even athletes performing at the major league 
level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of her work 
is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-7223, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The record does not otherwise demonstrate 
that the Petitioner has garnered the national or international acclaim in the field and that she is one of 
the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) 
of the Act; see also 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
