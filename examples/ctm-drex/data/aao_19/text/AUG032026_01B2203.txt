Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a hammered dulcimer artist and music educator, seeks classification as an individual 
ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not satisfy at least three of the IO initial 
evidentiary criteria. USCIS also determined that the record did not establish that the Petitioner would 
continue to work in the area of claimed extraordinary ability. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of their achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 10 categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). If a criterion at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) does not readily apply to 
an occupation, petitioners may submit comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010).

A. Membership in Associations in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
USCIS acknowledged that the Petitioner is a member of the Chinese Musical Instrument Association 
(CMIA) and the Chinese Musicians Association (CMA). However, USCIS observed that the record 
does not establish that either association requires outstanding achievements of their members, as 
judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. USCIS farther 
stated that the Petitioner's elected positions in the CMIA and the Chinese Ethnic Minorities Vocal 
Music Society (CEMVS) exceed the entities' general membership requirements and, thus, they would 
"go beyond the substantive or evidentiary requirements set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii)." Based 
on those issues, USCIS determined that the record does not satisfy this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that, although USCIS' analysis was "textually accurate," it 
nevertheless "did not folly consider the context of professional associations in creative and artistic 
fields, nor did it review all pertinent evidence submitted." More specifically, the Petitioner claims that 
the CMIA and CMA bylaws' references to "certain accomplishments" or "certain influence" are "the 
standard practice across China's premier creative arts societies ... as the standard formulation for 
peer-evaluated outstanding achievement." The Petitioner also asserts that USCIS "may not have 
reviewed the evidence pertaining to the [CEMVS], an association for which the Petitioner was 
appointed as an Expert Consultant." 
Here, neither the CMIA nor the CMA bylaws, as translated into English in the record, show that they 
require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international 
experts in their disciplines or fields. Moreover, we are unpersuaded by the Petitioner's reference to 
"the standard practice across China's premier creative arts societies." Although similar Chinese 
associations in various fields may routinely use language like "certain accomplishments" or "certain 
influence," the CMIA and CMA bylaws nevertheless do not define these terms in a way that may show 
these particular associations require outstanding achievements of their members, as opposed to 
ambiguous "certain accomplishments" or "certain influence." Thus, even ifusing these phrases were 
"the standard practice across China's premier creative arts societies" as the Petitioner claims, the 
standard practice does not satisfy a plain-language reading ofthis criterion's requirements. Moreover, 
if the standard practice across creative arts societies is to require the same accomplishments or 
influence as other creative arts societies, such a practice would not appear to require outstanding 
achievements but, rather, the same standard accomplishments or influence as all other members of all 
other creative arts societies. 
And contrary to the Petitioner's claim on appeal, USCIS addressed his elected position in the CEMVS. 
More importantly, the Petitioner has not established that his membership in a vocal music society is 
material to his field as a hammered dulcimer educator, given that operating a hammered dulcimer­
or, more to the point, educating others to do so-is not a vocal music performance.

B. Original Contributions of Major Significance in the Field 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
USCIS acknowledged that the Petitioner published books and musical compositions, and that he has 
worked as an editor and editorial committee member. However, USCIS noted that the record does not 
establish that his published books or musical compositions "have garnered significant attention and/or 
utilization throughout the field" or that he was offered his editorial positions "because [hi] madel 
contributions of major significance." USCIS also noted that, although the Petitioner is among 
I l" the record does not establish whether "the 
wider music field considers this recognition commensurate with making contributions of major 
significance." USCIS further observed that the Petitioner has had speaking engagements in 2023 and 
2024, and that his published works have received citations. However, USCIS concluded that the 
record does not establish how the two speaking events demonstrate contributions of major 
significance, given that "those in [his] field are generally expected to attend meeting[s] or 
conferences," and USCIS noted that the record does not show that his published works have had major 
significance in the field. Based on those issues, USCIS determined that the record does not satisfy 
this criterion. 
We concur with USCIS' conclusion that the record does not establish the Petitioner's contributions to 
the field have been of major significance. For example, the record does not show how the Petitioner's 
publications, editorial contributions, judging roles, public speaking, and similar activities have 
provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others working in the field, rather than 
merely being acknowledged among other, similar contributions. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
As another example, the record does not contextualize the citations the Petitioner's publications 
received among the number of citations other authors received for similar publications, in order to 
show that his work has been highly cited relative to others' work in the field. See generally id. The 
record furthermore does not contextualize the substance ofthe citations, to clarify whether the citations 
the Petitioners' publications received are, on balance, favorable or critical of his contributions. 
The Petitioner claims on appeal that USCIS' analysis of his citation history "is methodologically 
invalid" because it compares information from the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) 
database to Google Scholar metrics, which he characterizes as "prioritiz[ing] English-language, 
international, and often scientific journals" and generally providing "poor coverage of Chinese 
publications." However, the Petitioner does not support his criticism of Google Scholar metrics' 
alleged flaws with credible, probative evidence. See Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76 
(providing that petitioners must establish their claims are more likely than not true by submitting 
relevant, probative, and credible evidence); 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(8)(i) (providing that USCIS will 
approve benefit requests only when the evidence of record-not information outside the record­
establishes eligibility). And more to the point, he does not otherwise show how his citations are high 
relative to others' work in the field, given that he does not establish-through probative, credible

evidence in the record-what the citation rates of others in the field are. See Matter of Chawathe, 
25 I&N Dec. at 375-76; 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(8)(i). The Petitioner claims that citation comparison 
"does not reflect the nature of my field, where citation volume is not an appropriate primary measure 
of impact."3 Rather, he states that "teaching, institutional adoption, and professional practice rather 
than citation metrics" measure contributions to the field of hammered dulcimer education. However,

See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 3 75-76; 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )(8)(i). 
The Petitioner asserts on appeal that an opinion letter written by I Icorroborates the 
Petitioner's claim that his contributions have been of major significance. Specifically, the Petitioner 
addresses the letter's statement that the Petitioner developed an "influential teaching program [with a] 
profound impact on music education in China." The Petitioner also quotes the letter's generalization 
that the Petitioner is "one of the very few leading experts in dulcimer and cross-cultural music in 
China." And the Petitioner references the letter's characterization of one of the Petitioner's articles as 
"one of the earliest and few truly specialized academic works on dulcimer in China [that] combined 
Western music theory with detailed explanations ... to chart new directions for dulcimer pedagogy." 
As a matter ofdiscretion, we may use opinion statements submitted by a petitioner as advisory. Matter 
of Caron Int'l, Inc., 19 I&N Dec. 791, 795 (Comm'r 1988). However, we may give an opinion less 
weight if it is not in accord with other information in the record or if it is in any way questionable. 
Id. We are ultimately responsible for making the final determination regarding an individual's 
eligibility for the benefit sought; the submission of expert opinion letters is not presumptive evidence 
of eligibility. Id.; see also Fed. R. Evid. 702 (providing that an expert opinion may "help the trier of 
fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue," not that it is evidence of fact itself). 
Here, the opinion letter froml lbears minimal probative value because it purports to be 
evidence of fact, rather than helping us understand evidence to determine a fact in issue. See Fed. R. 
Evid. 702. For example, although the letter claims that the Petitioner developed an influential teaching 
program, it does not address evidence of the influence the Petitioner's teaching program had and how 
that evidence may establish such a fact. See id. Likewise, although the letter asserts that the Petitioner 
is "one of the very few leading experts in dulcimer and cross-cultural music in China," it does not 
reference evidence in the record that may establish such a fact, for example credible and authoritative 
publications that recognize the Petitioner as such. See id. Furthermore, although the letter states that 
I Ibelieves the Petitioner wrote "one of the earliest and few truly specialized academic 
works on dulcimer education in China [that] combined Western music theory with detailed 
explanations ... to chart new directions for dulcimer pedagogy," that opinion is not supported by 
probative, documentary evidence of specialized academic works on dulcimer education in China that 
may place the Petitioner's article among the earliest. See id. Moreover, even if the Petitioner wrote 
one of the earliest such academic works, the opinion letter does not establish how being early relative 
to others is of major significance in the field of hammered dulcimer education.

Because the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
which is dispositive, we reserve our opinion regarding whether the Petitioner would continue to work 
in the area of claimed extraordinary ability. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the

alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
