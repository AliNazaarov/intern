Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software engineer, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner 
met three of the initial ten evidentiary criteria for this classification listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work m the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a software engineer in I I Canada. At the time of filing he was employed by 
Twilio in this capacity. He indicates that he intends to continue to work in this role in the United 
States. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claims to have met four criteria relating to lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized awards, participation as a judge of the work of others in the 
field, performing a leading or critical role, and commanding a high salary, under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (viii), and (ix), respectively. 
SCOPS determined, and we agree, that the Petitioner satisfied two of the four claimed initial 
evidentiary requirements relating to judging the work ofothers in the field and to performing a leading 
or critical role. However, SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner had not shown that he met the 
remaining two claimed criteria relating to lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards and 
to commanding a high salary, and therefore that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the ten 
evidentiary criteria for extraordinary ability, as required.

excellence in the field of endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l),

Finally, the Petitioner again argues that the standards of this criterion do not readily apply to his 
occupation and therefore that we should consider comparable evidence in the record below to establish 
his eligibility. 1 The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) states "[i]fthe above standards do not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence to establish the 
beneficiary's eligibility." This regulation provides petitioners with the opportunity to submit 
comparable evidence to establish their eligibility, if it is determined that the evidentiary criteria 
described in the regulations do not readily apply to their occupation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). When evaluating such comparable evidence, officers must consider 
whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether 
the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. Id. 
Here, the Petitioner contends that this criterion is not readily applicable to his occupation due to the 
nature of the work that he performs. In a statement submitted with his RFE response, the Petitioner 
asserted that "[t]he projects and software programs that I develop are owned by my employer" and 
that accordingly he is "not allowed to publish or present my work to external parties" thus "limit[ing] 
my ability to be considered for lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards." With 
this RFE response, the Petitioner provided a sample confidentiality clause for his current employer 
showing that employees are prohibited from disseminating intellectual property. However, the 
Petitioner does not show or explain how the circumstances of his current employment demonstrate 
that the awards criterion is not readily applicable to the occupation of software engineer. 
Further, the Petitioner has not established that software engineers are unable to receive nationally or 
internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field of endeavor. The fact that the Petitioner 
did not receive such an award is not evidence that the criterion does not apply to his occupation. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner has not satisfied his burden to establish that the criterion does not readily 
apply to his occupation. Zizi v. Cuccinelli, No. 20-CV-07856-SVK, 2021 WL 2826713, at *8 (N.D. 
Cal. July 7, 2021) (holding that "it is Plaintiff's burden to demonstrate that the standards in [ (h )(3)] 
did not readily apply to his occupation before [U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS)] 
is required to consider comparable evidence"). In addition, the Petitioner has not shown how the 
submitted confidentiality agreement is "comparable" to the "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of 
lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor" 
required at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
For the reasons stated above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he satisfies this criterion.

a "high salary" or "significantly high remuneration." More comprehensive wage data is needed to 
effectively demonstrate high earnings relative to peers. 
The salary report from jobsbankcanada.ca submitted by the Petitioner similarly is insufficient to 
demonstrate that the Petitioner satisfies this criterion. According to this report, software engineers in 
thel ~ earn between $33.65 CAD and $84.13 CAD. The Petitioner contends that his 
hourly wage of $160 CAD demonstrates that he has commanded a high salary relative to others in his 
field. Here, the Petitioner does not show that the wages reported by jobsbankcanada.ca include 
bonuses and restricted stock such that this wage report reflects an "apples to apples" comparison. 
These salary reports are therefore insufficient to demonstrate that the Petitioner commands a high 
salary in relation to others in the field. See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, 
at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 
5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) (finding plaintiff must show that salary reports reflect an apples to 
apples, rather than an apples to oranges, comparison). 
Regarding the salary report from moneygenius.ca/blog/top-1-income-canada, which the Petitioner 
contends shows that he is in the top two percent of earners in Canada and therefore reflects that he 
satisfies this criterion, the Petitioner has not offered sufficient evidence to show how being in such a 
bracket reflects that he has commanded a high salary relative to others in his field. 
Finally, the Petitioner contends on appeal that "USCIS' interpretation that local or national salary 
levels do not allow for an appropriate basis for comparison in determining a high salary" does not 
comply with the USCIS Policy Manual. Specifically, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS' decision 
imposed a novel evidentiary requirement because 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l), offers 
as an example of evidence relevant to high remuneration, "[ c ]omparative wage or remuneration data 
for the person's field, such as geographical or position-appropriate compensation surveys", and he 
provided geographically appropriate compensation surveys in the record below. This argument is not 
persuasive. SCOPS' decision indicated that it was the Petitioner's use of "average local or national 
salary levels" ( emphasis added) that did not allow for an appropriate basis for comparison in 
determining whether the Petitioner commanded a high salary relative to others in the field. As we 
explained in the foregoing analysis, average local or national average salaries are insufficient to 
establish that the Petitioner commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field. See Strategati, 
LLC, No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *7. Thus, while the record shows that the 
Petitioner offered geographical compensation surveys, these surveys provided average salary levels.

Accordingly, this evidence did not provide a sufficient basis for comparison to establish that the 
Petitioner commanded a high salary relative to others in the field. 
For the reasons stated above, the Petitioner has not shown that he meets this criterion. 
In summary, the Petitioner has not shown on appeal that he satisfies the evidentiary criteria related to 
lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards and to high salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) 
and (ix), respectively. Accordingly, as the Petitioner has demonstrated only two of the four criteria he 
claims to satisfy, he has not established that he satisfies at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria for 
extraordinary ability as 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) requires. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve arguments concerning 
a final merits determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
