Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a research-based biopharmaceutical company, seeks to classify the Beneficiary as an 
alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 
8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to 
those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner demonstrated that the Beneficiary 
meets at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, it did not establish that the 
Beneficiary has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and is among those individuals 
in the small percentage at the very top of her field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. The alien must seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area 
of extraordinary ability and the evidence must show that the alien's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of a beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Alternatively, the petitioner can submit 
evidence that the alien beneficiary meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly 
articles. If those standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once the initial evidence requirements have been met, the next step is a final merits determination, in 
which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of their field of endeavor. 
See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022); 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established the Beneficiary's receipt of a major 
internationally recognized award, the Petitioner must demonstrate that the Beneficiary satisfies at least 
three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner previously 
claimed that the Beneficiary could meet up to six of these ten criteria. USCIS determined that the 
Beneficiary met three criteria through documentation showing that she had judged the work of others 
in her field, made original contributions of major significance, and authored scholarly articles in 
professional publications. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (v), and (vi). 
Having concluded that the Beneficiary met this first evidentiary threshold, USCIS proceeded to a final 
merits determination, where they concluded that the Petitioner had not demonstrated, by a 
preponderance of the evidence, that the Beneficiary had garnered sustained national or international 
acclaim and that her achievements have been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating that 
she is one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Beneficiary satisfies additional criteria and that the 
evidence in the aggregate establishes her eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. We need 
not determine whether the Beneficiary satisfies additional evidentiary criteria because the appeal 
hinges on the final merits determination. 1 Therefore, we will evaluate the totality of the evidence 
based on the documentation presented to USCIS in the context ofthe final merits determination below. 
We further note that on appeal, the Petitioner cites Golan v. Allan, 22-10202 (E.D. Mich. July 31, 
2023), a district court decision that pertains to a case where USCIS determined that the petitioner did 
not meet the requirements of the original contributions criterion. To the extent that the cited decision 
focuses on a petitioner's ability to meet the elements of a specific criterion, the Petitioner's reliance 
on that decision is placed as meeting the elements of a specific criterion is substantially different from

B. Final Merits Determination 
Given the structure of the regulations and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, the satisfaction 
of at least three evidentiary criteria alone does not automatically or presumptively establish eligibility. 
In a final merits determination, we evaluate whether a petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance 
of the evidence, their beneficiary's sustained national or international acclaim, 2 that the beneficiary is 
among the small percentage at the very top of their field of endeavor, and that the beneficiary's 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. Therefore, we 
analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their 
successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 3 In this matter, we conclude that 
the Petitioner has not shown the Beneficiary's eligibility.

her Ph.D., the Beneficiary has worked as a clinical pharmacologist specializing in the field of 
pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics and is currently working for the Petitioner "in the clinical 
development of multiple autoimmune therapies." The Petitioner asserts that the Beneficiary "has 
established herself as a leading expert in clinical pharmacology" and has "directly contribut[ ed] to the 
approval and expansion of [it]'s immunology portfolio for the treatment of various immune-mediated 
diseases." The Petitioner stresses the value of the Beneficiary's work and abilities, stating that she 
"has had a direct impact on drug development and regulatory decision-making." 
As discussed further below, the Beneficiary has judged the work ofothers in her field through the peer 
review process, has made contributions of major significance, and has authored scholarly articles in 
professional publications. The Petitioner also provided an article about some of its proprietary
