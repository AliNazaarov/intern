Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a pharmacist, seeks immigrant classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner met at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, petitioners can demonstrate 
international recognition of their accomplishments in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If petitioners do not submit this evidence, then they 
must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows petitioners to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to their occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a pharmacist employed as the Director of Clinical Services at I ~ an 
independent pharmacy in Florida. The Petitioner holds a doctoral degree in pharmacy and has been 
working in her profession for over two years. The Petitioner claimed to meet five of the initial 
evidentiary criteria relating to lesser awards, published material, judging, original contributions, and 
high salary at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (iii)-(v), and (ix). SCOPS concluded the Petitioner met two 
criteria, but not the remaining three criteria she claimed to meet. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts 
SCOPS erred by not considering relevant evidence and claims she meets all five criteria. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not shown that she received a major, internationally recognized award, she 
must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS 
determined the Petitioner met the criteria relating to published material and judging, but did not meet 
the criteria related to lesser awards, original contributions, and high salary. The record supports 
SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner meets the criterion related to judging as it contains evidence 
that the Petitioner serves on the Editorial Advisory Board ofl I For the reasons 
discussed below, the record also supports SCOPS's determinations that the Petitioner does not meet 
the criteria for lesser awards and original contributions. The record does not, however, support 
SCOPS's determinations that the Petitioner met the criterion relating to published material, or that the 
Petitioner did not meet the criterion relating to high salary.

High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. Comparative wage 
or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location should be submitted to 
establish that a petitioner's compensation is high relative to others working in the field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted information from the Occupational Outlook Handbook stating that the 2023 
median pay for pharmacists was $136,030 a year. The Petitioner also submitted a printout from 
Salary.com stating the average salary of a retail pharmacist in the United States is $166,352, but can 
go up to $181,049. The Petitioner also submitted search results from the Florida Department of Labor 
stating the highest level of salary for pharmacists from July 2024 to June 2025 in North Florida where 
her pharmacy is located is $140,525. In response to SCOPS' RFE, the Petitioner also submitted an 
6 
article from drugchannels.net stating that the average salary of retail pharmacists was $126,990 in 
2023. 
The Petitioner submitted her 2023 Form W-2 Wage and Tax Statement showing she earned $208,846 
and pay statements indicating her total pay in 2023 was $218,600. She also submitted her 2024 Form 
W-2 Wage and Tax Statement showing she earned $277,926 and pay statements indicating her total 
pay in 2024 as of November 29, 2024 was $275,371. In response to SCOPS' RFE, the Petitioner 
submitted a letter from her employer who states her total wages were $208,846 in 2023 and $277,926 
in 2024 and the average salary for pharmacists in their region is approximately $120,000. 
The evidence shows the Petitioner has commanded a high salary in relation to other pharmacists and 
she meets this criterion. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring petitioners to 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show that their achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, the Petitioner has not met at least three ofthe initial evidentiary criteria and has not satisfied 
the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Accordingly, we cannot 
conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits under the 
statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 
1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
oftheir respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing towards the top. USCIS has long held 
that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary 
ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r. 1994). Here, the Petitioner 
has not shown that the significance and recognition of her work are indicative of sustained national or 
international acclaim as one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of her field, as 
extraordinary ability is described at section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The 
Petitioner is consequently ineligible for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability.
