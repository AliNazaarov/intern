Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a physical therapist from Brazil, seeks classification under the employment-based, 
first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, petitioners must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

3. Display of Work in the Field in Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence ofthe display ofthe alien's work in the 
field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." In addition to demonstrating that the displayed work was the 
alien's work product, the venues (virtual or otherwise) where the work was displayed must be artistic 
exhibitions or showcases. Although Merriam-Webster's online dictionary definition of "exhibition" 
includes public showings other than those that are artistic in nature, the plain language of the criterion 
includes the modifier "artistic" and explicitly requires that the exhibitions or showcases be artistic in 
nature. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). As such, various 
forms of artistic display, including visual art, performing arts, music, graphic art, and other examples of 
the fine arts, may satisfy this criterion's requirements and comparable evidence may be accepted as we 
evaluate the artistic nature of the display and venue. 
The Petitioner claimed that she is submitting comparable evidence to fulfill this criterion "since [her] 
endeavor is not related to the arts." She asserted that her scientific poster presentations at academic 
conferences, her scientific research that was publicly exhibited and peer-reviewed through international 
scientific conferences and symposia in poster and oral formats, and her participation in the 2025 
conference3 with her employer to demonstrate the functionality of beds, 
constitute comparable evidence to meet this criterion. 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows for submission of comparable evidence to establish 
eligibility if the standards of the initial evidence at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to 
the petitioner's occupation. When evaluating such comparable evidence, USCIS must consider whether 
the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence 
provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
Here, the Petitioner's sole reason for claiming comparable evidence under this criterion is that her 
occupation is not artistic. But an inability to meet a certain criterion is not persuasive explanation for how 
the criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's occupation. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
does not require meeting of all ten regulatory categories, but at least three of the ten categories. The ten 
categories in the regulations, therefore, are designed to cover different areas; not every criterion will apply 
to every occupation. While some special and unusual display or events that are non-artistic might qualify 
as comparable evidence under this criterion, scientific poster exhibits or conferences are typical and 
inherent to the health and science related fields for which other criteria are available. In claiming 
"comparable evidence," the Petitioner must show that her evidence is truly be comparable to the type of 
conference, as described in the record, appears to attract healthcare organizations and clinicians to present 
advanZements in medical technology and patient care. 
6 
3 
exclusive artistic showcase or exhibit that would be required of a visual artist. But the Petitioner has not 
persuasively addressed why her evidence showing that her oral and poster presentations in science and 
health conferences and symposiums should be deemed comparable to the type of evidence specified by 
the display criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F .2(B)(l ). 
In sum, the Petitioner has not submitted sufficient documentary evidence that conferences or

C. Reserved Issues 
The Petitioner has not satisfied eight of the claimed ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) and 
therefore does not meet the initial evidence requirement for this classification. Therefore, we reserve our 
opinion on the remaining two claimed evidentiary criteria as such discussion cannot change the outcome 
ofthis appeal. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are 
not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate 
decision). 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
12 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for 
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here.
