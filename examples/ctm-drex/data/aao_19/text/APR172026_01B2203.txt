Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an economist and former politician from Armenia, seeks classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 
203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

On remand, SCOPS should conduct a final merits review based on the totality of the evidence, 
evaluating whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained 
national or international acclaim, his status as one of the small percentage at the very top of his field 
of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) ofthe Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. In doing so, SCOPS should examine various fields of expertise claimed by the 
Petitioner, as indicated in the USCIS policy manual: "[t]o qualify as a person with extraordinary 
ability, the beneficiary must intend to continue to work in the area of his or her expertise." See 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(A)(2). 
We note here that the parameters of the Petitioner's expertise appear broad, encompassing legislative 
and public affairs, economic policy advising, and business development consulting. The Petitioner 
has also maintained multiple areas of intended employment. For instance, the Petitioner stated that he 
will work as an economic policy advisor, "continuing to advise local governments, nonprofit 
organizations, and small-to-medium enterprises on strategies for economic growth, policy innovation, 
and administrative modernization" through hisl I. At the same, the Petitioner 
asserted that "alongside his policy consulting work, he will continue serving as a business development 
consultant, supporting entrepreneurs and early-stage businesses" in the areas of market entry 
strategies, financial modeling, and organizational design. In addition to economic policy consulting 
and business development consulting, the Petitioner discussed several of his other business ventures 
in the field of healthcare and automotive service industry and stated that "he envisions an integrated 
ecosystem where his consulting, healthcare, and service businesses support each other." Hence, it is 
difficult to determine whether the Petitioner's intended employment falls sufficiently within the 
bounds of his area of extraordinary ability. Id. 
We acknowledge the Petitioner's contention that his fields of expertise share some common 
characteristics. Therefore, in making a final merits determination, SCOPS should consider the totality 
of the material and give proportionate weight to evidence of acclaim in all areas of expertise he has 
claimed2 even though the Petitioner's evidence of acclaim as a legislator may not suffice by itself to 
establish extraordinary ability as a consultant in economic policy and business development. SCOPS 
must ultimately decide whether the Petitioner has shown that his economic consulting and business 
development activities he plans to engage in the United States, in addition to his former political career, 
are at a level consistent with sustained national or international acclaim, that he is among the small 
percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in 
the field through extensive documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1115-20.
