Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes 
immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through 
extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de 
novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we 
will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claims he earned a foreign bachelor's degree in engineering, then a master's degree in 
2014 and a Ph.D. in Biological and Agricultural Engineering in 2021, each from U.S. institutions. In 
2023, he began working as an engineering technologist for a state university where he is responsible 
for developing agricultural robots, novel sensors, and imaging technologies for high throughput 
phenotyping and digital agriculture among other responsibilities. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met three of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria relating to judging and the authorship 
of scholarly articles but that he had not satisfied the contributions ofmajor significance criterion. After 
reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude he has not demonstrated he is eligible for this 
highly restrictive immigrant classification. 
1. Original Contributions of Major Significance under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 134 (D.D.C. 2013)). The 
regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. 
Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision is to be given effect 
and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or 
to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed his citation record demonstrates his contributions to the field 
when compared to the average citation percentiles for certain years. As evidence, the Petitioner relied 
2 
on material from Clarivate Analytics to show his work had garnered "the highest citation percentiles." 
SCOPS questioned why the Petitioner relied on the overly broad category of Engineers that included 
specializations that did not apply to the Petitioner's field of agricultural engineering in which a more 
appropriate comparator might be agricultural sciences. SCOPS also noted Clarivate Analytics 
indicated: "Citation frequency is highly skewed with many infrequently cited papers and relatively 
few highly cited papers. Consequently, citation rates should not be interpreted as representing the 
central tendency of the distribution." SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the 
requirements of this criterion. In his appellate response, the Petitioner emphasizes that a skewed 
citation frequency is natural to citation distributions. Even so, that does not address that he relied on 
the incorrect category in which to compare to his citations. 
Turning to the actual citations to his work, the Petitioner notes that those citing to his work come from 
six different countries. Citations from international researchers are insufficient to establish an original 
contribution of major significance. The statutory and regulatory framework requires objective 
evidence that the Petitioner's work has materially influenced the field as a whole, not merely that it 
has been used or acknowledged. International attention may show geographic reach, but it does not, 
by itself, satisfy the elevated "major significance" standard. In today's highly collaborative, digitally 
connected scientific environment, cross-border citation and data sharing are routine and expected. As 
a result, international citations are better understood as a natural consequence of modem scholarship 
than as reliable indicators that a contribution meets this criterion. Absent additional evidence showing 
that these citations reflect paradigm-shifting methodologies or foundational advances recognized by 
the broader scientific community, they are inadequate to demonstrate an original contribution ofmajor 
significance. 
To illustrate his citations were high when compared to the average, the Petitioner's appellate brief 
included a chart showing his articles were more frequently cited than what he characterizes as the 
average. But it is unclear from where the Petitioner found then copied and pasted this chart. At any 
rate, we view a comparison of citations to individual scientific articles to be more relevant for this 
criterion to establish the overall field's general view of a contribution of major significance. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual (providing that 
"published research that has ... been highly cited relative to others' [individual] work in that field, 
may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of endeavor"). 
We note agency policy here does not reference the entire body of work of other researchers compared 
with a petitioner's body of work as he advances in this petition. Rather, USCIS policy focuses on 
whether a petitioner's individual published article is highly cited relative to other researcher's 
individual work in the field. See also Gadhave v. Thompson, No. 3:21-CV-2938-D, 2023 WL 
6931334, at *5 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 19, 2023) (finding although the petitioner provided evidence showing 
that a few of his articles have received several times more citations than average, the record did not 
indicate what number of citations was being compared to: all articles within his specific field, all 
articles written by researchers of his age or level of expertise, or all scientific articles ever published). 
SCOPS mentioned such a comparison and on appeal the Petitioner pushes back on this method 
claiming the proper comparison would be to the field as a whole, returning to his claims relating to 
the average number in the broader field. As we and agency policy have already put that argument to 
rest, we will not address it any further.

Within the appeal brief, the Petitioner provides a list of journals and their corresponding impact 
factors. The list on appeal does not fully align with the list the Petitioner provided in the request for 
evidence response, nor does the impact factor related to each of the journals match that previously 
provided. The Petitioner has offered no explanation for this divergence, which weighs against his 
claims relating to these journals and their relative importance. Here, the Petitioner has introduced an 
inconsistency in the record, and he has not acted to resolve that issue. Similar to the long-standing 
precedent in Matter of Ho, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988), Matter of O-M-0-, 28 I&N Dec. 
191, 197-98 (BIA 2021) affirms that where the record contains material inconsistencies a petitioner 
bears the burden ofresolving these discrepancies with independent and objective evidence. See also 
Matter of Dobrotvorskii, 29 I&N Dec. 211, 215 (BIA 2025) ( concluding that unreconciled 
inconsistencies in the record undermine the filing party's eligibility); Matter ofChristos, Inc., 26 I&N 
Dec. 537, 539 (2015). Under these precedent decisions, such an unresolved conflict hinders the 
Petitioner's ability to establish the true aspects of his claims, rendering his documentation insufficient 
to meet the preponderance of the evidence standard. 
Next, the Petitioner claims his "work has regularly inspired further advances in intelligent agricultural 
robots, sensors, and instruments for agricultural tasks, particularly those efforts centered on improving 
the performance of agricultural tasks and preventing damage to agricultural yields." The Petitioner 
alleges this also demonstrates his contributions to the field were of major significance. USCIS policy 
addresses that issue indicating that "published research that has provoked widespread commentary on 
its importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative 
to others' work in that field, may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the 
field of endeavor." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
Of particular importance, we highlight the policy reflects one should both provoke widespread 
commentary and be highly cited. First, as it relates to the articles that cite to his work, he only provided 
a few pages of the articles and we are unable to fully gauge how important the Petitioner's work was 
within the piece. But more than that, while we acknowledge the inclusion of the Petitioner's prior 
work in the provided material, characterizing this as "inspiring further advances" misrepresents both 
the nature and extent ofthe citation. Within the provided examples, the papers made a single reference 
to the Petitioner's work as a passing mention included to establish general background context rather 
than to support, replicate, or build upon a main finding. In academic literature, foundational reliance 
should include substantive integration of a source's methodology, data, or central theoretical 
arguments into the core findings ofthe new study. Because these citations do not serve to substantiate 
the authors' primary thesis or advance their core methodology, it is more accurately defined as 
marginal acknowledgment rather than inspiring future advances. 
The appellate examples the Petitioner provides of marginal mentions fall far short of the in-depth 
discussion offered as a representative example in USCIS policy, which expects evidence ofpublished 
research that has provoked "widespread commentary" regarding its importance. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l); Venkatnarayan, 2024 WL 4008312, at*7 (upholding 
USCIS' finding that there was no evidence distinguishing the alien's work from all the other works 
cited in the articles to show the major significance of his work). Additionally, while the Petitioner 
may have shown that some of his work is well cited in the field, even one paper that is highly cited, 
what he did not demonstrate is that his work has spurred widespread commentary from others in the 
4 
field on its importance. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)( I) (noting to satisfy this 
criterion, it requires more than showing a paper is highly cited). 
Moving to the letters the Petitioner discusses, they outline the type of acknowledgement of his original 
findings that we would expect for published scholarly work. Much like the articles that cite to his 
work, each of the letters' authors mention his work that furthered their original research, but none of 
the identified text explains how the Petitioner's original findings have been significantly influential in 
the broader field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, B.1 (requiring that one's 
"contributions are ofmajor significance to the field"); see also Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 ( citing Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35 (upholding a finding that a ballroom dancer had not met this criterion because 
she did not corroborate her impact in the field as a whole)). 
In sum, the letters are not representative of the "widespread commentary" USCIS policy anticipates 
to demonstrate an alien has satisfied this criterion's mandates. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l); Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *20 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 
2025) (upholding that a petitioner did not submit evidence of work provoking widespread public 
commentary in the field). 
The Petitioner then discusses the following in support of this criterion: 
[His] strong background in artificial intelligence, electronic and mechanical design, and 
robot development, and his expertise in agricultural engineering concepts like high 
throughput phenotyping and quality assessments, has allowed his research to be 
particularly original, resulting in his success in fundamentally transforming sensors, 
instrumentation, and agricultural robotics technologies. [He] is particularly 
well-known for his contributions to field-based high throughput phenotyping for cotton 
plants .... 
In support of this assertion, he generally referred to an abundance of evidence in the original petition 
filing, but he did not discuss what support derived from what evidence. In visa petition proceedings, it 
is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. Section 291 ofthe Act, 
8 U.S.C. § 1361; Matter of Rehman, 27 I&N Dec. 124, 125 (BIA 2017). Commensurate with that 
burden is the responsibility for explaining the significance of offered evidence. Innova Sols., Inc. v. 
Baran, 338 F. Supp. 3d 1009, 1023 (N.D. Cal. 2018) (citing Repaka v. Beers, 993 F. Supp. 2d 1214, 1219 
(S.D. Cal. 2014)). See also Eguchi v. KelZv, No. 3:16-CV-1286-D, 2017 WL 2902667, at *3 (N.D. 
Tex. July 7, 2017). 
Filing parties should not submit large quantities of evidence without notifying the appellate body of the 
specific documentation that corroborates their claims as doing so places an undue burden on the appellate 
body to search through the documentation without the aid of the filing party's knowledge. Nolasco­
Amaya v. Garland, 14 F.4th 1007, 1012-13 (9th Cir. 2021) (citing Toquero v. INS, 956 F.2d 193, 196 n.4 
(9th Cir. 1992)); Nazakat v. INS, 981 F.2d 1146, 1148 (10th Cir. 1992). 
It is the filing party's responsibility to inform us of what errors the lower entity committed and how 
their claims and evidence satisfy which eligibility requirements. Nolasco-Amaya, 14 F.4th at 
1012-13; Spear Mktg., Inc. v. BancorpSouth Bank, 791 F.3d 586, 599 (5th Cir. 2015); S.E.C. v. 
5 
Thomas, 965 F.2d 825, 827 (10th Cir. 1992); see also Harolds Stores, Inc. v. Dillard Dep 't Stores, 
Inc., 82 F.3d 1533, 1540 n.3 (10th Cir. 1996) (concluding that where the evidence in the record is 
voluminous, it is imperative that an appellant provide specific references to record); Uli v. Mukasey, 
533 F.3d 950, 957 (8th Cir. 2008) ( citing to Matter ofD-I-M-, 24 I&N Dec. 448, 451 (BIA 2008) and 
noting when a case includes voluminous background materials, it is necessary to specifically identify 
the material one relies on to come to their conclusion). The truth is to be determined not by the quantity 
of evidence alone but by its quality. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 3 76 ( citing Matter ofE-M-, 
20 I&N Dec. 77, 80 (Comm'r 1989)). 
He further claims that funding for his projects "is affirmative of the major significance of his original 
contributions to the field." SCOPS noted the shortcomings with these claims in their denial decision 
explaining that "while potentially demonstrating the work's originality, will not necessarily establish, 
on its own, that the work is of major significance to the field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l ). 
But the Petitioner's appeal merely reasserts the same claims he offered to SCOPS that they rejected 
without identifying how they might have erred in their analysis or determination. The reason for filing 
an appeal is to provide an affected party with the means to remedy what they perceive as an erroneous 
conclusion of law or statement of fact within a decision in a previous proceeding. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3(a)(l)(v). It is insufficient to merely assert that SCOPS made an improper determination. 
Where a question of law is presented, supporting authority should be included, and where the dispute 
is on the facts, there should be a discussion of the particular details contested. Matter of Valencia, 
19 I&N Dec. 354, 355 (BIA 1986). Because we find no error in SCOPS' analysis here, we consider the 
Petitioner's arguments under this requirement to be forfeited or waived. Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 
693, 693 n.1 (BIA 2023) (finding arguments that do not meaningfully challenge an aspect of the 
underlying decision are deemed waived on appeal). 
He also shows that a device he helped create led to a patent grant. The Petitioner claims it has reached 
customers in multiple countries but the only evidence he offered in support of that claim was a letter. 
He did not provide probative evidence showing any sales or distribution ofthis device. Even the letter 
the Petitioner identifies does not assert how widely used the device is in the industry. This falls far 
short of satisfying the Petitioner's burden. The level of the impact in the field as a whole through the 
wide use ofthe innovation is a more appropriate measure to determine if an individual has sufficiently 
influenced his field rather than simple commercialization of a patented device. See Strategati, LLC v. 
Sessions, No. 3:18-CV-01200-H-AGS, 2019 WL 2330181, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019). See also 
Matter ofNew York State Dep't. of Transp., 22 I&N Dec. 215, 221 n.7, (Comm'r 1998) (although 
vacated as it relates to adopting a new framework for adjudicating national interest waiver petitions, 
the comparative analysis relating to patents remains valid and pertinent to this case). 
And finally, the Petitioner identifies evidence he submitted in the request for evidence response from 
OpenAlex and Google Scholar for other researchers in the field, but this material included information 
that postdated the petition filing date. We will not consider this evidence relating to this petition as a 
petitioner must establish eligibility at the time they file the visa petition. 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b )( 1 ), ( 12). 
USCIS may not approve a visa petition if the Petitioner was not qualified at the priority date but 
expects to become eligible at a subsequent time. See Matter ofIzummi, 22 I&N Dec. 169, 175-76 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1998); Matter ofKatigbak, 14 I&N Dec. 45, 49 (Reg'! Comm'r 1971).

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 O; Agrawal, 2025 WL 
2879217, at *12; Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 
2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an 
exhaustive historical review of the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the 
comprehensive regulatory scheme intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive 
this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Contra Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 
28, 2026) ( concluding the two-step framework was adopted without required notice-and-comment 
rulemaking). But that appears to misconstrue a conference report-which serves solely to explain 
how the House and Senate resolved differences between their two versions of the bill-as being more 
authoritative on this issue than a Judiciary Committee Report, the purpose of which includes guiding 
the executive branch in implementing the law and assisting the judicial branch in its interpretation. 
That view has been rejected by numerous federal courts before and since the Mukherji court made it. 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. Patel 
v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding 
agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision)). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
