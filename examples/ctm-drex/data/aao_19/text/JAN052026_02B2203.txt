Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an art history professor and researcher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish, 
as required, either a one-time achievement or that she met at least three of the ten initial evidentiary 
criteria for this immigrant classification. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics 
which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation; 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and 
(iii) the alien's entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 
2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt of a major internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met two criteria, relating to judging the work of others in her 
field and authorship of scholarly articles in professional publications. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) 
and (vi). Upon farther review, we conclude that the Petitioner also satisfies the published material 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). Therefore, we will proceed to a final merits determination. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Given the structure of the regulations and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, the satisfaction 
of three evidentiary criteria alone does not automatically or presumptively establish eligibility. In a 
final merits determination, we evaluate whether a petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of 
the evidence, their sustained national or international acclaim, 1 that they are among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that their achievements have been recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation. Therefore, we analyze an individual's accomplishments 
and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate 
extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.2

As authoring scholarly articles is often inherent to the work ofprofessors and researchers in academia, 
the citation history or other evidence of the influence of an individual's articles can be an indicator to 
determine the recognition they have received in the field and whether such recognition has been 
sustained. Such an analysis at the final merits determination stage is appropriate pursuant to Kazarian, 
596 F. 3d at 1122. Here, the Petitioner has emphasized that the individual and collective citations to 
her work, according to metrics and statistics, show that she has received the required recognition for 
her scholarly achievements. 
Regarding the Petitioner's overall citation record, she provided citation reports from CNKI.net for 
three of her articles that received 29, 28, and 10 citations, respectively. The record, however, does not 
establish how many ofthese are self-citations. The CNKI.net reports for her two most frequently cited 
articles indicate that their citation frequency "ranks in the top 5% ... in the field of archeology." The 
report for her remaining article states that its citation frequency ranks in "the top 5% ... in the 
Lingcheng ofAncient Chinese History." While the CNKI.net reports indicate that three ofPetitioner's 
individual articles have been cited at a rate that is well above average compared to others in the field, 
the comparative ranking for just three papers is not sufficient to establish that she is recognized as 
being among the small percentage of academics at the top of her field. Further, citation frequency 
which is quantitative in nature does not reveal the reasons for the citations, which involve a qualitative 
analysis. In other words, a higher citation number might show that others in the field have noticed the 
Petitioner's work, but it does not confirm that her work has impacted or advanced the field in a 
significant way that renders her nationally or internationally acclaimed. 
Overall, the Petitioner has not shown that the citations to her published research demonstrate 
recognition at a level consistent with being among that small percentage at the very top of her field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The statute demands "extensive documentation" of recognition. See 
section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. Here, the Petitioner relies to a significant extent on CNKI.net metrics 
and data to mathematically place herself at the top of the field. However, the record does not

Overall, while these letters attest to the Petitioner's skills and accomplishments, they do not establish

highlight the Petitioner's work, they do not reflect a career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering 
the required sustained national or international acclaim. See Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. l:19-CV-706 
(MAD/CFH), 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding the submitted letters failed 
to establish the plaintiff's contributions resulted in significant acclaim). 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. 10 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of "extraordinary ability"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary 
ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 
815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an 
exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). 
In this case, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of her work is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the 
field. See Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The evidence related to her media coverage, scholarly 
articles, citation rates, peer review service, and work atl !reflects that she has received 
some recognition for her contributions and expertise in the field but falls short of establishing that she 
satisfied the requirements for this classification at the time of filing. While the Petitioner need not 
establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is 
insufficient to demonstrate that she is among the small percentage at the top of her field. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability.
