The Petitioner, an information technology (IT) consultant, 1 seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. USCIS also concluded that the Petitioner had not established that his entry will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. The matter is now before us on appeal under 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. We will also enter a finding of willful misrepresentation of a material fact. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that the alien seeks to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a

one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

2. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields. 
The Petitioner is a professional member ofthe Association for Computing Machinery (ACM). A printout 
from the ACM' s website reads, in part: "Professional Members are those who subscribe to the purposes 
of the Association and satisfy one of the following qualifications: 1. Bachelor's Degree; 2. Equivalent 
level of education; or 3. Two years full-time employment in the IT field." A level of education or years 
of experience in a particular field are not outstanding achievements for the purposes ofthis criterion. See, 
generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner also submitted a letter attributed to ACM Member Services, stating that the Petitioner's 
"active role in the ACM community and his commitment to contributing to the field distinguish[] him as 
more than a regular member." The regulation indicates that outstanding achievements must be a 
requirement for gaining membership. The assertion that the Petitioner has taken an especially active role 
within ACM after becoming a member does not establish that ACM requires outstanding achievements 
as a condition for admission into the association. 
USCIS concluded that the "ACM does not require outstanding achievements of [its] members." On 
appeal, the Petitioner cites the letter quoted above, but the Petitioner's activities once he became a member 
ofthe ACM do not show that the ACM requires outstanding achievements as a condition ofmembership. 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the requirements of this criterion.

The Petitioner submitted letters from employers and consultants detailing the nature of the work the 
Petitioner performed. These individuals are in a position to attest to that work, but the burden remains 
on the Petitioner to establish the distinguished reputations of the employing organizations. 
The relative size or longevity of an organization or establishment is not in and of itself a determining 
factor but is considered together with other information to determine whether a distinguished 
reputation exists. Other relevant factors for evaluating the reputation of an organization or 
establishment can include the scale of its customer base or relevant media coverage. See, generally, 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
For a startup business, we consider evidence that the business has received significant fonding from 
government entities, venture capital fonds, angel investors, or other such funders commensurate with 
fonding rounds generally achieved for that startup's stage and industry, as a positive factor regarding 
its distinguished reputation. Id.

III. WILLFUL MISREPRESENTATION OF A MATERIAL FACT 
Misrepresentation of a material fact may lead to multiple consequences in immigration proceedings. 
Any alien who, by fraud or willfully misrepresenting a material fact, seeks to procure ( or has sought to 
procure or has procured) a visa, other documentation, or admission into the United States or other benefit 
provided under the Act is inadmissible. Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) ofthe Act, 8 U.S.C. § l 182(a)(6)(C)(i). 
Under Board oflmmigration Appeals precedent, a material misrepresentation is one which "tends to shut 
off a line of inquiry which is relevant to the alien's eligibility."4 A willful misrepresentation requires that 
the individual knowingly make a material misstatement to a government official for the purpose of 
obtaining an immigration benefit to which one is not entitled. 5 Material misrepresentation requires only 
a false statement that is material and willfully made. 6 The term "willfully" means knowing and 
intentionally, as distinguished from accidentally, inadvertently, or in an honest belief that the facts are 
otherwise. 7 
The Petitioner signed Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Worker, under penalty of perjury, 
thereby taking responsibility for the petition and its contents. See Matter ofValdez, 27 I&N Dec. 496, 
499 (BIA 2018) (stating "an alien's signature on an immigration application establishes a strong 
presumption that he or she knows the contents of the application and has assented to them"). The 
Petitioner did not identify any attorney, representative, or preparer who assisted him in preparing and 
filing the petition; he even stated that he personally prepared the translations in the record. In this 
way, the Petitioner has assumed full responsibility for all the statements and evidence submitted in 
support of his petition. 
As discussed below, we conclude that the Petitioner has willfully misrepresented material facts by

Furthermore, while the Petitioner repeatedly asserts that a finding of willful misrepresentation must 
rest on affirmative evidence of "intent to deceive," intent to deceive is an element of fraud, which is 
legally distinct from willful misrepresentation. Willful misrepresentation does not require an intent to 
deceive, but instead requires only the knowledge that the representation is false. Parlak v. Holder, 57 
F.3d 457 (6th Cir. 2009) ( citing to Witter v. INS, 113 F.3d 549, 554 (5th Cir. 1997); Forbes v. INS, 48 
F.3d 439, 442 (9th Cir. 1995). "The element of willfulness is satisfied by a finding that the 
misrepresentation was deliberate and voluntary." Mwongera v. INS, 187 F.3d 323,330 (3d Cir. 1999).

IV. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. at 25. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 
953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically 
meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 
4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability 
designation is "extremely restrictive by design"); Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 
2021 WL 2338316, at *5 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd, 2023 WL 1156801 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) 
( determining that EB-1 visas are "reserved for a very small percentage of prospective immigrants"); 
see also Hamal v. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 19-cv-2534, 2020 WL 2934954, at *l (D.D.C. June 
3, 2020) ( citing Kazarian, 596 at 1122 (upholding denial of petition ofa published theoretical physicist 
specializing in non-Einsteinian theories of gravitation) (stating that "[c]ourts have found that even 
highly accomplished individuals fail to win this designation")); Lee v. Ziglar, 237 F. Supp. 2d 914, 
918 (N.D. 2002) (finding that "arguably one of the most famous baseball players in Korean history" 
did not qualify for visa as a baseball coach). 
Here, the Petitioner has established success in his field, but has not shown a degree of recognition of 
his work that rises to the level ofsustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates a "career 
of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 
1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) ofthe Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate 
that the Petitioner is one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal.
