Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a chief executive, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability in business. 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S .C. § l l 53(b )(1 )(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner meets the 
classification's initial evidentiary requirements. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if they have extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and their achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; they seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the country. Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then they must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable material if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010); 
see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 
2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

7. Display of Work at Artistic Exhibitions or Showcases. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The Petitioner asserts that he qualifies for this criterion because he has spoken at various business 
events and been interviewed on podcasts. SCOPS concluded that these talks did not take place at 
artistic exhibitions or showcases, and so did not qualify under the plain language of this criterion. Id. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS failed to consider his argument that his speaking 
engagements qualify for this criterion under the comparable evidence provision at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). Upon review, SCOPS did not address the Petitioner's arguments regarding comparable 
evidence. However, this error is, at most, harmless, because the Petitioner has not met the 
requirements of the comparable evidence provision. See generally Matter of O-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 
330,350 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing cases where errors that were not material to a matter's end result were 
therefore considered harmless). 
A petitioner seeking consideration of comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) must 
demonstrate not only that an evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to their occupation, but also 
that the evidence provided is of comparable significance to the evidence required by that criterion. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). As noted by the Petitioner, the plain 
language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires display of one's work at artistic exhibitions, and his 
field of business and entrepreneurship is not one which involves such exhibitions. As such, the 
criterion does not readily apply to his occupation. However, he has not established that his speaking 
engagements have the same significance in the field of business as artistic exhibitions do in artistic 
fields. 
The Petitioner contends that the various talks he has given are the business equivalent of artistic 
exhibitions because those talks have taken place in front of or been distributed to public audiences, 
which meets the definition ofa display. However, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires 
the work which is displayed to be the person's work product in their field of extraordinary ability. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). For example, a painter's work product is a 
painting. If that painter appears on a podcast to discuss art, that appearance generally would not 
qualify for this criterion because it is not a display of the painter's work product in their field of 
extraordinary ability. 7 
Similarly, here the Petitioner's claimed area of extraordinary ability is entrepreneurship in the field of 
restaurant franchising. There is no indication in the record that his various podcast and conference 
talks constitute his work product in that field. The sole possible exception, his business strategy

B. Summary and Reserved Issue 
The Petitioner must demonstrate that he meets at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(i)-(x) to 
satisfy the initial evidence requirement for this classification. The record establishes that he meets the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), but the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets any of the 
other seven criteria. Because the Petitioner cannot meet the initial evidentiary requirement without 
meeting two additional criteria, detailed discussion of the remaining claimed criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v), relating to original contributions of major significance, cannot change the outcome 
of the appeal. Therefore, we will reserve this criterion for future consideration if the need arises. See 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to 
make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not provided the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) and so has not 
demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. As a result, the petition will 
remain denied and we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate and 
conclude that it does not support a conclusion that the Petitioner has the acclaim and recognition 
required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification that is intended for individuals already at 
the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing towards the top. See Matter ofPrice, 
20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (concluding that even major league athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of extraordinary ability; 
Visinscaia, 4 F.Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) (finding that the extraordinary 
ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"). The record demonstrates that the Petitioner 
is a successful business strategist whose skills and achievements have been valuable to his employer 
and contributed to increased sales within his assigned region. In recent years, he has also earned some 
external recognition through his book chapter and speaking engagements. However, the record does 
not demonstrate that the Petitioner has achieved a degree of recognition indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that his work is consistent with a "career of acclaimed 
work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also 
10 
section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the 
Petitioner has garnered sustained national or international acclaim in his field, and that he is one of the 
small percentage who has risen to the very top of his business field. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the 
Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-mentioned reasons, with each 
considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
