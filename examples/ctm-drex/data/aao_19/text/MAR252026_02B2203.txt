Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a music video producer and director, requests classification under the employment­
based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. 1153(b )( 1 )(A). Successful 
petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or 
international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition and the Petitioner's following combined 
motions to reopen and reconsider. SCOPS found that he met two of ten initial evidentiary 
requirements - one less than needed for a final merits determination. On appeal, the Petitioner 
contends that SCOPS overlooked evidence and misapplied evidentiary criteria. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with SCOPS that he has not satisfied the requisite number of initial evidentiary 
criteria. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten alternate evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b)(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and 
Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits determination. To merit final approval, 
the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and recognized achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (establishing a two-step process for 
adjudicating extraordinary ability petitions); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The Petitioner earned a bachelor's degree in administrative management and a master's degree in show 
business management from universities in his home country of the Ukraine. Over the following 11 
years, he produced and directed music videos and other promotional films and founded his own 
production company. In 2022, he came to the United States, where he seeks to continue his career as 
a producer and director. 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He therefore must meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports SCOPS' findings that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of: 
• His participation as a judge of others' work in his field; and 
• Display of his work in the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vii). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS erred in finding that he did not meet the following six 
additional evidentiary criteria: 
• Receipt of nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in his field; 
• Membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their members 
as judged by nationally or internationally recognized experts; 
• Published materials about him and his work in professional or major trade publications or other 
major media; 
• Original contributions of major significance in his field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration for services relative to 
others in his field.

B. The Claimed Evidentiary Criteria 
1. Nationally or Internationally Recognized A wards 
This criterion requires "documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines whether a petitioner - as opposed to their 
employer, for example - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F .2(B)(l ), 
Criterion One. If so, USCIS then considers whether the awards received national or international 
recognition and were issued for excellence in the relevant field. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he won "Best Music Video" awards at separate U.S. film 
festivals in 2016 and 2017. SCOPS found insufficient evidence that these awards garnered national 
or international recognition. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he "cure[d] that gap" by submitting documentary evidence about 
the festivals that issued the awards. "But, as reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the 
award, not the awarding organization." Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021). 
Despite national or international recognition that the awarding film festivals received, the Petitioner's 
evidence about them does not demonstrate that his "Best Music Video" awards received national or 
international recognition. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013)) ("Without 
evidence of how a larger audience viewed Plaintiff's awards, there was no way for the agency to 
evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion.") 
The Petitioner also submitted letters from the lead singer of a rock band for whom the Petitioner has 
produced music videos. The letters state that one video received a "Video of the Year" award at a 
Ukrainian music awards show in 2013. The lead singer's letters, however, are not sufficiently reliable 
to document the Petitioner's receipt of the purported award. See Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-
LB, 2021 WL 568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) ("[T]he agency's denial ... was not arbitrary 
and capricious" where the agency stated that "there was no 'primary' evidence ofthe awards (and only 
letters describing receipt of awards))"; see also 72andSunny Partners LLC v. Mayorkas, 
No. 222CV04465CASGJSX, 2023 WL 5661527, at *8 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 28, 2023) ("[I]t was not 
arbitrary or capricious for USCIS to determine that the support letters were of limited probative value

The Petitioner also submitted on line information about the 2013 awards. But this information lists the

The Petitioner did not submit documentation of his receipt of nationally or internationally recognized 
awards for excellence in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this criterion. 
2. Membership in Associations 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a pet1t10ner must submit "[d]ocumentation of the[ir] 
membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted letters from the president ofa Ukrainian association ofcinematographers and 
media professionals, identifying the Petitioner as an association member since 2017. One ofthe letters 
also identifies an association member who welcomed the Petitioner to the group, describing her as an 
experienced, international film producer. The Petitioner also provided excerpts from the association's 
regulations, indicating that the association requires its members to have: 
• At least seven years of professional experience; 
• Specialized higher education; 
• Receipt of one international award or several nationally significant awards; 
• Recommendations from active members or well-known personalities; and 
• Contributions to the development of Ukrainian cinematography. 
The regulations state that the association's membership application process involves a written 
application and an interview. 
SCOPS found that the evidence did not demonstrate that the assoc1at10n requires outstanding 
achievements of its members or that recognized national or international experts judged members' 
achievements.

On appeal, the Petitioner argues that that the third membership criterion - receipt of one international 
award or several nationally significant awards - shows that the association requires outstanding 
achievements of its members. He states: 
That is a merit-screen keyed to recognized excellence in the field, not a pay-to-join or 
tenure-only gate. The path is further narrowed to those who have an impact rather than 
just participate by the additional requirement to demonstrate contributions to the 
development of national cinematography. 
The Petitioner also asserts that the association's membership requirements for recommendations and 
an interview "serve as expert vetting." He states that recommenders like the film producer "reflect[] 
evaluation by recognized experts in the discipline." 
We agree with the Petitioner that the association's requirement for an international award or multiple 
nationally significant awards demonstrates that the association effectively requires outstanding 
achievements of its members. But the record does not establish that recognized national or 
international experts judge association members. The Petitioner has not demonstrated that the film 
producer or other association members are recognized national or international experts in their fields. 
See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Two ("The petitioner must show that 
membership in the association requires outstanding achievements in the field for which classification 
is sought, as judged by recognized national or international experts.") ( emphasis added). 
The Petitioner did not submit documentation of his membership in associations in his field requiring 
outstanding achievements of their members as judged by recognized national or international experts. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding on this criterion. 
3. Published Materials About the Petitioner 
This criterion requires "[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications 
or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii). "Such evidence shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and 
any necessary translation." 
USCIS first determines whether published material relates to a petitioner and their specific work in 
their field. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Three. If so, USCIS then 
determines whether the publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade publication, or 
major media publication. Id. For professional and major trade publications, relevant factors include 
the intended audience. Id. For major trade publications and other major media, USCIS considers the 
relative circulation, readership, or viewership. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted several published materials. But SCOPS found that only two sufficiently 
related to him and his work in his field. One was published in 2017; the other in 2021. SCOPS found 
insufficient evidence that either online article appeared in a qualifying publication. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the articles' publishers are professional or trade publications 
in the Ukraine. He argues: "[T]he record establishes that both outlets are edited media serving 
5 
significant audiences in their respective markets, with professional or trade orientation." But he also 
states that his "appeal does not rely on web-traffic analytics." Thus, under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), 
he cannot demonstrate the articles' appearance in "major trade publications." See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F .2(B)( 1 ), Criterion Three ("[R ]elevant factors include ... the relative circulation, 
readership, or viewership (for major trade publications and other major media).") He must therefore 
demonstrate the articles' appearance in professional publications. 
For immigration purposes, the term "profession" means "one of the occupations listed in section 
10l(a)(32) of the Act, [8 U.S.C. § l 10l(a)(32),] as well as any occupation for which a United 
States baccalaureate degree or its foreign equivalent is the minimum requirement for entry into the 
occupation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(k)(2). Thus, a "professional" publication means one whose intended 
audience are members of the same professional occupation. 
The Petitioner submitted information about the publication in which the 2017 article appeared, 
identifying the publication as an online magazine intended for women. Women work in a variety of 
occupations. Thus, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the publication constitutes a professional 
publication. 
The 2021 article indicates its appearance in the blog of a retail store chain that sells musical 
instruments and equipment. The record identifies the blog's intended audience as musicians and 
music lovers. The blog states that it "strives to cover the events of the musical world as widely as 
possible, providing its view on related areas." Thus, the record indicates that the blog's intended 
audience includes more than just professional musicians. The Petitioner therefore has not established 
the blog as a professional publication. 
The Petitioner's evidence does not include published materials about him and his work in his field in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' 
finding on this criteria. 
4. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[e]vidence ofthe[ir] original scientific, scholarly, 
artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
users first determines whether a petitioner made original contributions in their field. See generally 
6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(B)( l ), Criterion Five. If so, users then determines whether the original 
contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
SCOPS found that the Petitioner's work on music videos constituted original contributions to his field. 
But SCOPS concluded that he did not demonstrate the major significance of those contributions to the 
field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that support letters show that: a director/cinematographer "changed 
her own professional practice because of the Petitioner's techniques;" a recording artist "repeatedly 
commissioned the Petitioner, released the works to large audiences, and attributes measurable 
6 
promotional gains to the videos' execution;" and a producer "describes systematic diffusion of the 
Petitioner's techniques within a major production workflow across hundreds of projects." The 
Petitioner argues: "That is exactly the kind of adoption and downstream effect that distinguishes 
'major significance' from routine competence." 
These support letters may demonstrate the major significance of the Petitioner's original contributions 
to the individual director, recording artist, and producer who wrote the letters. But the letters do not 
sufficiently establish the claimed significance of his work to his field. See Skokos v. United States 
Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 209CV00193RLHPAL, 2010 WL 11538054, at *4 (D. Nev. Jan. 13, 
2010), aff'd sub nom. Skokos v. US. Dep 't of Homeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712 (9th Cir. 2011) 
("Although [ the petitioner's] talents and abilities have been significant in the lives of the people he 
has served, in order to satisfy the requirements of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h), his activities must be original 
and must extend to the profession as a whole.") (emphasis added); see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Five ("Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's 
original work constitutes major, significant contributions to the.field.") ( emphasis added). The letters 
that the Petitioner provided do not explain how his original contributions more broadly affected his 
field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), Criterion Five ("Submitted letters should 
specifically describe the person's contribution and its significance to the field.") 
The Petitioner did not provide evidence of original contributions of major significance in his field. 
We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding on this criterion. 
5. Leading or Critical Role 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for 
organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii). 
users first determines whether a petitioner performed in a leading or critical role for an organization 
or establishment, or a division or department of an organization or establishment. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Eight. A leading role means that a petitioner is (or was) 
a leader within the organization, establishment, division or department. Id. A critical role means that 
a petitioner "has contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the 
organization or establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or 
establishment." Id. 
users then determines whether the organization, establishment, department, or division for which a 
petitioner holds or held a leading or critical role has a distinguished reputation. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Eight. The word "distinguished" ordinarily means "marked by 
eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting an eminent person." Distinguished, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com. Relevant factors include: the relative size 
or longevity of an organization; the scale of its customer base; and relevant media coverage. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion Eight. 
The Petitioner submitted letters primarily from bands and recording artists who have hired him to 
produce and/or direct music videos or social media content. The record also contains letters from two 
companies for whom he produced and directed videos. 
7 
The record supports SCOPS' s finding ofinsufficient evidence that the Petitioner performed in leading 
or critical roles for those organizations. Some ofthe letters detail the number of online views received 
by videos that he produced or directed. Others describe the quality of his videos or his purported 
mastery ofthe details of the video-making process. But, while all the letters praise the Petitioner, they 
do not provide enough details or explain how he performed in a leading or critical role for those 
organizations. See Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *5 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 
2025) (finding conclusory statements about an alien's role insufficient to establish it as leading or 
critical); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 8 (stating that letters regarding a 
petitioner's claimed leading or critical role can be helpful "so long as the letters contain detailed and 
probative information that specifically addresses how the person's role for the organization, 
establishment, division, or department was leading or critical"). 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated his performance in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have distinguished reputations. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding on this 
criterion. 
6. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must provide "[ e ]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The Petitioner submitted a copy of his production company's 2021 Ukrainian income tax return. The 
tax return reflects the company's "total income" of 3,056,144 Ukrainian hryvnia (UAH), or, at that 
time, about $111,996. In the initial petition, he claimed that amount as his income. But, in his motions 
to reopen and reconsider, he claimed only 2,132, 937.24 UAH of it, or about $78,764, as his income. 
A letter from the company's chief accountant states: 
It is important to note that [the Petitioner] is the sole owner of [the company], and the 
majority of the company's income is attributed to his professional services.... The 
company's earnings reflect his professional contributions, with more than half of the 
total income being his personal earnings. Given that most workers at [the company] 
are not employed full-time, [his] earnings constitute the bulk of the company's income. 
The Petitioner also submitted evidence that the average annual salary ofa "clip maker," or music video 
director, in the Ukraine is 240,000 UAH a year, or about $8,723.23. He therefore argues that 
remuneration for his services far exceeds that of most Ukrainian music video directors. 
As SCOPS found, however, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that a majority of the company's total 
income reflects his personal income. The company's financial documents identify it as a Ukrainian 
limited liability company. "[A] corporation is an entity distinct from its stockholders." Matter of 
Silver Dragon Chinese Rest., 19 T&N Dec. 401,403 (Comm'r 1986). 
The Petitioner argues that USCIS policy recognizes that artists and directors are frequently paid on a 
per-project basis rather than on hourly or salaried schedules. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
8 
Criterion Nine. ("Many artists are not paid an hourly rate but instead are paid a daily rate (which may 
not be equivalent to 8 hours) or are paid a certain amount for a project (involving an unknown number 
of hours).") But the profit-and-loss statement accompanying the company's 2021 income tax return 
indicates that the 3,056,144 UAH total income figure that the Petitioner cites reflects the company's 
revenues before the payment of business costs, expenses, and taxes. After subtracting such payments, 
the profit-and-loss statement states the company's "net profit" of about 1,262,100 UAH. The 
Petitioner has not explained how he could have received 2,132,937.24 UAH of the company's total 
income as personal income if the company's net profit was only 1,262,100 UAH. See Matter ofHo, 
19 I&N Dec. 582, 591 (BIA 1988) (requiring petitioners to resolve inconsistencies of record with 
independent, objective evidence pointing to where the truth lies). 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that copies ofcontracts and invoices corroborate fees that his company 
charged customers and that copies of "bank receipts and owner-distribution ledgers" show the amount 
of money the company transferred to him. The record, however, does not contain any of those 
materials. He therefore has not demonstrated how much remuneration he receives for his services. 
Thus, he has not established his remuneration as significantly high in relation to others in his field. 
Also, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the reliability of his Ukrainian average salary information 
for the occupation. The online information states the median salary as 20,000.24 UAH "according to 
data from 3 resumes posted ... with the title 'Clipmaker' and on synonymous queries 'Videomaker' 
and others for the past <> months." The record does not indicate a sufficient number of data sources 
to reliably calculate the occupation's average salary. See 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)( 1 ), Criterion 
Nine ("Some websites provide user-reported salary data, which may not be a valid comparison it: for 
example, too few users reported their salaries or the data is otherwise not credible or reliable.") 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence that remuneration for his services is significantly high 
relative to others in his field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary 
criterion. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has met two of this category's ten initial evidentiary criteria - one less than needed for 
a final merits determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not make such a 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their ultimate 
decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not met the minimum required amount of evidentiary criteria for the requested 
immigrant visa category. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
