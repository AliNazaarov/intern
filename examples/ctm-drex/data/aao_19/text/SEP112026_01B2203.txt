The Petitioner, a postdoctoral research associate in the field of chemical engineering, seeks 
classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an 
alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification makes immigrant visas available to 
those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had satisfied 
at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, petitioners must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

Regarding the membership criterion, the Petitioner does not dispute USCIS' decision on appeal but 
merely preserves his right to challenge this criterion in future proceedings. Here, USCIS properly 
analyzed the evidence and concluded that the Petitioner has not sufficiently established that his 
membership with the Royal Society of Chemists requires outstanding achievements of their members. 
Therefore, we adopt and affirm USCIS' analysis of this criterion 3 and conclude that he did not meet 
the plain language in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The sole issue now is whether the Petitioner meets the criterion relating to original contributions of 
major significance. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that his response to the request for evidence 
(RFE) "supplied the exact type of evidence USCIS requested" demonstrating "independent adoption 
and use of [the Petitioner's] work by other researchers" consistent with original contributions ofmajor 
significance. 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." For 
this criterion, the Petitioner must establish that not only has he made original contributions but that 
they have been of major significance in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. Evidence of significant contributions can include published research that has 
provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others in the field, or documentation that 
research generated a high number of citations relative to others' work in the field. Id. When 
determining whether original contributions have major significance, detailed letters from experts in 
the field explaining the nature and significance of the contributions may provide valuable context, 
especially if accompanied by corroborating documentation. Id.

Petitioner's original scholarly contributions have been of major significance to the field of 
biochemistry research, as contemplated by the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). Therefore, the 
record does not satisfy at least three of the 10 criteria at 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten lesser criteria. As a result, we need not provide the type 
7 
of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for 
the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
