Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a secondary school teacher, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner meets the initial evidence requirements for this classification through evidence of 
either a one-time achievement (a major, internationally recognized award) or by meeting at least three 
of the evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met at least one of the evidentiary 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to her leading or critical role for an organization with 
a distinguished reputation, but did not provide an analysis of the evidence. For two additional criteria, 
relating to membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements and original 
contributions of major significance to the field, SCOPS discussed the evidence and indicated that it 
was insufficient, but ended the discussion with a statement that each of the criteria had been met. On 
appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the inconsistencies in SCOPS' decision warrant a withdrawal of the 
decision. She also asserts that she meets the evidentiary criteria relating to a relatively high salary, 
whereas SCOPS concluded that she did not. After reviewing all of the evidence in the record de novo, 
we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that she meets the initial evidence requirements for 
this classification. 
1. Evidence of Membership in Associations Requiring Outstanding Achievements of Members 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
2 
The Petitioner initially submitted evidence of her membership in three associations, but in responding 
to SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE) focused on only one of these, Phi Beta Delta honor society for 
international scholars. In discussing the evidence in its decision, SCOPS noted that the record did not 
include sufficient evidence to show that Phi Beta Delta required outstanding achievements of its 
members, noting the lack of bylaws or other evidence which might explain the association's 
membership requirements. 
However, as stated above, after initially concluding that the evidence was insufficient, SCOPS 
followed this with the single statement that "the submitted evidence does meet this criterion." Because 
this statement contradicts the discussion preceding it, we withdraw the statement and provide de novo 
analysis of the evidence below. 
In support of the Petitioner's assertion that her membership in Phi Beta Delta qualifies under this 
criterion, she provided two pages from the website https://honorsocietymuseum.org which appear to 
be the society's profile. This evidence describes it as "an international honor society that recognizes 
and promotes academic achievement in the field of international education." The webpages also 
include a general summary of the society's membership requirement: 
To become a member of Phi Beta Delta, individuals must meet certain academic 
requirements, including a minimum GP A and completion of coursework in 
international education. Members are also expected to demonstrate a commitment to 
promoting international understanding and cooperation. 
We first note that these requirements cannot be directly attributed to Phi Beta Delta, as they do not 
appear on the society's own website or reference any of its own material about membership 
requirements. More importantly, the requirements as stated do not show that the society requires its 
members to have outstanding achievements in the field of education, or international education, as 
they focus primarily on academic achievements. The Petitioner has not shown that the academic 
accomplishments ofstudents studying to enter a field or profession can be considered to be outstanding 
achievements in the broader field which they intend to enter. Further, much like membership 
requirements based on a certain level of education or experience, requirements of meeting an 
unspecified GP A and completing certain coursework will generally not support a conclusion that an 
association requires outstanding achievements of its members. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.dhs.gov/policy-manual. 
In addition, since this evidence provides no information regarding the selection process for members 
of Phi Beta Delta, it does not demonstrate that a membership candidate's achievements are judged to 
be outstanding by national or international experts in the field. 
For all ofthese reasons, we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that she meets this criterion. 
2. Original Contributions of Major Significance in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) seeks "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." In 
order to satisfy the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must establish that not only has 
3 
she made original contributions, but that they have been of major significance in the field. For 
example, a Petitioner may show that the contributions have been widely implemented throughout the 
field, have remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major 
significance. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126 (D.D.C. 2013). 
The Petitioner asserts that she has developed and implemented an ESOL teaching methodology 
employing cognate recognition and contrastive rhetoric that has helped her students more efficiently 
learn English and impacted the work of other ESOL teachers. To show her development of this 
methodology, she submitted a copy of a course book, SPARTA, which she states became popular with 
students and instructors at a college in Canada where she was employed. She also submits pages from 
a website, www.______.bom, in which she describes her teaching methods and materials. While this 
evidence shows that she has created original works in her field, as discussed below the record does 
not establish that these materials or methodology constitute contributions that have been of major 
significance in her field. 
In support of the impact or influence of her methodology in the field of language education, the 
Petitioner submitted two letters from others in her field who express interest in her work. A-F-, who 
states that he is an ESOL teacher who provides instruction to employees of small businesses, indicates 
his interest in using SP ART A in his work and invites the Petitioner "to propose a cooperation 
framework." He asserts that an agreement would expose thousands of ESOL learners across the 
country to her methodology. But the record does not include evidence that an agreement arose from 
this proposal that resulted in the use of the Petitioner's teaching methodology beyond her own 
classroom or those of her employer. 
A second letter was written by M-S-, who describes himself as the President of a manufacturer of 
molded products. In the letter he confirms his intent to use the Petitioner's services to aid in his 
company's program to provide English language instruction to its employees, noting that she would 
"work alongside the designated English language instructors using your English teaching 
methodology." However, as with the letter above, the record does not include evidence that the 
Petitioner provided these services. In addition, even if the record did show that she provided the 
services described in these letters, she has not shown that doing so would reflect a degree of 
implementation in the field of language education rising to the level of major significance. 
The Petitioner also submitted a letter from a former co-worker at English for Internationals. The writer 
lauds the Petitioner's performance as an ESOL instructor, stating that other instructors saw her as a 
leader and sought her advice in classroom and curriculum preparation. While also complementing her 
teaching and language skills, the letter does not indicate that the Petitioner's "pedagogical approaches" 
made a contribution beyond her own students and fellow instructors at English for Internationals. 
When responding to SCOPS' RFE, the Petitioner objected to the request for "patents, copyright or 
trademark material" to document her contributions and their impact on the field, asserting that this 
represents a requirement for evidence that exceeds the plain language ofthis criterion. While we agree 
that this evidence is not specifically required at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), we do not read the RFE or 
SCOPS' decision to impose novel evidentiary requirements. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 
1121 ("neither USCIS nor an AAO may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary 
requirements beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5.") As noted at the outset, it is the Petitioner's 
4 
burden to show her eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence, and the RFE described a type of 
evidence that might show that she meets this evidentiary burden for this criterion. While the Petitioner 
mentioned "peer-reviewed publications and academic citations" in her RFE response as evidence in 
the record which supports her claim to original contributions of major significance, the record does 
not include such evidence. And as explained above, the evidence of record is insufficient to show that 
the Petitioner's original works have been ofmajor significance in her field. Accordingly, we withdraw 
SCOPS' concluding statement regarding this criterion and determine that the Petitioner does not meet 
this criterion. 
3. Commanded a High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration in Relation to Others in the 
Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) seeks "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." 
The Petitioner submitted evidence confirming that her annual salary as a middle school teacher as of 
the time she filed her petition was approximately $69,000. She also submitted a salary report from 
www.ziprecruiter.com showing that the average salary for middle school teachers in Georgia was 
approximately $44,300 at the time. 
SCOPS determined that although the evidence showed that the Petitioner's salary was higher than 
average for teachers in Georgia, it did not establish that her salary was high relative to other teachers 
with her same level of education. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS did not consider 
evidence submitted with her RFE response which she states suggests that a salary difference of 
approximately $4200 is "significant." She also cites to a district court decision which found that the 
regulation does not require a comparison to others in the field with extraordinary ability. 
While we acknowledge that a comparison of the Petitioner's salary to that of "others in the field" does 
not limit that comparison to those who might qualify as aliens of extraordinary ability, the comparison 
should be specific to the Petitioner's position and work location. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l). This means that the comparison should consider others of a comparable level of 
education and/or experience in the field as well as those possessing similar skills. Matter ofPrice, 20 
I&N Dec. 953, 955 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a professional golfer's earnings versus other 
PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th 
Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others 
in the field); Crimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's 
salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) 
( comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). Since the 
ZipRecruiter data submitted by the Petitioner did not differentiate between starting level salaries for 
teachers holding bachelor's degrees and the earnings of those with higher levels of education and/or 
experience, or between those performing ESOL duties and those teaching the same subjects to English 
speakers, the Petitioner has not provided an appropriate basis for comparison to the Petitioner's salary. 
See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report 
and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) 
( finding that a petitioner's burden under this criterion includes the need to explain how the comparative 
salary evidence presented provides "a relevant, appropriate, and fair comparison."). 
5 
In addition, the Petitioner has not shown that the ZipRecruiter salary report, which considers the 
salaries of all teachers across the state of Grrgia,I provides the basis of a geographically-relevant 
comparison with her own salary. We note that County lies within the Atlanta metropolitan area, 
whereas a salary report including wages for teachers from the entire state would include rural areas in 
Georgia with a typically lower cost of living and lower wages. 
For all of the reasons discussed above, we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that she 
meets this criterion. 
4. Additional Evidentiary Criteria 
SCOPS also concluded, without providing any analysis, that the Petitioner had established that she 
played a leading or critical role for distinguished organizations at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(3)(3)(viii). But we 
need not discuss this additional criterion. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary 
requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the identified basis for denial is dispositive 
ofthe appeal. We therefore reserve the issue ofwhether the Petitioner meets this evidentiary criterion. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Per the foregoing discussion, the Petitioner has not established that she meets at least three of the 
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), and has therefore not met the initial evidence 
requirements for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. However, because SCOPS 
included concluding statements suggesting that the Petitioner met three of the criteria, we will briefly 
discuss whether she has shown that she has sustained national or international acclaim in her field and 
is one of the small percentage at the very top of her field. 
In a final merits determination, we examine and weigh the totality of the evidence to determine 
whether the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is one of the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in 
the field through extensive documentation. As extraordinary ability is an elite level ofaccomplishment 
whose recognition necessarily entails a judgement call, it cannot be established through meeting at 
least three of the evidentiary criteria alone. The final merits determination is the ultimate statutory 
inquiry of whether the applicant has extraordinary ability as demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim. Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, at 395 (2022). 
The evidence indicates that the Petitioner has enjoyed a long career in teaching languages, in the 
United States and abroad. Her level of education and development of original teaching materials 
reflect her commitment to her profession, and the reference letters in the record show that she has 
earned the respect of her peers throughout her career. But the record does not include evidence that 
she has achieved national or international recognition. For example, the letters from her superiors in 
thel I County School District indicate that she is a valued member of the teaching staff atc=J
lbut the record does not include evidence showing the same level of recognition 
in the broader field of education, or even language education. And while she has shown that her ESOL 
6 
I 
teaching methodology and materials have attracted interest from two potential clients, she has not 
demonstrated that this level of possible implementation ofher work places is indicative ofthe requisite 
sustained acclaim. Similarly, she has not shown that her current or past salary reflects compensation

reasons, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of extraordinary ability.
