Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner describes herself as chief executive and she seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(1 )(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner warranted a favorable determination in the case's final merits 
analysis. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the 
burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility to U.S . Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 
(AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de novo. Matter ofChristo 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 
537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)- (iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met five of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied three ofthe criteria relating to prizes or awards, authorship 
of scholarly articles, and performing in a leading or critical role but that she had not satisfied the 
criteria associated with membership or original contributions. SCOPS also decided the totality of her 
evidence did not waITant a favorable determination in the final merits determination. On appeal, the 
Petitioner maintains that she meets the evidentiary criteria relating to membership and original 
contributions as well as satisfying the requirements in the final merits. After reviewing all the evidence 
in the record, we conclude she has not demonstrated she is eligible for this immigration benefit.

As the Petitioner has submitted the requisite initial evidence we will evaluate whether she has

Preliminary matters. We begin with the Petitioner's appellate claim that SCOPS "was considering the 
wrong field of endeavor altogether" when they performed their final merits analysis to evaluate whether 
she is among the small percentage at the vecy top of her field of endeavor. Later under this same claim, 
the appeal brief provides that "[p]er the USCIS Decision, the analysis appears to focus on the absence of 
CEO-level notoriety, that reasoning is misplaced and inconsistent with both the plain language of the 
statute and cunent policy guidance." 
While we agree with the Petitioner that SCOPS was inconect when it stated "[t]he beneficiary now holds 
the position of CEO," a review of the final merits determination reveals that despite that misstatement, 
SCOPS did not place any focus on the aspect of whether or not she had presented achievements 
commensurate with someone operating at that high executive level. In other words, it was a harmless 
en-or and even though the Petitioner claims that "[p ]er the USCIS Decision, the analysis appears to focus 
on the absence of CEO-level notoriety, that reasoning is misplaced and inconsistent with both the plain 
language ofthe statute and current policy guidance," we disagree that they committed error to that extent. 
It is not enough to demonstrate en-ors in an agency's decision; a petitioner must also establish they 
were prejudiced by the mistakes. Shinseki v. Sanders, 556 U.S. 396, 409 (2009); Molina-Martinez v. 
United States, 578 U.S. 189, 203 (2016). As the Petitioner has not demonstrated she was prejudiced by 
SCOPS' error, such a lapse in propriety is harmless and is insufficient grounds upon which to base this 
appeal. En-ors can be overlooked when they had no bearing on the substance of an agency's 
decision. Aguilar v. Garland, 60 F.4th 401,407 (8th Cir. 2023) (citing Prohibition Juice Co. v. United 
States Food &Drug Admin., 45 F.4th 8, 24 (D.C. Cir. 2022)). The party that "seeks to have a judgment 
set aside because of an enoneous ruling canies the burden of showing that prejudice resulted." 
Shinseki, 556 U.S. at 409 (quoting Palmer v. Hoffman, 318 U.S. 109, 116 (1943)); Molina-Martinez, 
578 U.S. at 203. 
Next, the Petitioner argues that she met some ofthe regulatocy criteria under step one that SCOPS did not 
grant. But that bears no relevance to her claims in the final merits determination where our focus shifts 
from the standards as expressed in each ofthe regulatocy criteria to whether she has demonstrated she has 
achieved sustained national or international acclaim, that she is one ofthe small percentage at the vecy 
top of the field of endeavor, and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. So, we will not devote space considering whether individual criteria were 
satisfied as it would have no effect on our analysis in the final merits. However, we will still consider 
whether her claims relating to those criteria demonstrate she is among the top percentage in her field 
and merits an approval.

We also note that the Petitioner may not narrow or limit the field in which we compare her to only women. 
In the appeal brief, she states: 
In this scenario, [the Petitioner's] achievements manifestly satisfy-and indeed surpass­
the rigorous evidentiary requirements of 8 C.F.R. § 204.S(h) for first-preference 
immigrant classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. Her case stands as a 
compelling testament to the advancement of women in the technology sector, a field 
where they occupy fewer than 3% of top executive positions in Brazil, thereby 
emphasizing the "extreme rarity of such achievements in the market context" and 
fulfilling the requirement to demonstrate that the petitioner is "one of that small 
percentage who has risen to the ve1y top ofthe field ofendeavor" (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), 
Policy Manual Vol. 6, Part F, Ch. 2). 
It is "not the title ... but rather the person's performance in the role that dete1mines whether 
the role is (or was) critical" per USCIS Policy Manual, and statistical underrepresentation 
underscores the exceptional nature of Mrs.I ts success. As less than 3% ofN1/N2 
technology executive positions are held by women in Brazil, this reinforces the regulatory 
requirement that the petitioner is "within that small percentage who has risen to the very 
top of the field." 
It is not the Petitioner's particularized narrow group within her field that is the determining factor as 
to whether she is an alien with extraordinary ability under the Act. Rather, it is whether her evidence 
demonstrates that she "is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor," 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) and "that the alien has sustained national or international acclaim 
and that his or her achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nothing in the statute, the regulation, nor USCIS 
policy appears to support the position that an alien may restrict their field to a small subset of limited 
peers as the Petitioner appears to have attempted here. 
Concluding otherwise would render the requirement meaningless that the Petitioner establish she is 
among the small percentage at the very top of her field by allowing her to narrow her field until she 
ranks among the top of a small group in that so-called field. See Buletini v. INS, 860 F. Supp. 1222, 
1229 (E.D. Mich. 1994) (finding an alien's area ofextraordinary ability was not limited to the specific 
study of nephrology but rather was within the general field of medical science). 
Awards. Turning our attention to the Petitioner's appellate arguments about her award, the brief states 
"USCIS acknowledges this criterion, but in its Final Merits Determination ... directly reverts the 
analysis for the USCIS Officer's opinion, rather than the case's material substance or legal context." 
We do not agree that the adjudicating officer inserted their own opinion in substitution for the type of 
analysis required in a final merits determination. The officer recounted the award and type ofmaterial 
the Petitioner submitted in support of her award, then concluded that a single award, even a notable 
one that was adequate to meet the regulatory requirement of being a lesser award, did not rise to the 
standard here. Despite this single achievement occurring at a notable level, this award is not 
4 
representative of sustained acclaim, nor is a single accolade indicative of the very high standard to 
present more extensive documentation. See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723 at 59 (1990); Employment-Based 
Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. at 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). We are not persuaded that SCOPS erred in 
their analysis. 
Memberships. SCOPS determined the Petitioner's evidence and claims were insufficient to meet the 
membership criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). While the Petitioner's appeal directly contests that 
determination in the appeal, whether she met the standards under the criterion in step one is not the type 
ofanalysis we employ in a final merits determination. Instead, we evaluate how the Petitioner claims that 
her acclaim and achievements propel her to be among the top percentage in her field. But she has not 
addressed that issue in her appeal. 
The Petitioner has a single membership in an association in her field. The association accepted her 
membership in 2021 and that membership is limited to women. While this may represent a notable 
membership for females in the Petitioner's field in her home country, this sex-restricted organization in 
her field appears to amount to a lesser level of accomplishment that's significantly below the stature 
associated with those who are among that small percentage who have risen to the ve1y top of their field 
of endeavor. Additionally, the statute and regulations require the Petitioner to demonstrate that her 
national or international acclaim has been sustained. See section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A)(i), and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). A single instance of membership occurring 
approximately two decades after entering the field, and three years before filing this petition, is not 
consistent with sustained national or international acclaim. 
Contributions. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not meet the plain language requirements ofthe 
original contributions ofmajor significance criterion under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). The Petitioner lists 
her acclaim stemming from work on her employer's! lthe book she authored on fuzzy 
logic, and her participation at symposia. 
She claims that the I Iset new standards for shipping fleet efficiency and it impacted more 
than the company she worked for, it "catalyzed broader industry change," and it's "ripple effect" "inspired 
other companies within the maritime logistics sector to pursue similar technological transformations." 
While it appears her efforts on this project had a lasting impact on the logistics and the relevant 
information technology areas in her home country, less apparent was its effect on the broader field. 
Neither she nor her supporting evidence illustrate this has sufficient influence in the field as a whole. The 
level at which the Petitioner's contributions have impacted her field, as a whole, is the determining factor 
as to whether the Petitioner is among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor and has sustained national or international acclaim at such an elevated level. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(2). 
The fuzzy logic research she performed and the publication of a subsequent book appear to be an original 
application of an existing concept to the area of modem computer engineering. What's not apparent is 
what level of impact this had on the field. Support letters noted efficiency gains made possible by her 
application of fuzzy logic, but only referenced the I Iwe discussed above and found 
inadequate. See Hamal v. DHS, No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), 
aff'd sub nom. Hamalv. DHS, No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *3 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) (upholding 
the decision that a petitoiner did not establish that he significantly influenced the field). One author

Regarding her participation at various symposia, many professional fields regularly hold conferences 
and symposia to present new work, to discuss new findings, and to network with other 
professionals. Professional associations, businesses, educational institutions, and government 
agencies promote and sponsor these conferences. Participation in these events, however, does not 
necessarily result in an original contribution ofmajor significance in the field and the Petitioner hasn't 
illustrated that her participation did so here. 
The amount that others are relying on her work does not rise to the level of this classification's overall 
requirements. While her work is valuable, when considering the totality of the evidence, the degree at 
which others have used her methods falls short of demonstrating (1) that her achievements have been 
recognized in the field of expertise through extensive documentation or (2) that her level of expertise 
places her among the small percentage at the very top of the field. 
Scholarly articles. The Petitioner's single publication is noteworthy, but authorship and publication do 
not automatically place one at the top of the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(2), (providing that publications should be evaluated to determine whether they were indicative 
ofbeing one ofthat small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor and enjoying 
sustained national or international acclaim). The Petitioner did not demonstrate how her limited 
publication record is consistent with having a career of acclaimed work and sustained national or 
international acclaim. The Petitioner did not submit evidence showing the significance of her 
authorship or how her publication compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top ofthe field. 
See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, at 59; section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Here, the Petitioner did not 
establish that her authorship reflects being among the small percentage at the very top ofthe field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) and 56 Fed. Reg. at 30704. 
Leading or critical role. After granting the leading or critical role criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3 )(viii) 
under the adjudication's step one, in the final merits SCOPS acknowledged the Petitioner's performance 
for two organizations characterizing it as commendable. But they ultimately concluded that she did not 
demonstrate that her positions within those companies placed her among those at the top ofthe field. On 
appeal, the Petitioner alleges that SCOPS had "gone beyond the acceptable limits of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii) by requiring evidence that [the adjudicating officer] personally deems relevant, even 
though this is not specified required per the legislation." Here, it appears the Petitioner incorporates the 
adjudicative step one requirements into the second step in the final merits. But that is not the standard 
and the Petitioner does not identify any legal source to support that incorporation. 
Later, the Petitioner acknowledges the proper standard for the final merits determination as outlined in 
the USCIS Policy Manual for the SCOPS officer to "consider all evidence in the totality." See generally 
6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(2). Even though she asserts within the appeal that her work 
6 
has "demonstrably impacted not only her organizations but also the field at large," she vaguely points 
to "[t]he recognition of her work by independent experts" and the single but notable award "as clear 
evidence that her contributions were both original and acclaimed on a national scale, satisfying the 
'leading or critical role' and 'sustained national or international acclaim' criteria." 
First, she seemingly conflates her leading and critical role arguments with those about her 
contributions to the field. But more importantly, her appeal does not adhere to the same USCIS Policy 
Manual she cites. When the USCIS Policy Manual discusses the final merits determination process, 
it requires more than vague references to a petitioner's supporting materials when it provides: 
In all cases, the petitioner must provide sufficient context regarding the above evidence 
and considerations to demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to 
establish the person's extraordinary ability in the totality of the circumstances. This 
means that the petitioner must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and 
how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or international 
acclaim and recognition in their field of expertise. 
Id. Ifher contention is that SCOPS did not properly consider all ofher evidence relating to her leading 
or critical roles with two organizations, as the above agency policy provides, it remains her burden to 
provide sufficient context about the evidence and explain its significance. But she has not done so in 
this appeal, nor did she achieve that in her previous claims about her leading and critical roles placing 
her among the top percentage of the field. Lacking is a salient argument to show her roles have 
elevated her to be situated among her peers in the top of the field. A petitioner's burden of proof 
comprises both the initial burden of production, as well as the ultimate burden of persuasion. Matter 
ofY-B-, 21 I&N Dec. 1136, 1142 n.3 (BIA 1998) and she has not made such a showing in this appeal. 
While we agree that she has proven her value to organizations and could collectively be considered as 
representative of a successful career, these roles, by themselves, are not extensive evidence of the 
Petitioner's national or international acclaim. As discussed above, the remaining record does not 
demonstrate the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim or that she has risen to the very 
top of her field. 
Conclusion. During her two decades in the field, the Petitioner rests her acclaim and achievements in the 
field on one award, one membership, one publication, two contributions to the field, and her performance 
for two organizations. While she is an accomplished information technology executive, she seeks a 
highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their respective 
fields, rather than for those progressing toward that goal. USCIS has long held that even athletes 
performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. 
Matter o_f Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Considering the full measure of the 
Petitioner's ability and achievements, the level of her national or international acclaim and the extent to 
which her achievements have been recognized in the field are not indicative of a record of sustained 
acclaim. Also, she has not submitted extensive documentation exhibiting she has attained a level of 
expertise placing her among that small percentage that has risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor.

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's 
burden to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that 
burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
