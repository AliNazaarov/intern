Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a dog sled racer, seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability in 
athletics. Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not establish that she bas either a one­
time achievement under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) or bas met least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We dismissed a subsequent appeal. The matter is now before us on motion to 
reconsider. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon review, we will dismiss the 
motion. 
I. LAW 
A motion to reconsider must establish that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of 
law or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record of proceedings 
at the time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(3). Our review on motion is limited to reviewing our 
latest decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(ii). We may grant motions that satisfy these requirements and 
demonstrate eligibility for the requested benefit. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability who: have 
extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; seek to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and whose entry will offer substantial prospective benefits 
for the country. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis for the adjudication of 
extraordinary ability petitions filed under section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. First, a petitioner must 
meet the initial evidentiary requirements by either showing that they have a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award) or by providing evidence that meets at least three 
ofthe ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). If a petitioner meets these initial requirements, 
we then conduct a final merits determination which considers whether the totality of the evidence 
demonstrates sustained national or international acclaim and that the individual is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 
(9th Cir. 2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 
772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1342-43 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

The Petitioner has not provided a statute, regulation, precedent case, or policy stating that USCIS may 
not consider an award' s level of public recognition when determining whether it qualifies as a one­
time achievement under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The extraordinary ability statute and regulations do 
not define the term "one-time achievement" beyond equating it to a "major internationally recognized 
award" and implicitly differentiating such awards from "lesser nationally or internationally recognized 
prizes or awards" under the alternate evidentiary criteria. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) ofthe Act, 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i); see also Rijal, 772 F.Supp.2d at 1345 ("Nothing in either [the Act] or the regulations 
implementing it explains how USCIS or a reviewing court is to differentiate between 'major' and 
lesser awards."). 
The Petitioner argues that Congress, by concluding in H.R. Rep. No. 101-723(1), at 59 (1990), 
reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 6739, that "admission under this category is to be reserved for 
that small percentage of individuals who have risen to the top of their field of endeavor," intended that 
the top prize in a field of endeavor always constitutes a one-time achievement regardless of that 
award's recognition level with the broader public. First, however, the House report is not itself a law, 
regulation, precedent case, or otherwise binding policy. Further, the quoted passage does not describe 
what constitutes a one-time achievement but instead describes the classification's overall requirements 
after stating that eligibility may be demonstrated either through such an achievement or through "a 
career of acclaimed work in the field." Id. The report also states that the "extensive documentation" 
is required to show extraordinary ability "may include ... media accounts of the alien's contributions 
to his profession," indicating that publicity is a valid consideration in determining eligibility. 
As noted above, a one-time achievement is sufficient in and of itself to meet the initial evidentiary 
requirements, while a person with a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize or award 
would still need to establish eligibility under two other criteria in order for USCIS to proceed to the 
final merits determination. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The regulation therefore implicitly requires a one­
time achievement to be of a higher caliber than a "lesser" award - simply being nationally or 
internationally recognized in the field is insufficient. Id. Federal courts have repeatedly recognized 
that one factor which may distinguish a "major" award from a "lesser" one is its level of recognition 
by the general public. See, e.g., Visinscaia, 4 F.Supp.3d at 133 (upholding AAO dismissal which 
considered media coverage and general public recognition of a prize in an "esoteric field" when 
adjudicating a one-time achievement). The Petitioner has not provided any binding legal authority to 
the contrary. 
The Petitioner also contends that our reasoning is legally incorrect because it means that a person 
cannot qualify under the one-time achievement provision if they work in a smaller or more obscure 
field. We acknowledge that the law only requires a person's field of extraordinary ability to fall within 
the areas of athletics, business, education, or the arts, and does not require that field to be highly 
publicized or well-known to the general public. Section 203(b )(l )(A) of the Act. It is not apparent, 
however, that this means that individuals in any field, no matter how obscure, must be able to qualify 
specifically through a one-time achievement, given that an alternate route to eligibility exists. In cases 
where an individual cannot obtain a one-time achievement, including instances where it is not

Finally, the Petitioner contends that the only relevant consideration in determining whether a prize is 
a one-time achievement is whether that prize demonstrates the winner's "status as one of the small 
percentage at the very top of the field." However, as noted, the Petitioner conflates the two parts of 
the Kazarian analysis. The issue of whether a person is one of that small percentage at the very top 
of their field is only considered during the final merits determination. Our analysis does not reach that 
stage unless the Petitioner first fulfills the initial evidentiary requirements either through a one-time 
achievement or through evidence meeting at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1119-20. She has not done so here.
