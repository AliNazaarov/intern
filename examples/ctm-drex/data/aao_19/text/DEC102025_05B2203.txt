Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur in the field of restaurant, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b )( 1 )(A). This first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner 
established that be satisfies the initial evidentiary requirements for this classification, he did not 
demonstrate that he has sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small percentage 
at the very top of his field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
On appeal, the Petitioner presents additional evidence and contends that by agreeing that the Petitioner 
has met at least three of the regulatory criteria, the Petitioner has met his burden as an individual with 
sustained national or international acclaim, and SCOPS should have analyzed on the final merits 
determination whether this sustained national or international acclaim has been maintained and 
whether he has risen to the very top of the field. The Petitioner argues that SCOPS overlooked the 
evidence submitted, abused its discretion, and applied a higher standard of review than the 
preponderance of the evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 1 
We adopt and affirn1 SCOPS' decision regarding its final merits determination. See Matter of 
Burbano, 20 I&N Dec. 872, 874 (BIA 1994); see also Giday v. INS, 113 F.3d 230, 234 (D.C. Cir. 
1997) (noting that the practice of adopting and affirming the decision below "is not only common 
practice, but universally accepted").

While the Petitioner claims that he meets his burden as an individual with sustained national or 
international acclaim because he satisfies at least three of the regulatory criteria, objectively meeting 
the regulatory criteria in the first step alone does not establish that the Petitioner in fact meets the 
requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. The question of whether the petitioner is 
one ofthat small percentage who has risen to the very top ofthe field ofendeavor and enjoys sustained 
national or international acclaim is addressed in the second step of the analysis (final merits 
determination). See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2). In all cases, the petitioner 
must provide sufficient context regarding the evidence submitted and considerations to demonstrate 
that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary ability in the 
totality of the circumstances. Id. This means that the petitioner must establish the significance of the 
submitted evidence and how it demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or 
international recognition in their field of expertise. Id. 
Although the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS overlooked the evidence submitted in its entirety, SCOPS 
clearly acknowledged and analyzed various documents on record but concluded overall that the quality 
of the evidence lacked probative value in supporting that he possesses the high level of expertise 
required for the EB-1 classification. Under the preponderance standard, we consider not only the 
quantity, but also the quality of the evidence, including its relevance, probative value, and credibility. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 
1989). Moreover, SCOPS is not required to name every single piece of evidence on record. When 
SCOPS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition and has made adequate findings, it will not 
be required to specifically address each claim the petitioner makes, nor is it necessary for it to address 
every piece of evidence the petitioner presents. Guaman-Loja v. Holder, 707 F.3d 119, 123 (1st Cir. 
2013) (citing Martinez v. INS, 970 F.2d 973, 976 (1st Cir. 1992); see also Kazemzadeh v. US. Atty. 
Gen., 577 F.3d 1341, 1351 (11th Cir. 2009); Casalena v. US. INS, 984 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1993). 
Here, the record reflects that in its final merits determination, SCOPS considered all evidence in the 
totality even though SCOPS did not address each piece of evidence individually, articulated the 
specific reasons as to why SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner has not demonstrated by a 
preponderance of the evidence that he has extraordinary ability, and made adequate findings in 
concluding the Petitioner has not demonstrate that he has sustained national or international acclaim 
and his achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise, indicating he is one of that small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act; 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
