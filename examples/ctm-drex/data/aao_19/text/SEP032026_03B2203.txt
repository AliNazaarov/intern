Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner works in communications and seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant 
classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner is a communications specialist, broadcaster, and producer with approximately two 
decades of experience working in audiovisual media and public communications. 
A. Step One (One-time Achievement and Regulatory Criteria) 
The Petitioner claims he received a one-time achievement, but SCOPS informed him the award was 
not at a level needed to show that his achievement was a major, internationally recognized award. 
Because the Petitioner did not establish that he has received a major, internationally recognized award, 
he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before 
SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed he met seven of the regulatory criteria. SCOPS decided that the 
Petitioner failed to satisfy any of the claimed criteria relating to: prizes or awards, membership, 
published material, judging, original contributions, performing in a leading or critical role, or 
commercial success. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that he meets the same seven evidentiary 
criteria. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated his eligibility for this highly restrictive immigrant classification.

The Petitioner may rely on accomplishments in a specialized area if he demonstrates that his acclaim 
and recognition extend to the broader field at the required level. Such evidence may include prizes or 
awards that are nationally or internationally recognized in the broader field, contributions of major 
significance to the field as a whole, or a showing that he is among the small percentage who have risen 
to the very top of the wider field of endeavor, as described in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2)-(3). Also, 
illustrating sustained national or international acclaim through extensive documentation indicating that 
his achievements have been recognized in the larger field, consistent with section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of 
the Act. 
2. One-time Achievement 
The regulation establishes a multi-step analysis in demonstrating extraordinary ability. In the first 
step, an alien may submit evidence of a one-time achievement. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3); see also 
6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. Although the regulation 
provides that a one-time achievement represents a major, internationally recognized award, it does not 
specifically indicate any qualifying one-time achievements. Id. We therefore look to legislative 
history and congressional intent in further defining a one-time achievement. 
The House Report specifically cited the Nobel Prize as an example of a one-time achievement. See 
H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at 
*6739. Also, Congress intended that "admission under this category is to be reserved for that small 
percentage ofindividuals who have risen to the very top oftheir field of endeavor." Id. So, consistent 
with legislative history, a one-time achievement must be interpreted very narrowly, with only a small 
handful of awards qualifying as major, internationally recognized awards. 
The selection of Nobel Laureates, the example Congress provided, is reported in the top international 
media regardless of the nationality of the awardees, reflects a familiar name to the public at large, and 
includes a large cash prize. While an internationally recognized award could conceivably constitute a 
one-time achievement without meeting all of those elements, it is clear from Congress' example that 
3 
the award must be global in scope and internationally recognized. Other acknowledged one-time 
achievements include, as a non-exhaustive list of examples, an Academy Award, an Emmy, a 
Grammy, a Pulitzer Prize, and an Olympic Medal. 
Furthermore, the claim that a major, internationally recognized award may not exist in a particular 
field does not mean that we should diminish the nature of the one-time achievement and accept the 
highest award a person can achieve in their particular field as a major, internationally recognized 
award. In cases where a person cannot obtain a one-time achievement, including instances where it is 
not available in a field, "[a]n alien can also qualify on the basis of a career of acclaimed work in the 
field" by satisfying three of the ten categories of evidence. See H.R. Rep. at 59 and 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). Moreover, awards that may be nationally or internationally recognized in the field do 
not necessarily demonstrate that they are also major or consistent with one-time achievements. In 
those instances, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) allows a person to submit lesser nationally 
or internationally recognized awards for excellence in the field. 
Under this requirement, the Petitioner claimed the I Iaward he received in 2010. 
According to the award's founder this accolade is an international award with the purpose of 
celebrating and honoring SCOPS 
noted "the evidence in the record does not show the petitioner is a recipient of a major, internationally

is widely reported by international media, is recognized by the general public, or garnered attention 
comparable to other major, globally recognized awards such as the Nobel Prize winners." SCOPS 
acknowledged the letter from the issuing entity but concluded it did not describe adequate coverage 
of this award that a one-time achievement demands. 
On appeal, the Petitioner notes that SCOPS did not discuss the recognition the award received from a 
city in Florida and media recognition of the award that is essential for the promotion of the I I I lmedia professionals and artists. Even though this award might have been important for 
the Petitioner's career, that does not transform it into an award on par with a Nobel Prize, an Academy 
Award, an Emmy, a Grammy, a Pulitzer Prize, or an Olympic Medal. The Petitioner has not offered 
evidence to support his claims that the I Iaward is a qualifying one-time achievement akin 
to an Academy Award, an Emmy, a Grammy, a Pulitzer Prize, and an Olympic Medal. 
3. "Documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner claimed the I Iaward under this criterion as well. SCOPS determined that 
the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion, noting the record lacked evidence of the 
award's national or international recognition in his field of endeavor. We note-as the Petitioner 
claimed in the initial filing-his field of endeavor is audiovisual media and public communications.

But we do not find those arguments adequate to satisfy this criterion's national or international 
recognition requirements. We conclude the record lacks the award's requisite recognition, he attempts 
to narrow the field adversely impacting whether it was issued for excellence, and through that attempt 
to thin out the field the Petitioner has not presented an award in his field of endeavor. 
First, it is unclear why the Petitioner referred to users policy on scholastic awards. Second, users 
determines whether a prize or award is a lesser nationally or internationally recognized prize or award 
for excellence in the person's field of endeavor. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual 
F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. In making this determination, users focuses on the 
recognition of the particular prize or award, rather than merely on the reputation of the issuing 
organization, the prominence of the individual who signed the award, or the reputation of the 
competition or institution associated with it. 
National or international recognition does not automatically transfer from a well-known competition, 
institution, organization, or individual to every prize, certificate, or honor issued under its name. See 
Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (explaining that "the focus is on the 
award, not the awarding organization" and finding that evidence concerning a festival did not establish 
the recognition of the particular award); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.e. 
2013). Instead, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular accolade is recognized nationally or 
internationally as an award for excellence in the field. So, an entity or event may be nationally or 
internationally known while particular accolades it issues are not themselves nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence. 
users policy reinforces the requirement that the focus is on the award and not the issuer ( explaining 
that the agency considers the "national or international significance of the awards or prizes in the 
field") and it addresses the requisite level of recognition noting that "national or international 
recognition is most often associated with awards given to individuals at the highest level in a given 
field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(8)(1). A petitioner, however, must submit 
evidence establishing the significance and recognition ofthat specific award, rather than relying solely 
on the existence or reputation of the competition, institution, or issuing organization. Relevant 
evidence may include national or international media coverage of the particular award, material 
demonstrating that the award is widely known or regarded as prestigious within the field, or other 
independent evidence demonstrating that the accolade carries national or international recognition for 
excellence in the field. The weight of such evidence depends on its specificity and probative value. 
Within the appeal, although the Petitioner states that his field of endeavor is communications, the 
argumentshe makes relating to his award under this criterion attempt to narrow that field to "the 
communications field." While an accolade such as the I Iaward may be an 
honor for an individual operating in I Icommunications, it is one in too narrow a field to 
constitute a recognition of the Petitioner's excellence. Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 erv. 3372 (PAE), 
2024 WL 69056, at *5 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding an alien's medal in a student competition too

4. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that he is a member of an association in his field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL

5. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, the 
Petitioner must establish the circulation statistics are high relative to other similar forms of media. 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided several articles under this criterion, but SCOPS determined they were 
inadequate. SCOPS noted that much of the evidence did not contain elements that the regulation 
mandates, such as the author or the date the article was published. For the material that contained all 
the required elements, SCOPS noted some of it was advertisements or promotional materials and that 
the record lacked circulation statistics to demonstrate the articles appeared in one of the required 
publication types. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that the regulation's clear text-that "evidence shall include the title, 
date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation"-is actually optional and that 
"[i]mmigration adjudications are also governed by flexible evidentiary principles emphasizing 
probative value and fundamental fairness rather than technical compliance." 
The regulations have the force and effect of law and are binding on all USCIS employees, and we 
cannot simply ignore those textual requirements. Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 693, 701 (BIA 2023) 
7

motion, not to its inclination, but to its judgment; and its judgment is to be guided by sound legal 
principles." Id. (internal quotation marks and alteration omitted). In the end, we will not ignore the

B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner claims he meets eight criteria, because his arguments fail on 
any of the six criteria discussed above, that means he cannot numerically meet the required number of 
at least three criteria and it is unnecessary for us to make a decision on these additional grounds. As 
the Petitioner cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we reserve his additional claims or issues. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) 
(citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25-26 (1976) (finding agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); see also Matter 
of W-F-D-, 29 I&N Dec. 854, 858 n.5 (BIA 2026) ( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal 
where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring a petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. "In creating the 'extraordinary ability' visa, Congress hoped to 'draw the world's best and brightest 
to American shores' by giving 'preferential treatment to highly talented immigrants."' Branski v. USCIS, 
No. 25-CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at *11 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) (quoting Amin, 24 F.4th at 386). 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and it is unnecessary that 
we provide further analysis of the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see 
also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 8 
C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative
