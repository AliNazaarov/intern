Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a human resources professional, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not 
establish that she meets the initial evidence requirements for this classification, either through her 
receipt ofa major, internationally recognized award, or, in the alternative, by submitting evidence that 
she satisfies at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria set forth in the regulations. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that "the criteria 
presented ... were not properly analyzed by the Director." 1 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability immigrant classification under section 203(b)(l)(A) 
of the Act if: 
• They have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which 
has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and their achievements 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation; 
• They seek to enter United States to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• Their entry will substantially benefit the United States in the future. 
1 The Petitioner stated on the Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion, that she would submit a brief and/or additional 
evidence directly to our office within 30 days of filing her appeal. The record indicates that she has not submitted a brief 
or any supplemental evidence during or after that 30-day period. We consider the record complete as presently constituted. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, evidence that they have received a major, internationally recognized 
award). Ifa petitioner does not submit this evidence, then they must provide documentation that meets 
at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing 
a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number 
of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also, Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 
F.4th 383, 391-392 (5 th Cir. 2022); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner, who has a bachelor's degree in psychology from a Brazilian university, states that she 
has worked primarily in the human resources field since completing her degree in 2010. She indicates 
her intent to work as the managing partner and chief executive officer of a new Florida-based human 
resources consulting and recruitment business if granted lawful permanent residence in the United 
States. She claims eligibility for this classification based on extraordinary ability in the human 
resources and occupational psychology fields. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner does not claim to qualify for extraordinary ability classification based on a one-time 
achievement. Accordingly, she must submit evidence meeting at least three of the ten initial 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Director acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted evidence relating to five of the ten criteria, 
including evidence relating to her membership in an association in her field, her participation as a 
judge of the work of others, her contributions in her field, her performance in leading or critical roles

(viii) and (ix). 2 The Director concluded she submitted sufficient evidence to satisfy only one of the 
five claimed criteria. Specifically, the Director determined that the Petitioner demonstrated that she 
has participated as the judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field and thus met the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv).

outstanding achievements in the field include, but are not limited to, instances where the person's 
membership was based on attaining a level of education or certain amount of experience in the field, 
or where the membership is a requirement, compulsory or otherwise, for employment in an occupation.

2. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
A petitioner may satisfy the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) by providing evidence that (1) 
they have performing in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments; and (2) these 
organizations or establishments have a distinguished reputation. 3 The petitioner must satisfy both 
elements to meet the plain language requirements of the criterion. 
The Director acknowledged that the Petitioner submitted letters from former supervisors and 
colleagues describing her work-related responsibilities and achievements, as well as "general 
information" regarding the companies that employed her. However, the Director concluded that the 
evidence was insufficient to demonstrate that she performed in a leading or critical role and that she 
has worked for an organization or establishment that has a distinguished reputation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner objects to the Director's determination that "the establishment in which she 
worked most of her career [was] not to be considered as of distinguished reputation." She does not 
contest the Director's conclusion that the previously submitted evidence was insufficient to 
demonstrate her performance in a leading or critical role. Accordingly, she has not overcome the 
Director's determination regarding that first element of this evidentiary criterion. 
Nevertheless, we will address the Petitioner's suggestion that the previously provided evidence was 
sufficient to demonstrate the distinguished reputation of the organizations that employed her. To 
determine whether an organization or establishment has a distinguished reputation, its relative size or 
longevity is not, by itself: a determining factor, but is considered together with other information. 
Other relevant factors for evaluating an organization or establishment's reputation can include 
evidence of the scale of its customer base or relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). USCIS applies a common dictionary definition of the term 
"distinguished" which means "marked by eminence, distinction or excellence." Id.

In a letter accompanying the Petitioner's response to the RFE, she acknowledged that she must 
demonstrate that she earned more than an "above average" salary to meet the plain language of this 
criterion. The Petitioner argued that "[l]ogically, an 'above average salary would be any amount that

She farther reasoned that a "high salary" would imply a comparable increase of "at least 50%" and 
asserted that the Petitioner's earnings meet this threshold. We agree with the Director's conclusion 
that the record does not contains sufficient support for the Petitioner's assertions that a "high salary" 
in any given field is one that is "at least 50%" higher than average. Moreover, the salary surveys the 
Petitioner provided are variable, indicating average salaries ranging from under R$ 3,000 to over R$ 
5,000. While the Petitioner's documented earnings exceeded these averages, they are lower than the 
"high" salaries reported in most of the submitted salary surveys. 
When evaluating whether a comparison between a petitioner's documented remuneration and the 
remuneration in a given survey is accurate, USCIS may consider factors such as the description of the 
occupation and the validity of the survey. Here, the Petitioner provided salary survey data for the 
position of "occupational psychologists" but has not shown that this role is the same or similar to the 
human resources development coordinator position she held in 2015. In addition, some websites, like 
Glassdoor and VAGAS, rely on user-reported salary data, which may not be a valid comparison if too 
few users reported their salaries or the data is otherwise not credible or reliable. See generally, 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). For example, the evidence the Petitioner submitted from 
VAGAS indicates a higher average salary for human resources coordinators than it does for human 
resources managers, an incongruity that may reflect that only small pools of data were available for 
one or both of these occupations. 
Finally, as noted by the Director, the Petitioner has documented her earnings for the 2015 calendar 
year but has not provided any comparative salary data for that period. Overall, based on the evidence 
submitted, the record does not sufficiently demonstrate that the Petitioner commanded a "high salary" 
in relation to others in the field as required by the plain language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
Accordingly, we conclude that she has not met this criterion.

B. Summary and Reserved Issue 
The Petitioner must demonstrate that she meets at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(i)-(x) to 
satisfy the initial evidence requirement for this classification. She claims that she can meet five of 
these ten criteria. However, the record does not establish that the Petitioner meets any of the three 
evidentiary criteria discussed above. Because the Petitioner cannot meet the initial evidentiary 
requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), detailed discussion of the remaining two 
claimed criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and (v), cannot change the outcome of the appeal. 
Therefore, we will reserve these criteria for future consideration if the need arises. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not submitted the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria. As a result, we need not provide the type of final 
merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise that we 
have reviewed the record in the aggregate and conclude that it does not support a finding that the 
Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification. USCIS has long held that even athletes 
performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" 
standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not 
shown that the significance of her work in the human resources profession is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner has 
garnered sustained national or international acclaim in the field, and that she is one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). In fact, the record does not contain evidence that she has worked in her 
field since 2016. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
