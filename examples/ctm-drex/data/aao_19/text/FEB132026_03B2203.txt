Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is an agricultural economist who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record 
did not establish that the Petitioner had a major, internationally recognized award, nor did she 
demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility to 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance ofthe evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo . Matter of Christo 's Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met five of the regulatory criteria. 
SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied one criterion, the authorship of scholarly articles but that 
she had not satisfied the criteria associated with prizes or awards, original contributions, performing 
in a leading or critical role, or a high salary or remuneration. On appeal, the Petitioner maintains that 
she meets the same evidentiary criteria. After reviewing all the evidence in the record, we conclude 
the record establishes she satisfies the scholarly articles criterion, but no other regulatory criteria. 
1. "Documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized prizes 
or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements, all ofwhich must be met to satisfy the regulation. 
According to the plain language of the regulation the evidence must establish: (1) the alien is the 
recipient of the prizes or the awards; (2) those accolades are nationally or internationally recognized; 
and (3) each prize or award is one for excellence in the field of endeavor. 
The Petitioner claimed three awards qualified under this criterion offering evidence of the awards, 
information about the entity that issued the awards, and two support letters asserting the significance 
of the awards. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion 
concluding the record lacked probative material demonstrating the significance of the awards or that 
they were issued for excellence in the field. 
The Petitioner's appeal only lists the evidence she provided before SCOPS stating the information 
about the issuing entity "clearly explains thau lis an academic journal in Turkey published 
quarterly," and asserts that one ofthe support letters was not simply an unsupported conclusory letter 
as SCOPS alluded, but instead it was "very specific and objective evidence ofthe organization which 
granted the award." 
First, simply because an academic entity has a quarterly publication and issues awards does not 
demonstrate those awards are nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence 
2 
in the field. National and international recognition results, not from the entity that issued the prize or 
award, but through the awareness of the accolade in the eyes of the field nationally or internationally. 
Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) (finding "the focus is on the award. not 
the awarding organization"); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013). A 
national or international level competition may issue lesser awards that merely receive local or regional 
recognition, which do not meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. The requisite 
recognition should be evident through specific means; for example but not limited to, national or 
international-level media coverage. 
Agency policy addresses the requisite level of a prize's or award's recognition noting we consider the 
"national or international significance of the awards or prizes in the field" and states "national or 
international recognition is most often associated with awards given to individuals at the highest level 
in a given field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. See also Guida v. Miller, No. 20-CV-01471-LB, 2021 WL 
568850, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 16, 2021) (upholding the detennination that awards must be nationally 
or internationally recognized). 
Second, the support letter the Petitioner raises on appeal does not address any of her awards and the 
author only mentions the issuing entity once in passing. This is inadequate to demonstrate the 
Petitioner's awards meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
2. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
The plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires the Petitioner to submit 
evidence of a "high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others 
in the field." That means a petitioner must provide comparative evidence for those performing similar 
work, during the same approximate time and geographic location, within the field. And the 
presentation of only average salaries in the field is generally inadequate to demonstrate a petitioner's 
earnings are high, or their other types of remuneration are significantly high, in relation to the rest of 
the field. 
The Petitioner initially provided evidence from her employer in her home country reflecting her 
monthly salary in 2016 was tl2,2566 (Turkish lira) converted to 4,154 U.S. dollars. This resulted in 
an annual salary of t147,072, and the employer's letter equated that amount to 49,848 U.S. dollars. 
The employer's letter also reflected the minimum monthly salary in Turkey in that same year was 440 
U.S. dollars per month without presenting a figure in Turkish lira. She also submitted material relating 
to yearly salaries for financial specialists in the United States in New Jersey, and other evidence 
relating to the United States for 2023. In response to the request for evidence, the Petitioner provided 
documentation relating to her social security in her home country, and a letter from an individual 
working in her former employer's agricultural department in her home country listing their salary in 
2016. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the requirements of this criterion. 
First, the Petitioner has provided evidence comparing her earnings in Turkey compared to a related 
occupation in the United States. Irrespective of which currency those salary figures were presented 
in, she must provide documentary evidence from the same geographic region in Turkey in which to 
3 
compare to her own salary. Agency policy provides that when a filing party makes such a comparison, 
the location and currency are relevant factors and it states: "Officers evaluate persons working outside 
of the United States based on the wage statistics or comparable evidence relevant to the applicable 
work location, rather than by simply converting the salary to U.S. dollars and then viewing whether 
that salary would be considered high in the United States." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(l). This is important for multiple reasons, the first ofwhich is salaries can vary widely 
based on the location, even within the same country. Also, what an occupation demands in one country 
is generally disconnected from what the markets might bear elsewhere in the world. 
Aside from that issue, we note the temporal disparity of the comparative evidence from 2023 when 
likened to the Petitioner's earnings data for 2016. So, any relative comparison should generally be 
from the same timeframe as the Petitioner's own earnings evidence. Several factors including inflation 
or developments in an occupation can cause salaries to change every year whether such amendments 
are gradual or more significant. Comparing earnings from disparate years is like comparing apples to 
crab apples-without the proper proportional perspective, one might mistake a handful for a harvest. 
See Xia v. Miller, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5817324, at *6 (D. Or. Nov. 18, 2021), report 
and recommendation adopted, No. 3:20-CV-01468-JR, 2021 WL 5825999 (D. Or. Dec. 6, 2021) 
(finding a petitioner must show that salary reports reflect an apples to apples, rather than an apples to 
oranges, comparison). Because the Petitioner provided earnings from 2016 but compared earnings 
from 2023, she did not offer appropriately similar data. See Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 
(PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (upholding the "record lacked sufficient 
'comparable data' as to [the petitioner's] compensation"). 
For the evidence from Turkey during the relevant timeframe, the Petitioner submitted a letter from 
someone who worked at her previous employer. The author of that letter stated they "are currently 
employed a~._______,~n an Agricultural Banking Department," and that in 2016 their annual salary 
was t43,800. First, the Petitioner only provided the letter but not any probative evidence to 
corroborate the claims presented in it, which is an evidentiary deficiency. But even if that were not 
the case, the salary comparison offered in the letter is not an adequate one as it only applied to one 
individual rather than a broader representation of the field as the regulation's plain language requires. 
Further, the letter lacks pertinent information only illustrating the person worked in the Agricultural 
Banking Department. It did not specify what occupation that individual occupied, nor did it present 
the duties they performed. Salary information for those performing work in a related but distinct 
occupation with different responsibilities is not a proper basis for comparison. Rather, the Petitioner 
must present evidence of objective earnings data showing they have earned a "high salary" or 
"significantly high remuneration" in comparison with those performing similar work during the same 
time period. See Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 955 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering 
professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. DHS, 420 F. App'x 
712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is not a 
comparison to others in the field); Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 2780815, 
at *6 (M.D. Fla Sept. 30, 2025) (upholding USCIS determination that a petitioner's comparative 
evidence must relate to their specific field and not a general characterization ofan overly broad field); 
Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus 
other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N.D. Ill. 1995) (comparing salary of 
NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen).

We agree with SCOPS conclusion that the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain

3. "Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). 
The primary requirements here are that the Petitioner's contributions in their field were original and 
they rise to the level of major significance in the field, rather than to a project or to an organization. 
See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (citing Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134). The regulatory phrase "major 
significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. Nielsen v. Preap, 586 U.S. 392,415 
(2019) (finding that every word and every provision in a statute is to be given effect and none should 
needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate another provision or to have no 
consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the Petitioner's work has 
significantly impacted the field. Further, the Petitioner's contributions must have already been 
realized rather than being potential, future improvements. The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying all of these elements to meet the plain language requirements ofthis criterion. 
SCOPS discussed the Petitioner's articles and support letters she submitted and dete1mined that the 
evidence in the record did not meet the requirements of this criterion. On appeal, the Petitioner 
advances a generalized claim about her support letters and states they specifically discussed her 
"contributions to the field of agri-finance and economics by creating credit card facilities for 
cooperatives and farmers through growth and expansion." She also claims press coverage of those 
banking programs, a letter from her previous employer, and awards from that employer demonstrate 
her eligibility here. 
About her letters, the only correspondence she specifically identifies in the appeal is from her former 
employer's chief executive officer, I Idescribed several instances of 
improvements that the Petitioner made in the bank, specifically in the Agricultural Banking Unit. 
While he describes some services she performed and information she shared with their customers, 
lacking is an explanation of how her work resulted in a contribution to the field of agricultural 
economics. The plain language ofthe phrase "contributions ofmajor significance in the field" requires 
evidence of an impact beyond one's employer and clients or customers and mandates the Petitioner's 
work has significantly impacted the broader field. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 394 (finding that 
contributions under this criterion require "specific information relating to the impact [ of a petitioner's 
work] on the field as a whole" and citing Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134). 
Pertaining to her remaining claims, she has not identified what evidence in the record constitutes press 
coverage of the relative banking programs, and she does not explain how awards that she might have 
received from her former employer demonstrate a contribution to the field rather than recognition of 
her performance with the employer. 
Ultimately, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that her contributions have had a sufficiently 
appreciable impact in the field, and as a result she has not met this criterion's requirements.

B. We Reserve the Petitioner's Remaining Claims 
We conclude that although the Petitioner satisfies the scholarly articles criterion, she does not meet 
the criteria for lesser prizes or awards, a high salary, or original contributions. While she argues and 
submits evidence for one additional criterion on appeal relating to performing in a leading or critical 
role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), it is unnecessary that we make a decision on this additional ground 
because she cannot numerically meet the required number ofcriteria. As the Petitioner cannot fulfill 
the initial evidentiaiy requirement of three criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we reserve the 
remaining issues/issue. Patel v. Garland, 596 U.S. 328, 332 (2022) (citing INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 
U.S. 24, 25-26 ( 1976) (finding agencies are not required to make "purely adviso1y findings" on issues 
that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision)); see also M-C-C-, 29 I&N Dec.401, 405-06 (BIA 2026) 
( declining to reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and we need not further 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 
784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits detennination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
