Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an engineering researcher, requests classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § l l 53(b)(1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively 
document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 201(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met 
two of the requested immigrant visa category's ten evidentiary requirements - one less than needed 
for a final merits determination. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that he satisfied the following 
three additional criteria: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in his field; 
• Memberships in organizations in the field requiring outstanding achievements of their 
members; and 
• Original contributions of major significance to the field. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), (ii), (v). 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he met the requisite minimum number of evidentiary criteria by submitting 
evidence of original contributions of major significance in his field. We will therefore withdraw the 
decision and remand the matter for a final merits determination and entry of a new decision. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )( 1 )( A )(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(implementing a two-step extraordinary ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(8), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that universities in the Petitioner's home country of South Korea awarded him a 
bachelor of science degree in chemical engineering and master of science and doctoral degrees in 
chemical and biological engmeenng. He has more than ten years' experience as an engineering 
researcher. 
At the time of the petition's filing, the Petitioner was a post-doctoral researcher at a U.S. university 
working to develop new catalysts to convert ethanol to jet fuel. He seeks to continue his engineering 
research in the United States.

On appeal, the Petitioner notes that SCOPS' decision in this matter contradicts itself. The decision first 
states that he met the requisite minimum number ofthree evidentiary requirements but later indicates that 
he did not. 
The record shows that the Petitioner claimed to meet the following five evidentiary requirements: 
• receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards; 
• memberships in associations in the field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• participation as the judge of others' work in the field; 
• original contributions ofmajor significance in the field; and 
• authorship of scholarly articles.

1. Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Awards 
This criterion requires "[ d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i). 
When adjudicating this requirement, USCIS first determines ifa petitioner- as opposed to their employer, 
for example - received prizes or awards. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 1. 
The Agency next determines if the awards honored excellence in the applicable field and have national 
or international recognition. Id. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he won: 
• a~------~'in 2017 from an~---------------~ 
• a 2013-2015 scholarship from a South Korean government program; and 
3 
• a 2014 creative research contest for graduate students. 
SCOPS found insufficient evidence of the awards' national or international recognition. SCOPS stated: 
The evidence simply does not show that [the awards are] recognized in the field on a 
broader level, beyond the organization which issued them. For instance, the evidence 
does not show [that] the award recipients receive a level of media coverage that is 
commensurate with a nationally or internationally recognized prize or award for 
excellence in the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that his young scientist award is an "internationally recognized" award 
for excellence in his field. He states that, at the time he received the award, it was issued every other year 
to a researcher under age 34 who made "outstanding contributions and demonstrated exceptional promise 
in catalysis engineering with a particular focus on acid and/or base catalysis." 
A letter from a researcher who served as a presenter at the conference where the Petitioner received the 
young scientist award supports his contentions. But, as SCOPS found, the record does not demonstrate 
the award' s national or international recognition. The Petitioner argues that, consistent with USCIS 
policy, the award's grantor is a "well-known professional association." See 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 1 ("Examples of qualifying awards may include, but are not limited to: ... Certain 
awards from well-known national institutions or well-known professional associations.") The record, 
however, does not establish the award's grantor as well-known. The statements of the Petitioner and the 
presenter are insufficient to show national or international recognition. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 
3d 126, 136 (D.D.C. 2013) ("Without evidence ofhow a larger audience viewed Plaintiff's awards, there 
was no way for the agency to evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy 
this criterion.") 
The Petitioner contends that his other honors are "nationally recognized" because "each of the awards is 
open to members of the academic institution [that issued them]." Counsel asserts that the research 
university that issued his 2014 contest award is a "household name" in South Korea that is frequently 
rated among the top 100 schools in the world. The record, however, does not corroborate counsel's 
assertions. See INS v. Phinpathya, 464 U.S. 183, 188 n.6 (1984) ("Counsel's unsupported assertions ... 
do not establish that the respondent could satisfy the 'continuous physical presence' requirement.") Also, 
even if the university that issued the award has national recognition, it does not necessarily follow that 
that particular prize has national recognition. See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 
2021) ("[A]s reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not the awarding 
organization.") ( emphasis in original). 
Contrary to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), the Petitioner has not documented that his awards received national 
or international recognition. We will therefore affirm SCOPS's decision regarding this evidentiary 
requirement. 
2. Memberships in Associations Requiring Outstanding Achievements 
To meet this requirement, a petitioner must submit "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
4 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of his membership in several engineering, chemical, and energy 
associations and information about the organizations. But SCOPS found insufficient evidence that the 
associations "require outstanding achievements or use recognized national or international experts to 
determine which individuals qualify for membership." 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that evidence of his membership in one association, al.________. 
meets this criterion. He states that, unlike other engineering organizations, the society does not have a 
"fellow" membership level requiring exceptional contributions in the field. But, because the co­
chairperson of a society committee selected him to review members' research papers, he states that his 
membership "should not be treated as a general membership that requires only good standing in the 
occupation (such as Iicensure) and membership dues, but a higher level ofmembership within [the] highly 
prestigious organization." 
Under USCIS policy: 
[t]he level of membership afforded to the person must show that in order to obtain that 
level of membership, recognized national or international experts judged the person as 
having attained outstanding achievements in the field for which classification is sought. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 2. 
The Petitioner indicates that the catalysis society does not have a membership level requiring outstanding 
achievements of its members as judged by experts. He submitted society information stating that a 
member can be "[a]ny person from North America having interests in supporting, promoting or 
encouraging the growth and development of thel I" Thus, his evidence does not meet 
this criterion because, even assuming that his review of members' research papers constituted an 
outstanding achievement, he has not demonstrated that his membership required that or another 
outstanding achievement. See Braga v. Poulos, No. CV 06-5105 SJO (FMOx), 2007 WL 9229758, at *5 
(C.D. Cal. July 6, 2007), ajf'd, 317 Fed. Appx. 680 (9th Cir. 2009) ("[T]he organization itself must require 
outstanding achievement as a condition of membership.") Also, he has not established that recognized 
national or international experts judged his membership in the society. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated that the associations to which he belongs required outstanding 
achievements of their members as judged by recognized national or international experts. We will 
therefore affirm SCOPS finding regarding this criterion. 
3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
This criterion requires"[ e ]vidence ofthe alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business­
related contributions ofmajor significance in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v).

USCIS first determines whether a petitioner made original contributions in their field. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. The Agency next determines whether the original 
contributions are of major significance to the field. Id. 
To meet this criterion, the Petitioner submitted a variety of evidence, including: letters of 
recommendation and support; records of his publications and citations; and information regarding his 
patents and technology transfers. SCOPS found: "Although the evidence shows that the petitioner has 
engaged in original research studies and produced original results, the evidence fails to show that this 
research and its findings constitute contributions ofmajor significance in the field." 
As the Petitioner contends, however, SCOPS overlooked evidence. The record shows that, from 2020 to 
2023, the Petitioner served as a senior researcher on a project to produce synthetic gasoline for cars using 
carbon dioxide as a raw material. His work led to two U.S. patents and three published research papers.

Evidence of the consortium's plan to commercialize the Petitioner's low-carbon hydrogen method 
persuades us that his contribution had major significance in his field. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual

significant attention or commercialization may establish the significance of the person's original 
contribution to the field.") 
The Petitioner submitted evidence ofhis original contributions ofmajor significance to his field. We will 
therefore withdraw SCOPS' contrary finding. 
D. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner met the requisite minimum number of evidentiary requirements. USCIS must therefore 
conduct a final merits determination in this matter. SCOPS did not make such a finding. Rather than 
make one in the first instance, we will remand the matter. 
On remand, SCOPS must determine whether the Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim 
and recognition of his achievements sufficient to identify him as one of that small percentage who has 
risen to his field's very top. See general~v 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). SCOPS should consider 
any potentially relevant evidence ofrecord, even if it does not fit one ofthe regulatory criteria or was not 
presented as comparable evidence. Id. The Petitioner's burden ofproof includes the burden ofexplaining 
the significance of his submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that he has achieved sustained 
national or international acclaim and recognition in his field. Id. The petition's approval or denial should 
hinge on the evidence's type and quality. Id.
