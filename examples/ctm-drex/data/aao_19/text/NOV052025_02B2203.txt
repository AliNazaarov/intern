Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a karate coach, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-
1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. 
permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) 
of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not meet 
any of the category's ten evidentiary requirements - three less than needed for a final merits 
determination. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). On appeal, we found that he met two of the evidentiary 
requirements but affirmed the petition's denial. See In Re: 37736000 (AAO Apr. 24, 2025). About 
three months later, we reopened the matter on our own motion, and allowed the Petitioner to submit 
additional evidence. See 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(5). In response to the reopening, he contends that we 
"erred in not considering the substantial evidence in the record to qualify him for at least one other 
criterion." 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we again conclude that he has not met a requisite third evidentiary requirement. We further 
find that, even ifhe satisfied the required number of evidentiary criteria, he would not have established 
himself among the small percentage at his field's very top in a final merits analysis. We will therefore 
dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(implementing a two-step extraordinary ability analysis); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

5. Display of the Petitioner's Work at Artistic Exhibits or Showcases 
This criterion requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in the field at artistic exhibitions 
or showcases." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii). 
The Petitioner contends that proof of his coaching at national and international karate competitions 
serves as "comparable evidence" to meet this criterion. He states that, at these events, his student 
athletes use the athletic skills he has taught them and, by winning championships or medals, display 
those skills to the public. 
As previously indicated, a petitioner may submit comparable evidence to meet an evidentiary criterion 
if the requirement does not "readily apply" to their occupation. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(4). "When 
evaluating such comparable evidence, officers must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily 
applicable to the person's occupation and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable 
to the criteria listed in that regulation." 6 USCJS Policy Manual F .2(B)( l ), "Comparable evidence." 
In agreeing with the Petitioner that this criterion does not "readily apply" to his karate coaching field, 
our prior decision interpreted the requirement's phrase "artistic exhibitions and showcases" to limit 
qualifying presentations to visual - as opposed to dance or performance - arts. On reconsideration, 
we find that we erred in interpreting this criterion to apply only to visual arts. The phrase "artistic 
exhibitions and showcases" does not expressly limit the exhibitions and showcases to visual art. 
10 
Rather, we find that a better interpretation of the phrase's term "artistic" more broadly refers to any 
variety of art. See Loper Bright Enters. v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. 369, 400 (2024) ("In the business of 
statutory interpretation, if it is not the best, it is not permissible.") We will therefore withdraw our 
prior interpretation. 
Nevertheless, we continue to agree with the Petitioner that karate competitions fall outside the scope 
of "artistic exhibitions and showcases." In this immigrant visa category, the plain language of the Act 
indicates that Congress considered arts and athletics to be separate fields. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) 
of the Act (requiring an alien to have extraordinary ability "in the sciences, arts, education, business, 
or athletics") (emphasis added). Thus, this criterion does not readily apply to the Petitioner's karate 
coaching field in athletics. See also 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l ), "Criterion 7" ("USCIS only 
considers non-artistic exhibitions or showcases as part of a properly supported claim of comparable 
evidence [ for this requirement]. ")4 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that his karate coaching evidence closely compares to 
the criteria listed in the regulation to meet the comparable evidence standard. As our prior decision 
found, this evidentiary requirement focuses on "the display of the alien's work." The Petitioner's 
evidence does not establish that karate competitions "display" his coaching work. 
The Petitioner disagrees, stating that his: 
unique combination of karate styles is what [his] students display to the public and the 
audience. It is [his] combination of techniques and skills taught to his students which 
are displayed by his students. Hence, without [him], his athletes could not perform in 
the way that the audience could visualize the actual and final performances of the 
athletes at the competitions. 
The Petitioner notes that the ordinary meaning of the word "exhibition" can refer to a public showing 
of "athletic skill." See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), Criterion 7 (quoting exhibition, Merriam­
Webster Online Dictionary, www.merriam-webster.com). He states that the evidence shows that his 
students "are utilizing his 'athletic skills' and showing those skills to the public at large in winning 
their championships." 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that his karate coaching evidence closely compares to 
a display of work at artistic exhibitions or showcases. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
Comparable evidence (requiring officers to consider . . . "whether the evidence provided is truly 
comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation"). 
We agree with the petitioner that the evidentiary criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) does not 
readily apply to karate coaching. But he has not demonstrated that his coaching evidence closely 
compares to the regulation's requirements. We will therefore affirm our finding that the Petitioner has 
not met this evidentiary requirement.

7. Commercial Successes in the Performing Arts 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of commercial success in the performing arts, as shown by box 
office receipts or record, cassette, compact disk, or video sales." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x). 
The Petitioner continues to argue that evidence of his participation in national and international karate 
competitions as a national coach qualifies as "comparable evidence" under this criterion. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). He states that, in appreciation of his coaching, some of his students gave him "bonuses 
and extra remuneration." 
We agree with the Petitioner that karate coaches generally do not generate sales or receipts in the 
performing arts. But he has not demonstrated that his coaching at national and international karate 
competitions closely compares to commercial successes in the performing arts. The record does not 
indicate how much the Petitioner received in additional compensation or whether other karate coaches 
receive such compensation. Without such evidence, we cannot determine whether the Petitioner's 
additional compensation reflects "commercial success" as he asserts. Also, the Petitioner's additional 
compensation is part of his remuneration for his karate coaching services and is therefore best

C. Final Merits Determination 
Our finding that the Petitioner has not met at least three evidentiary requirements resolves this matter. 
Like SCOPS before us, we need not make a final merits determination. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 
U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on 
issues unnecessary to their ultimate decisions). Nevertheless, even if the Petitioner met at least three 
evidentiary requirements, he would not demonstrate his claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
To merit final approval in this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate - through 
evidence of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition of their achievements in their 
field- that they are one of the small percentage who has risen to the field's very top. 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(2). They "must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it 
demonstrates that the person has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition 
in their field of expertise." We review all evidence in the totality and must consider "any potentially 
relevant evidence in the record, even if such evidence does not fit one of the ... regulatory criteria or 
was not presented as comparable evidence." Id. 
The Petitioner has not demonstrated or documented sufficient acclaim or recogrnt10n for his 
achievements to place him among the small percentage at his field's very top. We recognize that his 
lengthy tenure as coach of his country's national karate team, coaching of successful national team 
members, his federation's production of many national team members, his judging of international 
karate competitions, his students' international successes, and his contributions to the Russian city 
have generated acclaim. But the Petitioner's evidence heavily relies on letters from.__________.
I !officials and his former students. The record lacks sufficient evidence to provide context 
for evaluating his place in the broader karate coaching field. For example, the Petitioner has not shown 
that having two published articles that reference him in passing are indicative of a person who is one 
of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of their field of endeavor. 
These evidentiary deficiencies prevent the Petitioner from establishing his claimed place among the 
small percentage at his field's very top. He contends that he submitted sufficient evidence to qualify 
for the requested immigrant visa category. But the letters and other evidence ofrecord do not compare 
him to and place him among the top coaches in the field. Congress required aliens with extraordinary 
ability to demonstrate recognition of their achievements "through extensive documentation." Section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act.

Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens ofextraordinary ability by requiring "sustained national 
or international acclaim" and "extensive documentation" to show recognition of their achievements in 
their field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act. "The regulations regarding this preference classification 
are extremely restrictive." Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1120 ( citation omitted). Even athletes performing 
at the "major league" level do not automatically meet the extraordinary ability standard. Matter of 
Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). 
Upon review of the entire record, the Petitioner has demonstrated that he is a talented and respected 
karate coach. But there is insufficient evidence of sustained acclaim and recognition placing him 
among the top coaches in his field. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386 (5th Cir. 2022) ("[G]iven 
the lofty bar for extraordinary ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily 
when it determined that Amin was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good.") 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not met the required number of evidentiary criteria. Even if he has, he has not 
demonstrated enough acclaim or recognition to establish extraordinary ability in the karate coaching 
field.
