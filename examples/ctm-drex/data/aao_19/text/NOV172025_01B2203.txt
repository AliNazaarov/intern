Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a sled dog racer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability under 
section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A), 
which makes immigrant visas available to those who demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their 
field through extensive documentation. Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, 
concluding that the Petitioner did not establish her eligibility for this highly restrictive fust preference 
immigrant visa classification. We dismissed her appeal and a subsequent motion to reconsider. This 
matter is now before us on a second motion to reconsider. Upon review, we will dismiss the motion. 
A motion to reconsider must show that our prior decision was based on an incorrect application of law 
or policy and that the decision was incorrect based on the evidence in the record ofproceedings at the 
time of the decision. 8 C.F.R. § 103 .5(a)(3). Because the scope of a motion is limited to the prior 
decision, we will only review the latest decision in these proceedings. 8 C.F.R. § 103.5(a)(l)(i), (ii). 
We may grant motions that meet these requirements and establish eligibility for the requested benefit. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of establishing her eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
The Petitioner asserted below she has a one-time achievement based on a major, international 
recognized award as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), and alternatively claimed she also met at least 
three of the ten criteria to establish extraordinary ability, specifically at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) 
(receipt of a lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards for excellence in her field); (ii) 
(membership in associations in the field requiring members' outstanding achievements, as judged by 
recognized experts); and (viii) (leading or critical role for distinguished organizations). SCOPS found 
that the record did not establish a one-time achievement as contemplated by 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), 
necessitating that the Petitioner satisfy all three regulatory criteria that she also claimed to have met 
in order to establish eligibility. However, SCOPS concluded that the evidence satisfied only two 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) and (viii), and thus denied the petition. 
On appeal, we agreed with SCOPS ' conclusion that the Petitioner did not satisfy 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
and therefore did not establish that she is an alien of extraordinary ability, and we dismissed the appeal 
without making a final merits determination under the Act. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1119 (9th Cir. 2010); Nagaiah v. Allen, F.Supp.3d, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) 
(finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner 
who seeks an extraordinary ability visa.").

8 C.F.R. § 103.S(a)(l)(i), (ii). The assertions in her instant motion to reconsider reiterate the same 
facts, issues, and arguments we have already considered in our previous decision. She broadly claims 
our decision dismissing her prior motion to reconsider "rests on a misapplication oflaw and regulatory 
standards governing extraordinary ability in specialized athletic disciplines." But in support of this 
assertion, she repeats her prior claim that we erroneously considered general "public familiarity" or 
recognition of the D awards, rather than considering only the esoteric level of recognition specific 
to the D races and awards as evaluated by the field experts. We have already considered this 
argument in our prior decisions and noted that she did not provide any authority for her assertion that 
we erred in considering an award' s level ofgeneral public recognition. She also does not explain how 
we misapplied law or p~licy Tfinding that she did not establish that awards she received were 
recognized beyond that o the awarding entity to the extent that they were major, internationally 
recognized awards as contemplated by 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Although the Petitioner also claims 
that we "appear[] to have erroneously interpreted" her 17th ranking in one ofherOraces, she does 
not specify how we erred or explain why her own document lists her as in the 17th ranking. Even if 
she had placed first in that particular race, as we explained in our prior motion decision, the record 
still does not show it qualifies as a one-time achievement (that is, major, international recognized 
award) to warrant reconsideration of our decision. 
The Petitioner also again claims that we misapplied Kazarian by "improperly collaps[ing]" our review 
of the record as it relates to 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)'s regulatory requirement and final merits analysis. 
But as noted above and in our decision dismissing her first motion to reconsider, we were not required 
to and in fact did not reach the merits of this case under the Kazarian framework. She also mentions 
several non-precedent decisions involving canine trainers but does not explain how they relate or apply 
to her case or show error in law or policy in our decision dismissing her prior motion. The Petitioner 
has not demonstrated that we erred in applying law or policy in our last decision or that the decision 
was incorrect based on the evidence then before us when we dismissed her prior motion. Accordingly, 
she has not established that reconsideration is warranted, and the following order will be entered. 
ORDER: The motion to reconsider is dismissed.
