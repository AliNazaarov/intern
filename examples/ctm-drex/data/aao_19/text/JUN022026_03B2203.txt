Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a healthcare consultant and platform developer, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(I )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the petition. The Acting Associate 
Director of Service Center Operations concluded that, although the Petitioner satisfied the requested 
category's initial evidentiary requirements, he did not demonstrate acclaim and recognition sufficient 
to establish extraordinary ability in his field. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the Director 
misunderstood, overlooked, and minimized the evidence's significance. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with the Director that the Petitioner has not established his claimed place among the 
small percentage at his field's top. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for the requested immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability" in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country as a permanent resident. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i-iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
Unless a petitioner received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three 
of ten initial evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 1 If a petitioner meets either evidentiary 
standard and the requirements at sections 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, USCIS must then make a 
final merits determination. To merit final approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a 
petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them 
among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th 
Cir. 2022) (holding that USCIS' two-step analysis "is consistent with the governing statute and 
regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
TI. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner earned a bachelor's degree in accounting sciences and a master 
of business administration degree in information technology management from universities in his 
home country of Brazil. He has more than l 0 years of consulting experience and worked for other 
consulting companies before founding his own consulting firm. He specializes in healthcare and 
finance, and stated that he has led successful hospital restructurings, supported national accreditation 
processes, and redesigned clinical workflows to improve healthcare performance and patient 
outcomes. 
The Petitioner seeks to continue his healthcare consulting work in the United States. He stated that, 
using his expertise in strategic management, financial sustainability, and digital innovation, he would 
improve systemic issues hindering U.S. healthcare delivery, such as fragmented workflows, escalating 
operational costs, and outdated systems that delay or disrupt patient care. He stated: "My goal is to 
implement robust management frameworks and integrated care models that not only elevate 
institutional efficiency but also enhance the quality of care across delivery networks." 
B. The Evidentiary Criteria 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He must therefore meet at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports the Director's findings that the Petitioner successfully submitted evidence of his: 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in his or an allied field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in his field; and 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations. 
See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi), (viii). 
1 If an evidentiary criterion does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, they may submit "comparable evidence" 
to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
2 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that he met three additional evidentiary requirements. But he already 
qualifies for a final merits determination based on his satisfaction of three evidentiary criteria. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). We therefore need not determine whether he meets any others. See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 ( 1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies need not make "purely

We will next review the Director's final merits determination. 
C. The Final Merits Determination 
To establish final eligibility for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they 
have sustained national or international acclaim and that their achievements have been recognized in 
their field sufficiently to place them among that small percentage who has risen to the field's top. See 
generalZv 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). Congress intended classification as an alien with 
extraordinary ability to reflect a "career of acclaimed work in the field." See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 
59 (Sept. 19, 1990). 
Under USCIS policy: 
[T]he petitioner must provide sufficient context ... to demonstrate that the evidence 
meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary ability in the 
totality of the circumstances. This means that the petitioner must explain the 
significance of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that the person has 
achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in their field of 
expertise. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). We must consider any potentially relevant evidence of record, 
even if it does not fit one of the regulatory criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence. Id. 
1. Awards 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that a cloud-based navigation platform that he developed for cancer 
patients received four awards. The record shows that, in 2022, the platform received an innovation 
award from a diagnostic imaging and laboratory company in the "patient digital journey" category and 
a "case report" award from a Brazilian cancer hospital. The platform was also one of 50 initiatives 
that a Brazilian cancer center recognized for advancing cancer treatment technologies. Further, in 
2023, the platform was one of five finalists in a startup acceleration program of the Brazilian 
development bank. 
On appeal, the Petitioner states that "[t]he significance ofthese recognitions is reinforced by the stature 
of the institutions involved and the competitive processes through which the awards were granted." 
For example, he states that the case report award was issued by the largest cancer-focused medical 
institution in Latin America. 
By their very natures, the awards constitute recognition of the Petitioner's platform. But the awards 
do not bear his name, and the record does not indicate that they garnered him national or international 
3 
acclaim. See Donskoy v. USCIS, No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 
11, 2021) ("[W]hat matters for the purpose of the visa classification is whether the award brought [ an 
alien] individual acclaim.") Thus, on their own, the platform's awards do not demonstrate the 
Petitioner's claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
2. Membership in Associations 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he is an "ambassador" in a Brazilian association for healthcare 
startup companies. He also documented his membership in a global healthcare information and 
management systems association and a global network of independent consultants. The record shows 
that his ambassadorship required a nomination by that association's directors, counselors, or 
ambassadors. 
The Petitioner's memberships reflect his professional standing in his field. But he has not 
demonstrated that they brought him national or international acclaim or recognition in his field. Thus, 
the memberships, alone, do not establish his claimed place among the small percentage at his field's 
very top. See Hamal v. United States Dep 't o_fHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *3 
(D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023) ("[W]hile demonstrating his professional stature," a petitioner's evidence of 
association memberships "did not establish the exceptional level of distinction required for this 
extraordinary-ability visa.") 
Also, the record shows that the Petitioner joined all three associations in 2025. Thus, any acclaim 
derived from the memberships would not be "sustained" over his career. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) 
of the Act (requiring "sustained national or international acclaim"). 
3. Publications About the Petitioner 
The Petitioner provided evidence that, between 2021 and 2025, various Brazilian media reported on 
his work. For example, ac=]2021 article in one of Brazil's largest newspapers focused on a "digital 
clinic" platform the Petitioner developed to support cancer patients. The article discussed his platform 
as an example of an increasing number of digital healthcare companies started in the wake of the 
COVID-19 pandemic. On appeal, the Petitioner states that media coverage of his contributions to the 
healthcare field "shows that independent media outlets viewed [his] work as noteworthy within the 
healthcare sector." 
The publications about the Petitioner brought him some national acclaim and recognition in his field. 
But the record lacks evidence of acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small 
percentage at his field's top. See Hamal v. US. Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. CV 19-2534 (RC), 2021 
WL 2338316, at *4 (D.D.C. June 8, 2021), aff'd sub nom. Hamal v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland 
Sec., supra (upholding our findings that a petitioner did not show that "his press coverage is indicative 
of a level of success consistent with being" at the top of his field or "that [he] has significantly 
influenced the field").

4. Judge of Others' Work 
The Petitioner documented that, in 2025, he served as an evaluator and speaker on a case reports panel 
at a two-day "bootcamp" for healthcare startup companies. He also provided evidence that he 
developed a technical evaluation system for research submissions at a 2024 ophthalmological 
conference and that, as director of strategy and innovation at a pharmaceutical distributor in 2020, he 
designed the company's sales consultant trainee program and made final hiring decisions. 
The Petitioner argues that these evaluative roles demonstrate recognition of his expertise "across 
multiple segments of the healthcare ecosystem" and illustrate reliance on his professional judgment 
"in both healthcare innovation forums and medical research environments." He states that his 
"repeated selection" for assessment roles "reflects confidence in his expertise and authority within the 
healthcare innovation ecosystem." 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that his assessment roles garnered him sufficient 
amounts of national or international acclaim or recognition in his field to place him among the small 
percentage at the field's top. He provided recommendation letters from an ophthalmologist who 
served on the conference's scientific committee and the former human resources director at the 
pharmaceutical distributor. But the record does not establish that his judging of others' work in his 
field resulted in acclaim or recognition beyond the events or the company where his judging occurred. 
See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (finding 
insufficient evidence that a petitioner's peer review experience places him among the small percentage 
at his field's very top); see also Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, 
at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) ("It is generally expected that an individual whose accomplishments 
have garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have received recognition for his or 
her accomplishments well beyond the circle of his personal and professional acquaintances;" 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). 
5. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of three original contributions he made to his field. First, as 
previously mentioned, the record shows that he developed the navigation platform for cancer patients, 
integrating digital technology with clinical management to reduce systemic inefficiencies and improve 
patient outcomes. The Petitioner states that, by April 2025, more than 5,770 cancer patients had used 
the platform, which he conceived in 2021. Initial results reported reduced emergency room visits, 
decreased lengths of hospital stays, and reductions in "no shows" and rescheduled appointments at 
clinics. 
In 2023, the Petitioner also launched a platform to manage medical referrals across all specialties and 
insurance networks. He states that more than 400 Brazilian doctors use the platform, which he 
designed to combat delays in specialist referrals. 
5 
Finally, the Petitioner developed the digital tele-oncology clinic. Medical professionals have 
purportedly used the application to conduct more than 16,000 consultations with cancer patients.2 
On appeal, the Petitioner states: 
These initiatives were not theoretical proposals but implemented healthcare solutions 
that produced measurable improvements in patient access, clinical coordination, and 
healthcare delivery efficiency. Such real-world impact demonstrates that [his] work 
has influenced how healthcare services are structured and delivered, a form of 
professional impact that the [Director's] final merits determination does not address. 
We recognize that the Petitioner's contributions have garnered him some acclaim and recognition in 
his field. As previously indicated, his navigation platform for cancer patients received awards, and 
multiple media reported on the launch of his tele-oncology clinic business. But the Petitioner's 
evidence does not contextualize where these contributions place him within his field. See 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) ("In all cases, the petitioner must provide sufficient context ... to 
demonstrate that the evidence meets the relevant criteria and to establish the person's extraordinary 
ability in the totality of the circumstances.") The record lacks independent evidence of his status in 
the field from others unassociated with him. See Mishra, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 ("It is generally 
expected that one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international acclaim 
would have received recognition for their accomplishments well beyond the circle of their personal 
and professional acquaintances."); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). 
6. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The Petitioner provided evidence that he wrote two articles that were published in a magazine about 
business, technology, and management in the Brazilian medical and hospital sectors. His 2021 article 
discussed potential effects of a sharp decline in cancer screenings and consultations during the 
COVID-19 pandemic. His 2025 article focused on the healthcare implications of rising interest rates. 
The Director found insufficient evidence that others in the Petitioner's field cited his articles, 
suggesting that the articles lack influence. 
The Petitioner contends that the Director improperly discounted his authorship of the articles by 
applying "an academic citation-based framework that does not fit [his] field of endeavor." He states 
that, in the healthcare innovation field, "professional influence is often demonstrated through invited 
analytical publications directed to industry leadership rather than academic citation metrics." He states 
that his authorship's significance "lies not in academic citation counts, but in the fact that his analysis 
was solicited by and disseminated through a major sector-specific publication read by the very 
professionals who shape healthcare operations, financing, and innovation policy." 
The Petitioner has demonstrated dissemination of his articles in a professional publication in his field. 
But the record lacks supporting evidence that the magazine solicited the articles from him as he claims.

7. Display of the Petitioner's Work 
For this evidentiary category, which requires evidence of the display of an alien's work at artistic 
exhibits or showcases, the Petitioner submitted comparable evidence. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) ("If 
the above standards do not readily apply to the [petitioner]'s occupation, the[y] ... may submit 
comparable evidence to establish the[ir] ... eligibility.") He provided evidence that, between 2021 
and 2025, he delivered 10 speeches on health topics at various conferences. The record shows that, at 
some conferences, he discussed platforms he developed. At others, he addressed more general topics, 
such as return on investment in healthcare innovation, innovations in real-time health monitoring and 
digital simulation technologies, and opportunities and challenges in scaling health technology startups. 
The record, however, does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's speeches garnered him national or 
international acclaim or recognition in his field. The speeches, alone, therefore do not establish his 
claimed extraordinary ability in his field. 
8. Performance in a Leading or Critical Role 
The Petitioner submitted evidence that he has served organizations in both critical and leading roles. 
The record shows that, while working as a consultant for a major global consulting firm, he led efforts 
to restructure and eventually sell a hospital, resulting in the largest healthcare acquisition in 
northeastern Brazil. Also as a consultant, he guided another Brazilian hospital in achieving full 
accreditation in less than five months, a process that normally takes a year or more. Further, the 
Petitioner submitted evidence that he: founded his consulting firm; launched the tele-oncology clinic; 
and helped establish a medical billing company. 
The Petitioner contends that, in the final merits determination, the Director "minimized" the 
significance of the critical roles he has played for healthcare organizations. He states that the record 
"shows that multiple distinguished institutions repeatedly relied on [him] to lead or execute high­
stakes initiatives central to institutional performance, valuation, accreditation, innovation, and 
growth." He states that "the repeated reliance of globally and nationally distinguished institutions on 
[him] to lead transformative initiatives is itself powerful evidence that his expertise is recognized as 
exceptional." 
We agree that the Petitioner has demonstrated that, over his career, he has performed in critical roles 
for various organizations. Despite this evidence, however, the record lacks independent proof that the 
Petitioner's roles garnered national or international acclaim, or recognition in his field beyond his 
former clients and co-workers. See Mishra, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 ("It is generally expected that 
one whose accomplishments have garnered sustained national or international acclaim would have

9. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration 
As evidence of the Petitioner's income, he submitted a letter from an accountant. The letter states 
that: 
• as director of strategy and innovation for the pharmaceutical distributor, he received a salary 
of 420,000 Brazilian reals (BRL) in 20203; 
• as a consultant at his own consulting firm, he earned "income in fees" of 357,440.69 BRL in 
2024 and 397,470.20 BRL in 2025; and 
• as partner/chief executive of the medical billing company, he received the "economic value" 
of 25,440 BRL in fees and from 74,592 BRL to 124,098 BRL in "equity participation" in 2024 
and 76,320 BRL in fees and from 223,776.40 BRL to 372,294 BRL in equity participation in 
2025. 
The Petitioner also submitted a letter from a second accountant stating that his consulting firm 
generated total revenues of 352,440.69 BRL in 2024. 
The first accountant's statements regarding the Petitioner's income amounts are unreliable. But, if his 
consulting firm grossed only 352,440.69 BRL in 2024 as the second accountant states, he has not 
explained how he could have earned 357,440.69 BRL in fees at the company during the same year? 
A petitioner must resolve inconsistencies of record with independent, objective evidence pointing to 
where the truth lies. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N Dec. 582, 591 (BIA 1988). Thus, without corroborating 
evidence of the Petitioner's income amounts, we do not credit the income information in the first 
accountant's letter. The evidence in this category therefore does not reflect his receipt of acclaim or 
recognition in his field. 
10. Commercial Successes 
The Petitioner submitted comparable evidence in an attempt to meet this evidentiary criterion, which 
focuses on commercial successes in the performing arts. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x), (4). He points 
to evidence that his healthcare consulting work has reduced healthcare expenses and has led to 
increases in the number of patients and revenues at clinics. 
The Petitioner, however, has not demonstrated that the financial results of his work have generated 
acclaim and recognition sufficient to place him among the small percentage at his field's top.

In sum, the record identifies the Petitioner as a talented, accomplished healthcare consultant who has 
made original contributions to his field. He met the requested category's initial evidentiary 
requirements and demonstrated his receipt of some national acclaim and recognition in the field. But, 
even considering the evidence in the aggregate, the Petitioner has not established himself among the 
small percentage at his field's very top. 
Congress intended to set "a very high standard" for aliens of extraordinary ability, reserving this 
immigrant visa category "for that small percentage of aliens who have risen to the very top of their 
field of endeavor." Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991 ). Considering the evidence as a whole, the Petitioner has not established his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[G]iven the lofty bar for extraordinary 
ability classifications, we cannot say that the agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [a 
petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely very good."); Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (holding that even athletes performing in their sports' premier leagues do not 
automatically meet the extraordinary ability standards). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner met the minimum amount of evidentiary criteria but did not demonstrate his claimed 
extraordinary ability in his field. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
