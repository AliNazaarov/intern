Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a journalist, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) 
immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act 
(the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-I classification makes immigrant 
visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. Section 203(b )( I )(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner 
meets at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' 
two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability as a journalist. The Petitioner 
states that she earned a bachelor's degree in social communication with a specialization in journalism 
(2007) and a postgraduate degree in digital marketing, innovation and strategy (2021 ), in Brazil. 1 She 
states that she has 19 years of experience in broadcast, print and digital journalism, and is "one of 
Brazil's most respected and decorated journalists." The Petitioner has worked as a reporter, anchor 
and editor for major television networks in Brazil, and has reported on cases of national significance 
in her home country and the United States.

4. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." 
Analysis under this criterion focuses on whether the person's original work constitutes major, 
significant contributions to the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
Evidence that the person's work was funded, patented, or published, while potentially demonstrating 
the work's originality, will not necessarily establish, on its own, that the work is of major significance 
to the field. Id. For example, published research that has provoked widespread commentary on its 
importance from others working in the field, and documentation that it has been highly cited relative 
6 
to others' work in the field, may be probative of the significance of the person's contributions to the 
field of endeavor. Id. 
The Petitioner claims to meet this criterion based on an original methodology ofcommunity-embedded 
investigative journalism she developed "throughout her career in Brazil's highest-level media 
institutions." SCOPS stated in the RFE that the Petitioner did not submit documentary evidence to 
support her claim that her contribution is original or of significance to the field. 
In response to the RFE, the Petitioner submitted evidence in the form of Wikipedia entries about the 
media institutions with which she has worked. The Petitioner also referenced expert opinion and 
support letters in the record that address "the originality and significance of [ the Petitioner's] 
methodology." 
SCOPS again declined to consider the information from Wikipedia. SCOPS also determined that the 
expert opinion letters, while supportive of the petition, did not provide a detailed explanation of how 
the Petitioner's contributions relate to the field ofjournalism, including whether her contributions are 
original and of major significance to the field as a whole. 
On appeal, the Petitioner references evidence already in the record and disagrees with SCOPS' 
conclusion. She states that her original approach is "reporting from within affected communities rather 
than from institutional sources, building trust with populations that traditionally distrust media, and 
translating complex governmental and legal failures into accessible public information that drives

5. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly 
articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." SCOPS 
concluded that the Petitioner did not provide evidence regarding this criterion. On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that SCOPS "ignores the employment records, expert letters, and published materials 
in the record that collectively establish [the Petitioner's] authorship of journalistic works in major 
media." 
A scholarly article should be written for "learned" persons in the field. "Learned" is defined as having 
or demonstrating profound knowledge or scholarship. Learned persons include all persons having 
profound knowledge of a field. 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(1 ). Here, the Petitioner has 
not established that her journalistic works in major media were for "learned" persons, rather than for 
the general population. Moreover, she has not shown that her works were peer reviewed by other 
experts in journalism or contain other characteristics ofscholarly articles. Id. (indicating that scholarly 
articles "should have footnotes, endnotes, or a bibliography"). The Petitioner therefore has not 
established that she satisfies this criterion.

6. Display at Artistic Exhibitions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) requires "[e]vidence of the display of the alien's work in 
the field at artistic exhibitions or showcases." To meet this criterion, the Petitioner must first show 
that the work that was displayed is her work product. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l). Second, the venues, whether they are virtual or otherwise, where the Petitioner's work 
was displayed must be artistic exhibitions or showcases. Id. The USCIS Policy Manual interprets 
"exhibition" as a public showing as of works of art, objects of manufacture, or athletic skill. Id. The 
plain language of the criterion includes the modifier "artistic" and explicitly requires that the 
exhibitions or showcases be "artistic" in nature. Id. We only consider non-artistic exhibitions or 
showcases as part ofa properly supported claim ofcomparable evidence. Id. For instance, a scientific 
researcher who displays his work at a conference, symposium, workshop, or meeting would not meet 
the requirements of this criterion. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1122 (finding that self­
publishing a textbook, lecturing at a community college, and presenting at conferences were not 
displays at artistic exhibitions or showcases consistent with the relevant regulatory language). 
The Petitioner initially claimed that she met this criterion based on "her productions [that] have been 
broadcast nationally, showcased at public award ceremonies, and honored in legislative sessions." 
However, the Petitioner provided no additional evidence in response to SCOPS' RFE and SCOPS 
concluded that she did not establish that she met this criterion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner does not contest SCOPS' decision that she did not meet this criterion. Any 
ground of ineligibility that is not raised on appeal is waived. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 
336 n.5.

8. Commanded a High Salary or Other Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." To meet 
this criterion, the Petitioner must demonstrate that her salary or remuneration is high relative to the 
compensation paid to others in the field. To determine whether a person's compensation is high 
relative to others, USCIS will consider comparative evidence, such as geographical or position­
appropriate compensation surveys. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Broad 
descriptions that include multiple occupations may not provide an accurate comparison to others in 
the field. Id. 
The Petitioner did not initially claim that she satisfied this criterion. In response to the RFE, however, 
she submitted her 2019 individual income tax return and a printout from optisalary.com, titled 
"Journalist salary in Brazil," and showing average salary data for 2026. As SCOPS noted, the 2026 
salary data is not probative of the Petitioner's 2019 income, and provides information about salaries 
in Brazil generally, without a more specific geographic location. 
On appeal, the Petitioner references "comparative labor market data from.....___________, [to 
establish] the average annual compensation for journalists in the same region and profession." She 
states that SCOPS erred in ignoring this evidence. Although the Petitioner also referenced this 
evidence in her cover letter with the RFE response, it was not, in fact, included with that submission. 
Nor was it submitted on appeal. 
Part of this criterion requires comparing petitioners to others in the field, which demands evidence 
regarding individuals with comparable roles. Without evidence of comparable data for the role of a 
journalist in the same year and geographic location, we do not have adequate information to determine 
whether the Petitioner meets this criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l ). 
It is the Petitioner's burden to establish eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Matter of 
Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. The Petitioner has not provided sufficient evidence to establish 
this criterion. 
B. Summary 
Upon review of the record, we conclude that the Petitioner has not submitted the required initial 
evidence of either a one-time achievement or documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). Thus, we need not folly address the totality of the materials in 
a final merits determination. Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we have reviewed the 
record in the aggregate, and conclude that it does not support a finding that the Petitioner has 
established the level of expertise required for the classification sought. 
11 
Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of her accomplishments as a journalist is 
indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not shown that she qualifies for classification as an individual of extraordinary 
ability under section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated 
reasons. In visa petition proceedings, it is the petitioner's burden to establish eligibility for the 
immigration benefit sought. Section 291 of the Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1361; Matter ofSkirball Cultural Ctr., 
25 I&N Dec. 799, 806 (AAO 2012). Here, that burden has not been met. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
