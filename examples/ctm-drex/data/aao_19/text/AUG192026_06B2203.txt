Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a chief executive officer (CEO), seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( 1 )(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b )( 1 )(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received 
a major, internationally recognized award, nor did it demonstrate that he met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)- (x). The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth two options for satisfying this classification's initial evidence 
requirements. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must 
provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and 
scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
material if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). This two-step analysis is consistent with our holding that the "truth is to be 
determined not by the quantity of evidence alone but by its quality," as well as the principle that we 
examine "each piece of evidence for relevance, probative value, and credibility, both individually and 
within the context ofthe totality ofthe evidence, to determine whether the fact to be proven is probably 
true." Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. 
II. ANALYSIS 
In his initial filing, the Petitioner claimed to have extraordinary ability in the field of social 
entrepreneurship. He plans to establish a company in I IGeorgia specializing in medical 
equipment integration, healthcare technology consulting, and professional training services. Because 
the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must establish at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner initially claimed four criteria relating to published materials, original contributions, 
leading or critical role, and high salary. USCIS determined the Petitioner satisfied two of the criteria 
relating to published materials and leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner continues to assert 
he meets the evidentiary criteria relating to original contributions and high salary. For the reasons 
discussed below, the Petitioner does not demonstrate he meets at least three categories of evidence. 1 
A. Original Contributions 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that his or her contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, 
have remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major

significance. See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an 
original contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1). 
The Petitioner contends he meets this criterion because he developed a "first-of-its-kind, 
groundbreaking public-private partnership (PPP) model" that has transformed Nigeria's healthcare 
infrastructure. The record includes various letters of support and media articles about his hospital 
rehabilitation project, along with the Petitioner's proposal to the Nigerian president, documents about 
the hospital rehabilitation project from the Petitioner's company, reports from the Nigerian 
government, and written endorsements and commitment to the hospital rehabilitation project from the 
Nigerian government. 
USCIS first determines whether the person has made original contributions in the field. Id. Here, the 
evidence does not show that the Petitioner is the original creator of the PPP model. Instead, it appears 
that he used an existing project delivery and procurement model to rehabilitate the hospitals in Nigeria. 
For instance, the letter from the Former Minister ofHealth in Nigeria states that the Petitioner adopted 
the PPP model to finance and execute the healthcare projects in Nigeria. Without more, the Petitioner 
has not established how implementing a model that was already in use between governments and 
private entities is an original contribution. 
Accordingly, the Petitioner does not demonstrate he satisfies this criterion. 
B. High Salary 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay 
statements. Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and 
location should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in 
the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner submitted a letter from his company's finance director. The finance director states that 
the Petitioner "does not receive a regular salary from our company. Instead, he receives payments for 
professional services rendered in his capacity as CEO/Chairman o±i I" The finance director 
also states that in 2024, the Petitioner received a total of N64,600,000.00 for his services. The 
Petitioner provided payment slips from September 2024 through February 2025, which range from 
N9,500,00 to Nl2,000,000 a month. In addition, he submitted a printout from Payscale which shows 
that the average base salary for a CEO in Nigeria is N5,100,681 per year and that the 90th percentile 
wage is N48,000,000 a year in 2025, based on "65 salary profiles," but the data does not indicate it

was for the Petitioner's field of social entrepreneurship. Furthermore, the Petitioner highlights on 
appeal the Pros hare report that was submitted in response to USCIS' request for evidence, which lists

Notably, the list of the top 10 CEOs in Nigeria shows that they are from various industries, such as oil

Broad descriptions that include multiple occupations or multiple industries may not provide an 
accurate comparison to others in the field. Id. The regulatory requirement that the commanded salary 
be high "in relation to others in the field" is interpreted as allowing comparisons to those employed in 
the same industry and occupation at a similar level. See Matter o_fPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. 
Comm'r 1994) (considering a professional golfer's earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); Skokos 
v. US. Dept. o_fHomeland Sec., 420 F. App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information 
for those performing lesser duties is not a comparison to others in the field); Crimson v. INS, 934 F. 
Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) (considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); 
Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) (comparing salary of NHL defensive player 
to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
Because the Petitioner has not provided sufficient evidence demonstrating the salaries or remuneration 
of other CEOs in the social entrepreneurship field, he has not established that he has commanded a 
high salary or other significantly high remuneration for services in relation to others in the field. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, not 
sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this 
matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
