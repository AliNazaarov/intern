Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a video game developer, seeks to classify the Beneficiary, a senior animator, as an 
individual of extraordinary ability in the arts. Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) 
section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant 
visas available to aliens who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not satisfy 
the initial evidentiary requirements for this classification by showing the Beneficiary's receipt of a 
major, internationally recognized award or providing evidence that she meets at least three of the 
alternate evidentiary criteria set forth in the regulations. The matter is now before us on appeal 
pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien 
is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if: they have extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and their achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; they seek to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the country. Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a 
beneficiary's one-time achievement (that is, receipt of a major, internationally recognized award). If 
that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then they must provide documentation that the 
beneficiary meets at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) 
(including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). The 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable material if they can 
demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the beneficiary's 
occupation. 
Where a petitioner demonstrates that the beneficiary meets these initial evidence requirements, we 
then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether 
the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is 
among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 
1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal 
v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The record reflects that the Beneficiary, who has a bachelor's degree in fine arts, has accrued credits 
for her work as an animator in both the video game and motion picture industries. The Petitioner 
currently employs her as a senior animator for video game projects and has offered her continuing 
employment in this capacity. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established the Beneficiary's receipt of a major 
internationally recognized award, it must provide evidence satisfying at least three of the alternate 
regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner claimed that the Beneficiary could 
meet five of the ten criteria and requested consideration of comparable evidence under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(4). SCOPS concluded that the Beneficiary met one criterion, based on evidence that she 
has participated as a judge of the work of others in her field, as required under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 1 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that the SCOPS applied an incorrect evidentiary standard and 
overlooked evidence establishing that the Beneficiary has received lesser nationally or internationally 
recognized awards, that she has made contributions of major significance to her field, and that she has 
performed in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations. 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), (v), and (viii), respectively.2 The Petitioner farther asserts that SCOPS did not 
correctly evaluate the comparable evidence it provided under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
For the reasons discussed below, we conclude that the Petitioner has not demonstrated that the 
Beneficiary meets at least three of the claimed criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Accordingly, it has not demonstrated her eligibility for the requested immigrant classification.

3. Comparable Evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
If any of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to a person's occupation, a 
petitioner may instead provide comparable evidence to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
For such evidence to be considered, however, petitioners must establish two factors. First, they must 
establish why a specific criterion is not readily applicable to a beneficiary's occupation. Second, they 
must demonstrate that the evidence provided is truly comparable to the evidence required by that 
criterion. A general unsupported assertion that a listed evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to 
the beneficiary's occupation is not probative. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
In its initial filing, the Petitioner stated that various provided support letters should be considered as 
comparable evidence because they show "significant recognition for achievements from organizations 
and other recognized experts in the field." In its response to the SCOPS' request for evidence (RFE), 
the Petitioner elaborated that the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) is not readily applicable to the 
Beneficiary's occupation because it "is reserved for traditional artists showcasing their work in 
exhibitions," and the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) is also not readily applicable since it 
concerns commercial success in the performing arts. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not 
provide sufficient evidence to show that the criteria are not readily applicable to the Beneficiary's 
occupation. SCOPS also appeared to conclude that the provided evidence did not qualify under the 
plain language of these criteria, but did not specify what evidence was analyzed to come to this 
conclusion. 
On appeal, the Petitioner reasserts that the artistic exhibition and commercial success criteria do not 
readily apply to the Beneficiary's occupation. The plain language of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) requires 
evidence of commercial success in the performing arts. Because animators are not performing artists, 
we agree that this criterion is not readily applicable to the Petitioner's occupation and withdraw 
SCOPS' conclusion to the contrary. However, while the Petitioner asserts that only "traditional 
artists" qualify for the artistic exhibition criterion, nothing in the plain language of 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(vii) restricts its application to artists working in a particular medium. An animator's 
work could, for example, be displayed at an artistic exhibition such as a film festival or a gallery 
display about video game art. Counsel's unsupported assertions about the inapplicability of this 
criterion therefore do not suffice to establish that we should consider comparable evidence instead. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l) (stating that "[a] general unsupported 
assertion that the listed evidentiary criterion does not readily apply to the petitioner's occupation is 
not probative," and that "general claims that USCIS should accept witness letters as comparable 
evidence are not persuasive.") 
The Petitioner further claims that it does not need to establish that its evidence is comparable to that 
required by any particular criterion, quoting an agency draft memorandum regarding the 0-1 
nonimmigrant classification which states that "[t]he comparable evidence provision was intended as a 
'catch-all' to allow additional evidence to be considered when the other enumerated criteria do not 
readily apply, in whole or in part ...". See generally Draft USCIS Policy Memorandum PM-602-0123, 
Comparable Evidence for O Nonimmigrant Visa Classification 2-3 (Jan. 21, 2016),

As noted by the Petitioner, the comparable evidence provision for 0-1 nonimmigrants at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2( o )(3)(iii)(C) is similar to the one applicable to extraordinary ability immigrant visa petitions 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). However, the cited memorandum is a draft and clearly states that it "does 
not constitute agency policy in any way or for any purpose." See generally Draft USCIS Policy 
Memorandum PM-602-0123, supra at 1. 8 Furthermore, even if this document constituted binding 
policy guidance, which it does not, its text clearly supports SCOPS' interpretation of the comparable 
evidence provision, stating that "a petitioner must only show that a particular criterion does not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation before a petitioner may offer comparable evidence with regard 
to that criterion" ( emphasis added), and that petitioners must explain "why the submitted evidence is 
'comparable' to the criterion listed in the regulations." See generally Draft USCIS Policy 
Memorandum PM-602-0123, supra at 3. This is consistent with the current USCIS policy guidance 
regarding comparable evidence for the extraordinary ability immigrant classification. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l) (providing examples of criteria that are inapplicable to 
particular occupations and comparable evidence that may be submitted to show eligibility under those 
specific criteria). 
The Petitioner has not provided an adequate explanation as to why the artistic exhibition criterion is 
not readily applicable to the Beneficiary's occupation or how the recommendation letters are 
comparable to evidence showing commercial success in the performing arts. As such, the evidence 
does not establish the Beneficiary's qualifications under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vii) or (x). 
B. Summary and Reserved Issue 
The Petitioner must demonstrate that the Beneficiary meets at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.S(h)(i)-(x) to satisfy the initial evidence requirement for this classification. The record 
establishes that she meets the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), but the Petitioner has not 
demonstrated that the Beneficiary meets either of the two evidentiary criteria discussed above, nor has 
it submitted comparable evidence demonstrating her eligibility. Because the Petitioner cannot meet 
the initial evidentiary requirement, detailed discussion of the remaining claimed criterion at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(viii), relating to leading or critical roles in organizations or establishments with a 
distinguished reputation, cannot change the outcome of the appeal. Therefore, we will reserve this 
criterion for future consideration if the need arises. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) 
(per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that 
are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not provided the required initial evidence of either a one-time achievement or 
documents that meet at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) and so has not 
demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. As a result, the 
petition will remain denied and we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced 
in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. Nevertheless, we advise that we have reviewed the record in the 
aggregate and conclude that it does not support a conclusion that the Beneficiary has the acclaim and 
recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks to classify the Beneficiary under a highly restrictive visa classification that is 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
towards the top. See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that 
even major league athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability; Visinscaia, 4 F.Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks omitted) 
(finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design"). The record 
demonstrates that the Beneficiary's artistic talent and animation skills are highly regarded and valued 
by her employers and colleagues, and that she has applied those skills to some critically acclaimed and 
commercially successful video games and motion pictures. However, the Petitioner has not shown 
that the significance of the Beneficiary's work in the animation field is indicative of the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a "career of acclaimed work in 
the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Beneficiary 
has garnered sustained national or international acclaim in the field, and that she is one of the small 
percentage who has risen to the very top of her field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(l)(A) of the Act 
and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility as 
an individual of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-mentioned reasons, 
with each considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
