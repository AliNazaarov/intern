Form 1-140, Irnrnigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a search engine opt1m1zer, seeks first preference immigrant classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act 
(the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can show their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner's evidence did 
not satisfy at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). This matter is before us on 
appeal, which we review de novo. Matter ofChristo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). 
The Petitioner must establish his eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 
25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). Upon de novo review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has 
been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation; (ii) the alien seeks to enter this country to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and (iii) the alien's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
"Extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of 
the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a 
multi-part analysis. First, an alien can show sustained acclaim and the recognition of achievements in 
the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award) or 
qualifying documentation that satisfies at least three of the ten criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) 
(including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial regulatory evidence requirements, we then consider the totality 
of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part analysis, first determining whether the documentation satisfies the required 
number of the initial regulatory criteria and then conducting a final merits determination). 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated, and the record does not otherwise show, receipt of a major, 
internationally recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. The 
Petitioner claimed below five criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), (vi), (viii), and (ix). SCOPS 
determined (in its request for evidence) that the evidence satisfied only two of the five criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii), (ix), namely that he has performed in a leading or critical role for 
organization or establishment having a distinguished reputation and commanded a high salary or other 
renumeration in the field. However, SCOPS determined that the record did not satisfy the remaining 
three criteria the Petitioner also claimed to have met, comprising published materials about himself, 
original contributions ofmajor significance in his field, and authorship ofscholarly articles in the field, 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v), (vi), respectively, and therefore denied the case concluding that the 
evidence did not satisfy at least three of the ten regulatory criteria. 
On appeal, the Petitioner does not contest SCOPS' conclusion that he did not meet the two criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and (v) (published materials about himself and original contributions of 
major significance in the field, respectively). We thus deem his claims as to these two criteria waived. 
Matter o_fGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 693 n.1 (BIA 2023) (noting that issues not raised on appeal are waived) 
(citation omitted); Matter of O-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (same). But he argues 
that the record establishes his claimed authorship of scholarly articles in his field, and avers that 
satisfying this criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), along with the above-referenced two criteria 
SCOPS found to have been met, shows that he has met at least three ofthe ten initial regulatory criteria. 
We conclude that the record does not satisfy 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi)'s "scholarly articles" criterion, 
and it therefore does not establish the Petitioner has met at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria. 
To satisfy 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi), there must be sufficient evidence of the Petitioner's "authorship 
of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 
We first determine whether he authored scholarly articles in the field. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f-chapter-2. In the academic arena, 
"a scholarly article reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical discourse. It is 
written by a researcher or expert in the field who is often affiliated with a college, university, or 
research institution." Id. They are "also generally peer reviewed by other experts in the field of 
specialization," "should have footnotes, endnotes, or bibliographies." Id. For other fields, it "should 
be written for learned persons in that field." Id. "Learned" is defined "as having profound knowledge 
gained by study" and "learned persons" include all persons having profound knowledge of a field. Id. 
Next, we consider whether a publication was a professional, major trade, or major media publication. 
Id. Relevant factors for professional and major trade publications include their intended audiences 
and, for major media, their circulations or readerships relative to other media in the field. Id.

The evidence does not show that this book constitutes "scholarly articles" for purposes of satisfying 
the plain language criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). The record does not contain any evidence, 
and the Petitioner does not claim, that: he was or is affiliated with any academic or research institution; 
his book was published in academia; it reports on original research, experimentation, or philosophical 
discourse; or that it was peer-reviewed by any expert in the field. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
at F.2(B)(l). Although he refers to his book as a "scholarly work" that also had some commercial 
success in China, the Petitioner did not submit any ofits substantive contents or evidence ofany related 
discourse that may indicate its claimed scholarly nature, as considered in the academic arena. Id. 
Further, he submitted no evidence that his book contains the features of scholarly articles, such as 
footnotes, endnotes, or bibliographies. Id. For these reasons, the evidence does not demonstrate that 
the Petitioner's book qualifies as "scholarly articles" in the academic arena. 
As stated, for "other fields," scholarly articles should be written for learned individuals who have 
profound knowledge in the field gained by study. Id. Here, although he highlights that many colleges 
have purchased his book and he has earned significant income from book sales, the record lacks 
evidence that it was written for learned persons in the field. The back cover page of the book states 
that it introduces various knowledge points of SEO technology, suitable "for beginners to reference 
and learn from the basics to the complicated"; and an online description states it is "suitable for SEO 
beginners and readers with some experience" and "as a textbook for e-commerce and computer 
application majors in colleges and universities." Apart from these descriptions, he does not clarify, 
and the record lacks evidence of: the book's specific audience and its actual readerships that may 
inform whether it was written for learned persons in the field. The record instead indicates that the 
book was written generally for beginners and potential students, rather than learned individuals who 
have profound SEO knowledge they gained through study. 
Accordingly, the record does not establish that the Petitioner's book qualifies as "scholarly articles" 
and he therefore has not satisfied the evidentiary criterion as set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 2 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring them to establish 
sustained national or international acclaim and present extensive documentation to demonstrate their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. Here, the 
Petitioner's inability to meet the above regulatory criterion means he cannot satisfy the initial 
evidentiary requirement at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). Therefore, we cannot conclude that he is an alien of 
extraordinary ability and need not further analyze the merits under the Act. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 
1119-1120; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, F.Supp.3d, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) 
(finding that the regulation "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). As our finding is dispositive of this appeal, we 
decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. See, e.g., 
INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not required to reach issues 
unnecessary to the ultimate conclusion).
