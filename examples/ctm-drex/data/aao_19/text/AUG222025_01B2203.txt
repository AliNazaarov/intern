Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a computer scientist, requests classification under the employment-based, first­
preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 (b)(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § l l 53(b )(1 )(A). Successful petitioners 
for U.S. permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international 
acclaim" and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 
203(b)( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the record did not establish that 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, as 
required. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if they have extraordinary ability in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or 
international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. Section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(b)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established his receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). SCOPS determined the Petitioner met only two (published material under 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii) and judging under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv)) of the four claimed categories of 
evidence. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he fulfills the two remaining claimed criteria. Any 
ground of ineligibility that is not raised on appeal is waived. See Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 l&N Dec. 330, 
336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (citing Matter ofR-A-M-, 25 l&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012)). 
A. Original Contributions of Major Significance (8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v)) 
The Petitioner claims he meets this criterion based on his publications, citations to his work, and 
recommendation letters from experts in his field. But this evidence demonstrates the Petitioner has 
published his research and his work has been cited to by others in his field. It does not preponderantly 
establish that he has made contributions of major significance to his field. So, we conclude the 
Petitioner does not meet this criterion. 
The Petitioner self-identifies himself as an "established expert in computer science, and within this 
broader field, [is] one of the few leading specialists in computer vision." The Petitioner claims that 
he "is especially well known for his development of a novel 6-dimensional object pose estimation 
algorithm that solves the issue of occlusion, allowing for the improved real-world applications of 
computer vision-enhanced automation." The Petitioner also credits himself with "novel methods and 
original discoveries" stemming from "sophisticated investigations of 3D and eve-based computer 
vision" specifically an original contribution in "developing point-based representation algorithms for 
improving 3D computer vision models." And the Petitioner indicates his "develop[ment of] 
algorithms to improve data extraction in high-speed event cameras" is an original contribution to his 
field. 
The Petitioner points to "10 peer-reviewed scientific articles (5 first authored)," the attraction of a 
"wide audience and [citation] by researchers both in the United States and abroad," and the 
testimonials of many individuals he identifies with a level of repute in his field describing the 
significance of his work to the field.

B. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration for 
services, in relation to others in the field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the person's salary or remuneration is 
high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
ManualF.2(B)(l), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. USCIS determines whether the USCIS does 
not interpret the phrase "has commanded" to mean that the person must have already earned such 
salary or remuneration in order to meet the criterion. Rather, a credible contract or job offer showing 
prospective salary or remuneration may establish that the person has been able to command such 
compensation. Id. 
Evidence regarding whether a petitioner's compensation is high relative to that of others working in 
the field can include compensation surveys and organizational justifications so long as the description 
ofthe occupation matches, the validity ofthe survey is credible and reliable, and the location, currency, 
and salary rate being measured are comparable. Id. 
In support of his commanded salary, the Petitioner submitted an offer letter for employment with his 
current employer containing his base salary, an award letter from his current employer describing a 
long-term cash award paid twice yearly for four years, a printout containing occupational employment 
and wage statistics for computer and information research scientists issued by the U.S. Bureau of 
Labor Statistics, a printout describing wage data for computer and information research scientists 
where the Petitioner works, a graphic describing the mean wage for the state of Texas, and job offer 
letters from competitors of his current employer with offered wages. 
The regulatory requirement that the commanded salary be high "in relation to others in the field" is 
interpreted as allowing comparisons to those employed in the same occupation at a similar level. See 
Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a professional golfer's 
earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dept. of Homeland Sec., 420 F. 
App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is 
not a comparison to others in the field); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) 
( considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-
5 
45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) ( comparing salary of NHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
The Petitioner's job offer letter from his current employer reflects that he is occupying the position of 
senior applied research scientist. But the record does not contain a description of the duties of this 
position at the Petitioner's place of employment. Consequently, we are unable to evaluate if the 
printouts relate to the Petitioner's field. There is insufficient material, relevant, or probative evidence 
to demonstrate that the senior applied research scientist position is in the same field as computer and 
information research scientists as the Petitioner claims. 
Moreover, the job offer letters the Petitioner submitted are for software engineer, machine learning 
engineer, and machine learning research engineer position. The alternate job offer letters similarly do 
not sufficiently describe the offered jobs such that we can evaluate if they are in the same field as the 
Petitioner's occupation. Or in other words, the evidence the Petitioner submitted to support his claim 
of eligibility under this criterion does not present adequate context to demonstrate that his salary with 
his current employer is high in relation to others in his field. For the reasons discussed above, the 
Petitioner did not show he meets this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that they are an alien of extraordinary ability and need not farther 
analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -
- F.Supp.3d---, 2025 WL 1508028, at* 10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes 
a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability 
visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required 
to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Regardless, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already 
at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 
I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league level athletes do not 
automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary 
ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (stating that extraordinary ability is 
such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087 at 
*1 ( stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of 
individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of 
his work is indicative ofthe required sustained national or international acclaim or it is consistent with 
a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. Whilst the Petitioner 
need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the 
record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2).
