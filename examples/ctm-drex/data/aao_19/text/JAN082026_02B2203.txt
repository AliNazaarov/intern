Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a medical director and research scientist, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that although the 
Petitioner demonstrated she meets at least three ofthe initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, 
she did not establish, as required, that she has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and 
is among those individuals in the small percentage at the very top of her field. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which bas been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioning alien must seek to enter the United States to continue work in 
the area of extraordinary ability and show that their entry into the United States will substantially 
benefit the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(b)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition oftheir achievements in the field through a one-time achievement in the form 
of a major, internationally recognized award. Alternatively, the petitioner can submit evidence that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those standards do not readily 
apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows the submission of 
comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383 (5th Cir. 2022); 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt of a major internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x). The Petitioner previously claimed that she could meet up to eight of these ten criteria. SCOPS 
determined the Petitioner met three criteria by documenting that she had judged the work of others in 
her field, authored scholarly articles in professional publications, and commanded a high salary in 
comparison to others in her field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv), (vi) and (ix). 
Having concluded that the Petitioner met this first evidentiary threshold, SCOPS proceeded to a final 
merits determination, where they concluded that she had not demonstrated, by a preponderance of the 
evidence, that she had garnered sustained national or international acclaim and her achievements have 
been recognized in the field of endeavor, demonstrating she is one of that small percentage who has 
risen to the very top of the field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that she satisfies two additional criteria and that the evidence in the 
aggregate establishes her eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability. We need not determine 
whether the Petitioner satisfies additional evidentiary criteria because the appeal hinges on the final 
merits determination. 1 Therefore, we will evaluate the totality of the evidence based on the 
documentation presented to SCOPS in the context of the final merits determination below. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
Given the structure of the regulations and the two-step analysis described in Kazarian, the satisfaction 
of at least three evidentiary criteria alone does not automatically or presumptively establish eligibility. 
In a final merits determination, we evaluate whether a petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance

The Petitioner completed her education in the United Kingdom, where she earned a bachelor's degree

The Petitioner asserts that her achievements in the field of cardiovascular medicine have helped her 
"establish a reputation as one of the top figures in the field, worldwide." 
As discussed further below, the Petitioner has judged the work of others in her field through the peer

achievements reflect a "career ofacclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress at H.R. Rep.

standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner 
to present more extensive documentation than that required for lesser classifications." 56 Fed. Reg. 
30703, 30704. Here the Petitioner has not established that she meets these very high standards. See 
Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 386-87 (stating that the narrow definition reflects the stringency of the

As authoring scholarly articles is often inherent to the work of scientists and researchers in academia,

The Petitioner has consistently emphasized that the individual and collective citations to her work 
show that she has received the required sustained acclaim for her publications and research 
achievements. Citing to a 2003 study on federal research finding and university research output 
published by an economics journal, which was not provided for the record, she asserted that "even 
faculty members of some of the most research-oriented universities ... publish, on average less than 
two papers and these are cited less than once." Based on the results of this study, she stated that her 
own citation record must be considered "remarkable." 
Evidence establishing that the total rate of citations to a petitioner's body of work is high relative to 
others, such as a high h-index, is weighed as a positive factor in the final merits determination. See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.5(B)(2). However, such a determination depends "on 
the field and the comparative data the petitioner provides." Here, the Petitioner's four most-cited 
articles, published between 2007 and 2012, have garnered what appears to be a high raw number of 
citations, with her most cited article receiving 641 citations since its publication in 2010, the others 
receiving 156, 200 and 316, citations, respectively, at the time of filing. But raw citation numbers 
alone do not establish that the citations are "high relative to others in the field." Here, the Petitioner 
did not submit sufficient field-specific comparative data to provide this context for her publication and 
citation record.

Overall, while these letters attest to the Petitioner's significant skills and accomplishments, they do 
not establish her extraordinary ability when considered in the totality ofthe evidence. The authors did 
not provide sufficient information and explanation to show that the Petitioner is viewed by the overall 
field, rather than by a solicited few, to be among the upper echelon or that she has garnered recognition 
on a national or international scale, consistent with being among the small percentage at the very top 
of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. l:20-cv-991 (LMB/TCB), 2021 WL 1566071, at

C. 0-1 Nonimmigrant Status 
The record reflects that the Petitioner was previously classified as an 0-1 nonimmigrant with 
extraordinary ability. However, the prior approval of an 0-1 petition is not determinative when 
evaluating eligibility for an immigrant petition for an alien of extraordinary ability. 13 We are not 
required to approve applications or petitions where eligibility has not been demonstrated merely 
because of prior approvals that may have been erroneous. See Matter ofChurch Scientology Int 'l, 19 
I&N Dec. 593, 597 (Comm'r 1988). Each petition is separate and independent and must be 
adjudicated on its own merits, under the corresponding statutory and regulatory provisions. Here, for 
the reasons discussed, the Petitioner has not met her burden to establish that she is eligible for the 
requested immigrant classification. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability.
