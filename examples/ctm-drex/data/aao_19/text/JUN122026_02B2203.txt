Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a cybersecurity specialist who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This 
first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for 
Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did he demonstrate that eligibility when considering the 
totality of the evidence. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 of the Act; 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter de 
novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, we 
will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022).

l. Appellate Claims that Two-step Process is not Authorized 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 810 (E.D. Pa. 2025); 
Agrawalv. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. 
v. Jaddou, No. 4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) ( citing Perez v. Mortg. 
Bankers Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of 
the congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. To date, no other 
federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such a thorough 
analysis of the legislative and agency history. 
That brings us to the Petitioner's claim that a single district court opinion established the two-step 
process is ultra vires (i.e., beyond USCIS' legal authority). But that opinion has no authority here. 
We are only bound to follow published case law from a U.S. court of appeals for sufficiently similar 
situations within the same circuit. Matter ofAnselmo, 20 I&N Dec. 25, 31 (BIA 1989); Matter of 
K-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715, 718 (BIA 1993). Cases before us that fall outside a U.S. court of appeals' 
jurisdiction carry no binding effect. Matter ofAnselmo, 20 I&N Dec. at 31 (finding administrative 
appellate bodies "are not required to accept an adverse determination by one circuit court of appeals 
as binding throughout the United States"). 
U.S. district courts' opinions carry even less of a binding effect as we are not required to follow their 
published decisions, not even within the same district. Matter ofDuarte-Gonzalez, 28 I&N Dec. 688, 
690 n.2 (BIA 2023). Although we may consider a district judge's reasoning as a useful aid in our 
analysis, we are not required to follow it as a matter of law. Matter ofN-A-G-C-, 29 I&N Dec. 662, 
665 (BIA 2026) (citing Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. at 718); see also Trump v. CASA, Inc., 606 U.S. 
831, 852 (2025) (concluding that as a matter of law, a district court's findings "extends only to the 
suing plaintiff-as evidenced by the fact that only the plaintiff can enforce the judgment against the 
defendant," and if another person or entity wishes to restrict the defendant's actions, they must file 
their own suit). 
But more than that, we do not agree with the district court's reasoning. The opinion the Petitioner 
references is Mukherji v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708, at *6 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026). 
There, the court noted that in 1995 the former Immigration and Naturalization Service proposed a 
two-step adjudicative process. The court also observed that the Office of Personnel Management's 
(OPM) docket showed that the 1995 notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) was classified as a 
substantive rule. Based on that classification of the entire NPRM, the court treated the two-step 
process as the sole reason the proposed rule was a substantive or legislative rule, and it did not evaluate 
whether the numerous other provisions within the proposed rule might have been the basis for OPM's 
classification. 
On that understanding, the court concluded that the two-step adjudicative process constituted a 
legislative or substantive rule that could be implemented only through notice-and-comment 
3 
rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act. In doing so, the court did not fully engage with 
USCIS' stated rationale for adopting the two-step process, namely that a federal circuit court required 
it, and that using one approach within that circuit and a different approach elsewhere was not prudent 
or feasible. USCIS' implementing memorandum discussed the circuit court's decision in detail and 
expressly adopted that approach. USCIS respectfully submits that this explanation satisfies any 
obligation to articulate a rational basis for the policy change. 
In addition, the Mukherji decision did not appear to address how that petitioner's satisfaction of the 
criteria in that case related to the method of adjudication. The court ordered approval of the petition 
without analyzing whether any favorable determinations made at step one had been reached under the 
prior approach in which USCIS did not conduct a qualitative sustained-acclaim analysis under each 
individual criterion. The five criteria the court found satisfied ( awards, published material, judging, 
contributions, and leading or critical role) thus were not adjudicated in the manner the court seemed 
to contemplate reinstating. USCIS respectfully submits that this omission was not harmless, because 
it bears directly on whether the court's remedy accords with the adjudicative framework it preferred. 
And about congressional intent, USCIS understands Congress to have intended that qualifying 
candidates for this immigration benefit be among those at the top of their field. The House Judiciary 
Committee explained: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small 
percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor." H.R. Rep. 
101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 at *6739 (House 
Committee Report). Mukherji, however, understood the legislative history differently, stating that the 
Conference Report on the Senate bill that ultimately became law "says absolutely nothing" about a 
requirement that persons seeking priority-worker extraordinary ability classification be part of "that 
small percentage of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field" or any similar 
formulation. H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, 101st Cong., 2d Sess. (Oct. 26, 1990). 
USCIS respectfully reads the legislative history differently. The "Senate Bill" material the court cited 
is from a Conference Report, while the "small percentage" language appears in a House Judiciary 
Committee Report. These documents serve different functions in the legislative process. When a 
conference report does not address a particular substantive issue, as is the case here, the committee 
reports that originally developed and explained the provision remain the most authoritative evidence 
of congressional intent. See Zuber v. Allen, 396 U.S. 168, 186 (1969) (noting that committee reports 
"represent the considered and collective understanding ofthose Congressmen involved in drafting and 
studying proposed legislation"); Garcia v. United States, 469 U.S. 70, 76 (1984) ( explaining that "[i]n 
surveying legislative history we have repeatedly stated that the authoritative source for finding the 
Legislature's intent lies in the Committee Reports on the bill"). 
The House Judiciary Committee Report, H.R. Rep. 101-723, supplies the substantive explanation of 
Congress's intent for the Immigration Act of l 990's extraordinary-ability classification, including the 
"small percentage" formulation. As summarized by the Congressional Research Service, committee 
reports typically outline the need and purpose of legislation and may include statements of legislative 
intent to guide both the executive branch in implementation and the judiciary in interpretation. See 
Committee Reports: Required Contents, Congressional Research Service Products (Library of 
Congress) (Jan. 22, 2008). 
4 
By contrast, the Conference Report, H.R. Conf. Rep. 101-955, primarily reconciles differences 
between the House and Senate versions and describes how those differences were resolved. Its focus 
is on the compromise text rather than on restating all underlying policy judgments that were already 
addressed in the committee reports. A conference report is just the final, official version of a 
compromise bill that only lists the specific legal text and the formal steps the House and Senate must 
take to pass it. Coeference Reports and Joint Explanatory Statements, CONGRESS.GOV (Jun. 11, 
2015), https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/98-382. In that light, the Conference Report's silence 
on the "top of the field" requirement is most reasonably understood as reflecting acceptance, rather 
than rejection, of the House Judiciary Committee's previously articulated intent. 
Accordingly, USCIS respectfully understands H.R. Rep. 101-723 to remain the controlling statement 
of congressional intent on this point. That understanding is reflected in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), which 
requires "a level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
Not only is the Petitioner incorrect in relying on this flawed analysis, but he also did not assert these 
claims in the proceedings before SCOPS and he may not advance them for the first time in his appeal. 
Whitton v. Dixon, No. 25-580, 2026 WL 1513270, at *2 (U.S. June 1, 2026) (declining to evaluate 
new claims, legal theories, or evidence that were not before the original deciding authority in the first 
instance); Santana-Gonzalez v. Bondi, 172 F.4th 736, 744 (9th Cir. 2026) (finding a filing party cannot 
raise new issues on appeal that it did not present to the lower trier of fact); Matter ofG-C-1-, 29 I&N 
Dec. 176, 177 n.2 (BIA 2025) (citing Matter ofH-L-S-A-, 28 I&N Dec. 228, 240 n.10 (BIA 2021) and 
noting that issues presented for the first time on appeal will not be addressed). 
2. Final Merits Analysis 
The Petitioner's appeal rests entirely on two arguments. First is that he meets two additional criteria, 
which we already indicated he satisfied the minimum of at least three meaning it is unnecessary that 
we address other criteria. Second, he claims the final merits determination process is unlawful. The 
Petitioner does not advance any argument in the alternative contesting the actual basis for SCOPS' 
denial: his failure to demonstrate his sustained national or international acclaim and that he is one of 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. 
We consider that issue waived on appeal as he addressed it below but fails to do so now. When the 
denial decision contains a specific basis for the adverse decision, it is the Petitioner's responsibility to 
demonstrate in their appeal that SCOPS made a prejudicial error when it denied on that basis. The 
failure to make such a demonstration-as we have here-means the below decision on this topic will 
stand. And by not raising issues relating to a final merits totality analysis, the Petitioner has waived 
that topic in this appeal. Matter ofGarcia, 28 I&N Dec. 693, 693 n.1 (BIA 2023) ( citing Matter of 
R-A-M-, 25 I&N Dec. 657,658 n.2 (BIA 2012) and finding issues that are not meaningfully challenged 
in the appeal are waived); Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 (BIA 2021) (finding when a 
filing party mentions an issue without developing an argument, the issue is deemed waived). 
And the Petitioner cannot revive it by filing a motion on this decision as a filing party cannot cure 
their failure to preserve an argument in a previous stage of the same proceedings through a 
5 
post-adjudicative motion. Philipp v. Stiftung Preussischer Kulturbesitz, 77 F.4th 707, 709-10 (D.C. 
Cir. 2023); United States v. Campbell, 26 F.4th 860, 875-76 (11th Cir.), cert. denied, 143 S. Ct. 95, 
214 L. Ed. 2d 19 (2022) (finding that unless it would result in a miscarriage ofjustice, in most cases 
an issue abandoned on appeal should still be dismissed in any subsequent motion without addressing 
the merits). Instead, if he wishes to raise those issues again, he should achieve that by filing a new 
petition. 
The Petitioner's only arguments that might be construed as being directed at the actual basis for the 
decision come couched within his discourse calling a final merits determination unlawful when he 
asserts that USCIS acted in an arbitrary and capricious manner. He claims he "submitted substantial 
documentation of his excellence," discussing "most impressive letters of recommendation touting his 
high-level contributions in his field," and how he authored a few articles in his area. The Petitioner 
then states "[i]t is unclear why the reviewing officer determined that Petitioner did not meet the 
'Excellent' level. There is no articulated standard, objective, or specific criteria upon which the 
Service can judge why he did not meet the standards." 
What is unclear is from where the Petitioner introduces the concept of excellence or "the 'Excellent' 
level." The only statutory or regulatory mention of excellence is under the prizes or awards criterion 
and the requirement that any of those must be recognizing excellence in the field. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i). This classification's standard is that he demonstrate his accomplishments establish 
he is extraordinary and that standard is conveyed through the statute as being "demonstrated by 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation" and through the regulation as "a level of expertise indicating that 
the individual is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
So we do not agree with the Petitioner's statement that "the reviewing officer failed to articulate the 
required standard and the failure to meet that standard by the Petitioner." And while we may agree 
that the statute doesn't "say anything about an individual being required to stay indefinitely at the top 
of their field," we cannot ignore the requirement that he demonstrate his national or international 
acclaim has been sustained. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i); 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). And we do not agree 
with the Petitioner's implication that SCOPS required him to show that he has remained indefinitely 
at the top of the field simply through the established two-step process. 
Because of the unusual fact pattern and the fact that the Mukherji court decision emerging a few days 
after SCOPS denied the petition appears to have preoccupied the Petitioner's efforts on appeal, we 
will still perform a final merits determination. 
The Petitioner's achievements fall far short of meeting this classification's stringent requirements. 
With regard to the Petitioner's judging experience, the record does not clearly demonstrate that was 
judging the work ofothers in the same or an allied field when he performed peer review for a publication. 
The publication in question publishes research across a wide array of scientific disciplines and the letter 
he offered doesn't specify he reviewed manuscripts relating to cybersecurity or an allied field. Even so, 
he has not illustrated that a single instance of conducting peer review is the type of performance 
distinguishing him from others in the field-such as receiving and completing independent review 
requests from a significant number of journals or conferences, serving in an editorial capacity for a 
6 
distinguished journal, or chairing a technical committee for a reputable conference-we cannot determine 
that he is among that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 
Nor is his publication record the type that might aid in qualifying for this restrictive immigrant 
classification. While one of the articles he authored appeared in a reputable resource, the vast majority 
ofthe remaining material appeared in two publications that have not earned an equally reputable standing. 
SeeJafarovv. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding 
it appropriate for USCIS to consider the publications' significance in the ultimate merits determination). 
And the Petitioner did not demonstrate that the low rate of citations to his work (between three and seven 
citations) depicts he is one who is among the small percentage at the top of the field and has sustained 
national or international acclaim, nor did he demonstrate "particular prestige or acclaim associated with 
publication in such journals." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(8)(2); see also 
Agrawal v. Miller, No. 4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *23 (upholding that minimal global citations 
from a petitioner's articles do not demonstrate acclaim in the field). 
As it relates to his contributions to the field, SCOPS determined under the criterion in step one, despite 
the originality of some of the Petitioner's work, that he did not demonstrate that work has "been of such 
influence and importance, as to effect the field of cybersecurity as a whole." The Petitioner developed an 
AI-powered, cloud-based cybersecurity platform that streamlined security testing and he primarily 
supported his claims of how widely that impacted the field through letters, and lesser instances of 
recognition or commendations. His publication record does not reflect that his work has provoked 
widespread commentary or received broad notice from others working in the field based upon his total 
number of citations. Regarding the Petitioner's citation history as it pertains to the significance of his 
contributions to the field, SCOPS noted that "while a moderate amount of citations to the petitioner's 
work demonstrates awareness ofthe work and its value, not every researcher who performs moderately 
valuable research has inherently made a contribution of major significance to the field as a whole." 
We similarly conclude that the Petitioner has offered insufficient evidence to support the contention 
that his work has established widespread recognition or attention from others in the field. 
While his work as a whole may be technically valuable, he has not shown objective indicators of 
field-wide impact such as substantial citations. Instead, the record shows primarily recent preprint 
publications and professional achievements that better align with a skilled practitioner, rather than 
evidence of"original contributions of major significance" under this classification's high standards. 
Moving to his work for organizations with a distinguished reputation, while the Petitioner claims his role 
as a lieutenant in cybersecurity for the national police ofhis home country, there was no showing that this 
institution enjoys a distinguished reputation or how his role was leading or critical to it. Nor did he 
illustrate how his roles for two other entities were indicative of or consistent with sustained national 
acclaim or a level of expertise indicating that he is one ofthat small percentage who have risen to the very 
top of his field. 
He did enjoy a qualifying performance for the company he founded and that entity enjoys a distinguished 
reputation in his field. But one instance of satisfying the criterion in step one does not necessarily show 
he has been able to sustain that level of acclaim. Chwyumov v. USCIS, No. 2:18-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 
WL 5802041, at *3 (W.D. Wash. Nov. 7, 2019), aff'd, No. 2:18-CV-00841-RAJ, 2019 WL 5802041 
(W.D. Wash. Nov. 7, 2019) (upholding that success of one business does not equate to national or 
7 
international acclaim). Other federal courts have also found that working in a leadership position for a 
recognized and famous company does not automatically show an alien has risen to the top of the field 
and has sustained acclaim. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *4 
(E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021). "In determining whether the beneficiary has enjoyed 'sustained' national or 
international acclaim, the officer should consider that such acclaim must be maintained." See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(l). According to Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), the 
definition ofsustain is "[t]o support or maintain, esp. over a long period ... [or] [t]o persist in making (an 
effort) over a long period." The Petitioner's performance in their leading or critical roles should reflect 
their performance led to or illustrates sustained national or international acclaim. Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 
3d at 820. This is not indicative of or consistent with sustained national acclaim or a level of expertise 
indicating that he is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of his field. 
Regarding the Petitioner's compensation, he wasn't able to align his claims and evidence to satisfy the 
requirements in step one. While our reasoning on that issue might have differed somewhat from SCOPS' 
analysis in the denial, we would have come to the same conclusion. The Bureau of Labor Statistics 
comparative data the Petitioner submitted only accounted for an annual salary and it did not include 
bonuses or anything of value beyond the annual wage. But he presented an offer letter with an annual 
salary of $155,000 and combined that with a bonus. In a separate claim, he also presented his hourly 
wage and extrapolated that into an annual salary without any evidence that those contracted jobs resulted 
in full-time work, much less a salary at the annualized rate. It is from those examples that he claims place 
him among the top ten percent of earners in his field. First, a petitioner's salary in the top 10 percent of 
employees is oftentimes not so impressive as to place him at the very top of the field endeavor. Jafarov 
v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9. 
Second, even if we were to consider the contents of the offer letter to be a high salary, earning a high 
salary alone may not be sufficient to establish he is a singularly extraordinary alien. See Hamal v. DHS, 
No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (D.C. Cir. Jan. 31, 2023); Bodhankar v. USCIS, No. 
119CV706MADCFH, 2020 WL 777211, at *5 (N.D.N.Y. Feb. 18, 2020) (finding that the plaintiffs high 
salary was not so high as to indicate that he has risen to the very top of the field). And third, the data the 
Petitioner provided was not contextualized to the geographic area where his work would occur and 
nationwide statistics are not a proper comparison. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, 
F.2(B)(l) (repeatedly reflecting that comparative wage or remuneration data should not only be in their 
field, but also in the geographic area); see also Berardo v. USCIS, No. 3:19-CV-01796-SB, 2020 WL 
6161459, at *6 (D. Or. Oct. 20, 2020) (citing to agency policy to conclude that "geographic differences 
are relevant to a determination ofwhether a salary is high in relation to others in the field"). The Petitioner 
has not provided information that would enable us to provide a proper determination ofwhether his salary 
is reflective "ofthat small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 
To summarize, the record does not show that the Petitioner's limited judging experience, modest and brief 
publication record, or cited contributions have influenced the cybersecurity field as a whole or elevated 
him into the small percentage at the very top of his field. His peer review activity is isolated and not 
clearly tied to cybersecurity, and his articles are largely published in outlets without particular prestige or 
significant citation impact. Apart from one qualifying role in his own company, his positions with other 
organizations do not demonstrate sustained national or international acclaim, and his compensation 
evidence is neither reliably calculated nor properly contextualized to establish that his earnings place him 
among the very top of practitioners in his field.

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above reasons, with each considered as an 
independent and alternate basis for the decision. In visa petition proceedings, it is a petitioner's burden 
to establish eligibility for the immigration benefit sought. The Petitioner has not met that burden here. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
