Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a logistician, requests classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-
1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality 
Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. 
permanent residence in this category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" 
and extensively document recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) 
of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner did not meet 
any of the requested category's ten evidentiary requirements - three less than needed for a final merits 
determination. 1 On appeal, he contends that he satisfied the following four evidentiary criteria: 
• Membership in associations in his field requiring outstanding achievements of their members; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in the field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 53 7, 53 7 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we conclude that he has not satisfied the requisite minimum number of evidentiary 
requirements. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW

The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).2 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) must then make a final merits 
determination. To merit approval, the record - as a whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained 
national or international acclaim" and recognized achievements placing them among the small 
percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding 
USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); 
see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and His Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner, a Brazilian native and citizen, has more than 30 years' experience 
in the logistics field. He served as chief executive officer (CEO) of his own logistics company in 
Brazil for 11 years. His experience includes: operational and administrative management; 
coordination of maritime, air, and land transportation; and implementation of strategies to optimize 
logistical processes. 
Now in the United States, the Petitioner has served as CEO of his own U.S. logistics company since 
August 2018. He stated that he intends to expand his business. 
B. The Evidentiary Requirements 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - his receipt of a major internationally 
recognized award. He must therefore meet at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
To satisfy these requirements, the Petitioner's evidence must objectively meet the parameters of the 
applicable regulatory descriptions. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual F.2(B). "[N]either USCIS 
nor [the] AAO may unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those 
set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010).

l. Membership in Associations 
To meet this evidentiary requirement, a pet1t10ner must submit "[d]ocumentation of [their] 
membership in associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding 
achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their 
disciplines or field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that his proof of membership in an international moving, shipping, 
and logistics association meets this evidentiary criterion. He states that "membership in the 
[association] is directly linked to notable achievements and professional accomplishments, thus 
meeting the criteria required by regulation." 
This criterion, however, requires "documentation of the alien's membership in associations." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii) (emphasis added). As SCOPS found, the Petitioner submitted a copy of a 2024 
certificate identifying the association member as the U.S. company he founded. 
The Petitioner states: 
My membership in [the association] is not limited to a corporate membership. As CEO 
and the person directly responsible for the operations ofmy company, I have personally 
met the requirements for membership. I have submitted the necessary documentation, 
including bylaws, letters of recommendation, and certification from the [company's 
home] State ..., which demonstrate the legitimacy of my membership and highlight 
my role as a professional with outstanding skills and accomplishments. 
Contrary to the criterion's plain language, however, the Petitioner has not submitted proof of his 
individual membership in the association. He therefore has not met this evidentiary requirement. 
Also, the record does not demonstrate that the logistics association requires outstanding achievements 
of its members as judged by recognized national or international experts. A printout from the 
association's website indicates that the organization's membership requirements include proof of a 
company's operations for at least one year and sponsorship letters from two association members. The 
association posts the names ofmembership candidates on its website for 30 days. The companies then 
become members "unless we receive member comments or information that would necessitate further 
evaluation." 
The Petitioner claims that the association requires outstanding achievements of its members. He 
states: "The requirements for qualified references and careful candidate review by experienced 
members demonstrate that the association values its members' merit and professional experience, 
ensuring that only individuals with notable achievements are admitted." The record, however, falls 
short of demonstrating that the association's membership requires outstanding achievements. 
Similarly, the Petitioner claims that association members constitute recognized experts who judge 
membership candidates. But the record does not identify these purported experts or demonstrate their 
national or international recognition.

The Petitioner has not documented his membership in associations in the field requiring outstanding 
achievements of their members as judged by nationally or internationally recognized experts. We will 
therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary requirement. 
2. Judge of Others' Work 
This criterion requires "[ e ]vidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a 
judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of specification for which classification is 
sought." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv). 
The Petitioner contends that he met this evidentiary re[uirement with a letter praising his selection 
and evaluation of talent and an email message from the I He states that 
"the evidence presented ... proves that I continuously and professionally perform the role ofevaluator 
and judge of talents and processes in the logistics sector." 
SCOPS faulted the Petitioner for not demonstrating that his judging activities occurred "outside the 
scope of his work." The regulation, however, does not bar judging as part of an alien's work. See 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1121 ("[N]either USCIS nor [the] AAO may unilaterally impose novel 
substantive or evidentiary requirements beyond those set forth in 8 C.F.R. § 204.5."); see also Kinuthia 
v. Rosenberg, No. 17-10255-LTS, 2018 WL 1243955, at *5 (D. Mass. Mar. 8, 2018) ("The regulation 
does not require that the petitioner show he judge the work of others outside of his or her job.") 
The letter submitted by the Petitioner is from his business partner in Brazil, who stated that the 
Petitioner "particularly excels in the selection and evaluation of talents in the field of logistics. His 
ability to identify professionals with exceptional potential and to judge the suitability oftheir skills for 
specific roles is unparalleled." The letter states that the Petitioner: 
deeply understands how individual competencies contribute to the success of complex 
logistical operations. This skill is crucial to ensure that teams are composed of 
individuals who not only have the necessary technical skills but are also capable of 
working cohesively to achieve common goals. 
In a letter with the petition's initial filing, the Petitioner indicated that he judged the talents of 
newcomers to the logistics field. He stated: 
It is known that for new people to enter an area of activity or contribute to a company, 
someone with broad expertise needs to judge their level of knowledge and training so 
that they can be considered capable of working in the field. During the years I worked 
in Brazil, due to my expertise, I was able to participate in the approval of other 
professionals in my area to work in the same field as me, demonstrating that my 
extensive experience allowed me to judge the competence and professional 
qualifications of peers. 
This criterion requires judgment of "the work of others in the same or an allied field." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iv). By assessing the skills of newcomers to the logistics field, the Petitioner has not 
4 
demonstrated that he judged others' work in the field. Rather, the record indicates that he judged 
whether newcomers have relevant skills to work in the field, but not their actual work in the field. 3 
The January 2019 email from the. ______________,invites the Petitioner to a "Change of 
Command Ceremony." He states that "the official communication from the [commission] reaffirms 
my role as a reference professional in the sector. ... This invitation is not limited to a mere formality 
but symbolizes the recognition of my involvement in critical logistics operations and my ability to 
offer strategic support in large-scale projects." 
The record does not explain how the invitation shows the Petitioner's participation as a judge ofothers' 
work. The invitation therefore does not satisfy this criterion. 
The Petitioner has not submitted evidence of his participation as a judge of others' work in logistics 
or an allied field. We will therefore affirm SCOPS' finding regarding this evidentiary requirement. 
C. The Remaining Criteria and the Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner must meet at least three evidentiary requirements, see 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), but argues 
his satisfaction of only two more. Thus, he cannot obtain a final merits determination. We therefore 
need not reach and hereby reserve consideration of his arguments regarding the final determination 
and evidence of performance in a leading/critical role and commandment of a high salary or 
significantly high remuneration. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) 
(holding that agencies need not make "purely advisory findings" on issues unnecessary to their 
ultimate decisions). 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not satisfied the requested category's mm1mum number of evidentiary 
requirements. We will therefore affirm the petition's denial.
