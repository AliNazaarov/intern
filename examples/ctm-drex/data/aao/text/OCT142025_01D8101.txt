Form 1-129, Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker (Extraordinary Ability- 0) 
The Petitioner, a hair salon, seeks to classify the Beneficiary as a makeup artist ofextraordinary ability. 
To do so, the Petitioner pursues 0-1 nonimmigrant classification, available to individuals who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 10l(a)(15)(O)(i), 8 U.S.C. § l 101(a)(15)(O)(i). 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner did not establish 
the Beneficiary's satisfaction of the initial evidentiary criteria applicable to individuals of 
extraordinary ability in the arts: nomination for or receipt of a significant national or international 
award or at least three of six possible forms of documentation. The matter is now before us on appeal. 
8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
As relevant here, section 10l(a)(l5)(O)(i) ofthe Act establishes 0-1 classification for an individual who 
has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics, which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim, whose achievements have been recognized 
in the field through extensive documentation, and who seeks to enter the United States to continue work 
in the area of extraordinary ability. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) regulations defme 
"extraordinary ability in the field of arts" as "distinction," and "distinction" as "a high level of 
achievement in the field of arts evidenced by a degree of skill and recognition substantially above that 
ordinarily encountered to the extent that a person described as prominent is renowned, leading, or well­
known in the field of arts." See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii). Next, DHS regulations set forth alternative 
initial evidentiary criteria for establishing a beneficiary's sustained acclaim and the recognition of 
achievements. A petitioner may submit evidence either of nomination for or receipt of "significant 
national or international awards or prizes" such as "an Academy Award, an Emmy, a Grammy, or a 
Director's Guild Award," or at least three of six listed categories of documents. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(A)-(B). A petitioner may submit comparable evidence if the criteria do not readily 
apply to the beneficiary's occupation. See 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C). 
The submission of documents satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria does not, in and of itself, 
establish eligibility for 0-1 classification. See 59 Fed. Reg. 41818, 41820 (Aug. 15, 1994) ("The 
evidence submitted by the petitioner is not the standard for the classification, but merely the 
mechanism to establish whether the standard has been met."). 1 Accordingly, where a petitioner 
provides qualifying evidence satisfying the initial evidentiary criteria, we will determine whether the 
totality ofthe record and the quality ofthe evidence shows extraordinary ability in the arts. See section 
101(a)(15)(o)(i) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii), (iv). 
II. ANALYSIS 
SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not claim the Beneficiary's nomination for, or receipt of: 
significant national or international awards or prizes under 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(A). In addition, 
SCOPS concluded the Petitioner did not establish the Beneficiary's eligibility for any of the four 
claimed alternative criteria, in which at least three must be fulfilled. On appeal, the Petitioner contends 
the Beneficiary satisfies four categories of evidence, including through the submission of comparable 
evidence. 2 
A. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(l) 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(i) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed, 
and will perform, services as a lead or starring participant in productions or events which have a 
distinguished reputation as evidenced by critical reviews, advertisements, publicity releases, 
publications contracts, or endorsements." This criterion requires evidence of the beneficiary's past 
lead or starring participation in distinguished productions or events, and the beneficiary's prospective 
lead or starring participation in distinguished productions or events. 3 Evidence may demonstrate a 
lead role by establishing that the person has a principal role in the event or production, and a starring 
role means a position of great prominence relative to others in the event or production.4 
As it relates to the future part of this criterion, SCOPS indicated in its request for evidence (RFE): 
Regarding future productions or events, it appears the beneficiary will be a 
Lead/Specialized Makeup Artist for your organization, [the Petitioner]. However, you 
did [not] identify specific productions or events that the beneficiary will participate in. 
As such, you provided insufficient evidence, in the prescribed form, to establish that 
the beneficiary will perform in a lead or starring role for productions or events with 
distinguished reputations.

On appeal, the Petitioner contends that "[t]here is no regulatory mandate that the Petitioner list specific 
future productions at its own organization to satisfy this criterion." Again, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(I) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has performed, and will perform, services 
as a lead or starring participant in productions or events which have a distinguished reputation as 
evidenced by critical reviews, advertisements, publicity releases, publications contracts, or 
endorsements." This criterion requires evidence of the beneficiary's past lead or starring participation 
in distinguished productions or events, and the beneficiary's prospective lead or starring participation 
in distinguished productions or events. 5 Here, the Petitioner petitions the Beneficiary to work as a 
makeup artist for its company, and the Petitioner did not indicate that the Beneficiary will work in any 
productions or events for anybody else. As such, the Petitioner must show that the Beneficiary will 
perform services as a lead or starring participant in the Petitioner's productions or events that have 
distinguished reputations. 
Moreover, the Petitioner argues that "the record does include a letter from [the Petitioner's] president 
... describing the Beneficiary's role as Lead/Specialized Makeup Artist for the company," and "[b]y 
the very nature of that title, the Beneficiary is entrusted with a principal role on the organization's 
projects." As discussed in SCOPS' decision, a letter from the Petitioner is not one of the prescribed 
5 See generally 2 USC1S Policy Manual, supra, M.4(D)(2). 
3 
types of evidence required under this criterion. Rather, the regulation requires "critical reviews, 
advertisements, publicity releases, publications contracts, or endorsements." The Petitioner did not 
demonstrate how its self-serving letter equates to any of these regulatory required documents. 
Further, the Petitioner asserts that "USCIS erred by demanding additional detail about how her future 
work at [the Petitioner] would be 'lead' or 'critical' to the organization as a whole - an extra 
requirement found nowhere in 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(]). SCOPS' decision for this criterion 
does not support the Petitioner's assertion. SCOPS did not determine for this criterion, as claimed by 
the Petitioner, that the Beneficiary's work would be leading or critical to the organization as a whole. 
Again, this criterion requires the Beneficiary's services in productions or events, and SCOPS' decision 
made no reference to the Beneficiary's role in an organization as a whole when specifically discussing 
this criterion. Compare 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(3) relating a beneficiary's leading, starring, or 
critical role for organizations and establishments with distinguished reputations. 
Finally, the Petitioner claims that it "has received some inquiries from diamond jewelry brands 
inquiring about Beneficiary's makeup services, as well as inquiries from stylist classes for local artists 
and beauty shows - demonstrating that the Beneficiary will perform in in [sic] a lead or starring role 
for productions or events with distinguished reputations for [the Petitioner]." However, the Petitioner 
does not point to evidence to support its assertions, let alone "critical reviews, advertisements, 
publicity releases, publications contracts, or endorsements." Counsel's unsubstantiated assertions do 
not constitute evidence. See, e.g., Matter ofS-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49, 51 (BIA 1998) ("statements in a 
brief: motion, or Notice of Appeal are not evidence and thus are not entitled to any evidentiary 
weight"). Moreover, the Petitioner refers to "inquiries" rather than evidence that the Beneficiary will 
perform services as a lead or starring participant in productions or events that have a distinguished 
reputation. 
Because the Petitioner did not demonstrate the Beneficiary's futuristic services as a lead or starring 
participant in productions or events that have a distinguished reputation, we need not determine 
whether the Petitioner established the Beneficiary's past services. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 
24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" 
on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not show the Beneficiary meets this criterion. 
B. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(2) 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(2) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has achieved 
national or international recognition for achievements evidenced by critical reviews or other published 
materials by or about the individual in major newspapers, trade journals, magazines, or other 
publications." In determining whether the submitted evidence demonstrates that the beneficiary has 
achieved national or international recognition for achievements, USCIS considers both the content of 
the published material and the level of recognition enjoyed by the publication in which it appears.6 
For example, favorable coverage or publication of the beneficiary's work in major media, as 
demonstrated by high relative circulation, readership, or viewership figures, could establish national

or international recognition of the beneficiary's achievements. 7 
The Petitioner argues that it "submitted published material in Cosmopolitan magazine profiling the 
Beneficiary as one of the top make-up artists in Romania." The record contains three translations for 
articles entitled, 
'----------------------------' None of the translations, however, contain the source for the articles. Although the titles reference "Cosmopolitan" and "Cosmo," the 
Petitioner did not show the articles were actually published by Cosmopolitan, or they simply reported 
on a Cosmopolitan-related event. 
Notwithstanding the above, the first article refers to the Beneficiary as "a professional make-up artist 
who is most likely behind the selfies of many local stars" and lists three of the Beneficiary's makeup 
tips. The second item shows an online article listing the Beneficiary with two others, without any 
specific discussion above her, and mentions them as "Romania's best-known make-up artists." The 
third article reflects an identical article as the second item but with an expanded section reporting on 
the event. Here, the Petitioner did not establish that the content ofthe material reflects the Beneficiary 
achieved national or international recognition for achievements. Moreover, the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate that the general references to the Beneficiary and lack of substance in the articles show 
national or international recognition. 
Furthermore, the Petitioner claims that it "submitted information showing Cosmopolitan to be the 
largest women's media brand in the world, with an average of 72 million readers per month across 
print, digital, video, and social media channels." Even if the Petitioner demonstrated that 
Cosmopolitan published the articles, the translations reflect the Romanian language thereby indicating 
publication in Cosmopolitan Romania and posted on its website. Although the Petitioner submitted 
evidence relating the overall Cosmopolitan brand, it did not offer documentation regarding circulation, 
readership, or viewership figures for Cosmopolitan Romania or its website, demonstrating major 
status. 
For these reasons, the Petitioner did not establish the Beneficiary fulfills this criterion. 
C. 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C) 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C) states that "[i]f the criteria in paragraph (o)(3)(iv) of

argues that eligibility for comparable evidence and asserts that "USCIS, however, failed to 
meaningfully analyze the evidence under this provision."

§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(C) at either initial filing or in response to the RFE. Accordingly, we will not consider

III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner did not establish the Beneficiary meets any criteria discussed above, including through 
the submission of comparable evidence. Although the Petitioner claims the Beneficiary's eligibility 
under 8 C.F.R. §§ 214.2(o)(3)(iv)(B)(3) and (5), we need not address these evidence categories 
because the Beneficiary cannot fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of at least three. We also 
need not provide a totality determination to establish whether the Beneficiary has sustained national 
or international acclaim, has received a high level of achievement, and has been recognized as being 
prominent, renowned, leading, or well-known in the field of arts. See section 101(a)(l5)(O)(i) of the 
Act and 8 C.F.R. § 214.2(o)(3)(ii) and (iv). 10 Accordingly, we reserve these issues. 11 Consequently, 
the Petitioner has not demonstrated the Beneficiary's eligibility for the 0-1 visa classification as an 
individual of extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with 
each considered as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
