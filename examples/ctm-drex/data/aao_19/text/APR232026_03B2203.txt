Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a marketing professional and entrepreneur, seeks classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the initial 
evidentiary criteria, as required, he did not show his sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
I 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022); Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 
2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has the foreign equivalent of a bachelor's degree in business administration and earned 
a master's degree in business administration (MBA) from a U.S. business school in 2013. He claims 
to have 20 years of progressive experience in marketing management, primarily in the pharmaceutical 
and healthcare industries. The Petitioner indicates that he intends to operate a U.S. business called 
Iwhich will provide a range of services for international students seeking to attend 
American colleges and universities. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The Petitioner initially claimed that he could meet seven of the ten criteria. 
SCOPS determined that he met three criteria, relating to published materials, judging the work of 
others, and performing in leading or critical roles. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iv) and (viii). SCOPS 
therefore evaluated the totality of the evidence in a final merits determination and concluded that the 
record did not demonstrate the Petitioner's sustained national or international acclaim and his 
placement among the small percentage at the very top of his field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that the previously submitted evidence was sufficient to 
demonstrate that he meets additional evidentiary criteria, specifically those relating to awards and 
original contributions of major significance. In addition, he contends that SCOPS did not sufficiently 
weigh the totality of the evidence in its final merits determination. 
Upon review, we conclude that the Petitioner has not demonstrated that he meets the requirements of 
at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As discussed below, the record does not support 
SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner provided evidence satisfying the published materials 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), nor does it show that the Petitioner meets any of the remaining 
claimed criteria. Accordingly, he has not established that he is eligible for classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability.

SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion because the evidence submitted in 
response to the RFE did not show that the prize or award is nationally or internationally recognized 
beyond the organization that issued it. While the Petitioner discusses this criterion and the previously 
submitted evidence in his appellate brief, he does not address SCOPS' specific reasons for determining

We acknowledge that I Iis a well-known international corporation and that the 
Petitioner's selection as a "Global Marketing Professional of the Year" can be deemed a recognition 
for excellence among the company's employees in the marketing field, based on the criteria for 
granting it. However, this does not lead to a conclusion that the Petitioner's receipt of a congratulatory 
letter through the company's internal employee recognition program equates to a lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized award or prize under the plain language ofthis regulation. A representative 
of the company confirms that it maintains internal records of these awards, but the record lacks 
3 
evidence that the company's marketing awards program enjoys national or international recognition 
in the broader field. 
USCIS recognizes that "certain awards" from "well known national institutions" may qualify under 
this criterion because they may be nationally recognized. But USCIS guidance also emphasizes that 
an award available to only persons within a single employer "may have little national or international 
recognition." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). Therefore, it is the 
Petitioner's burden to provide evidence of his award's national or international recognition beyond 
reliance on the fact that the issuing employer is "well-known." See Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 
3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021) ("[A]s reflected in the regulation's plain text, the focus is on the award, not 
the awarding organization.") ( emphasis in original). For example, certain awards that gamer national 
or international media coverage may qualify under this criterion. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra at F.2(B)(l). 
While the award does not need to have the same level of recognition as a one-time achievement such 
as a Nobel Prize, it should carry recognition and national or international significance in the field that 
extends outside of the organization that issued it. See Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 136 
("Without evidence of how a larger audience viewed Plaintiff's awards, there was no way for the 
agency to evaluate whether those awards were recognized widely enough to satisfy this criterion.") 
The Petitioner has not established that his selection as a I I"Global Marketing 
Professional ofthe Year" qualifies under this criterion, because his evidence does not demonstrate that 
this award for excellence in his field has national or international recognition.

3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "evidence of the alien's original scientific, 
scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the field." A 
petitioner may show that their contributions have been widely implemented throughout the field, have 
remarkably impacted or influenced the field, or have otherwise risen to a level of major significance. 
See Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 134-35. Relevant evidence of the significance of an original 
contribution may include, but is not limited to: 
• Published materials about the significance of the alien's original work; 
• Letters providing details about how the alien's original work has been significant in the field; 
• Citation of the alien's published scholarly work at a level indicative of major significance; and 
• Patents or licenses deriving from the alien's work or evidence of its commercialization. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

4. Authorship of Scholarly Articles 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) calls for "evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly

5. High Salary or Other Significantly High Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner provided a statement from a Brazilian CPA who indicated that she has served as his 
accountant since 2008. The accountant's statement provided information about the Petitioner's "total 
professional compensation" for the years 2008, 2016, 2018, 2019, 2021, and 2024. However, he 
claimed eligibility under this criterion based solely on his "professional income for the year 2024" 
which the accountant reported as R$768,492.09, equivalent to $153,698.42.3 The Petitioner stated he 
derived the income "from his activities as an entrepreneur and consultant" and the reported amount 
included "performance-based earnings and contractual payments aligned with senior-level business 
and marketing professionals." 
The Petitioner provided a copy of a salary survey published by SalarioBR, which included 2024-2025 
salary data for the occupation of "marketing director" in I ]Brazil, with salary breakdowns 
based on company size and level of professional experience. According to this data, the highest paid 
marketing directors included in the survey earned a monthly salary of R$47,000. 
Based on the data provided by his accountant, the Petitioner asserted that his average monthly income 
derived "from consulting and business ownership" in 2024 "exceeds the top salary range reported for 
marketing directors inl Ilargest corporations by approximately 36.2%." He claimed that his 
remuneration was "well above the highest professional benchmark in the industry" and sufficient to 
establish that he commended "significantly high remuneration" in relation to others in the field. 
We agree with SCOPS' conclusion that the Petitioner did not establish that he has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration in relation to others in his field.

USCIS considers several factors when evaluating whether a comparison between a petitioner's

Further, as noted, the Petitioner seeks to compare his total remuneration (including non-salary 
earnings) to the base salaries earned by others. A more appropriate and probative basis for comparison 
would be to compare his own total remuneration to the total remuneration commanded by others who 
are engaged in similar employment. The record does not contain this type of comparative data. Nor 
does the record indicate whether the Petitioner earns a salary or document what the figure is so that it 
could be used as a basis for comparison. Absent an appropriate basis for comparison, the Petitioner 
has not supported his claim that he has commanded significantly high remuneration for his services in 
relation to others in his field. 
Accordingly, for the reasons provided, the record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner satisfies the 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
B. Final Merits Determination 
While the Petitioner meets the judging and leading or critical roles criteria, he has not established he 
satisfies the criteria relating to awards, published material, original contributions or authorship of 
scholarly articles. Because the Petitioner's inability to meet three of the initial criteria is dispositive of 
his appeal, we need not provide the type of final merits determination referenced in Kazarian, 596 
F.3d at 1119-20. We therefore reserve this issue. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per 
curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are 
unnecessary to the ultimate decision). 
Nevertheless, we have reviewed the record in the aggregate and conclude that it does not support a 
conclusion that the Petitioner has established the sustained acclaim and recognition required for the 
classification sought. The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for 
individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. 
Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) ( concluding that even major league 
10 
level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an individual of 
"extraordinary ability,"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such 
an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *l 
(D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be available to only an exceedingly 
small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). While the Petitioner shows that he has built 
a successful career in business and has received some recognition for his expertise, he has not shown 
the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or 
it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) ofthe Act. While 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification 
sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his 
field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the 
reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. Accordingly, we will dismiss the appeal.
