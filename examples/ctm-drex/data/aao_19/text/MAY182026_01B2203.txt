Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner received a major, internationally recognized award or met the requirements of at 
least three of the ten evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. 
§ 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner satisfied two criteria relating to 
participation as a judge and performing a leading and critical role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) and 
(viii). However, SCOPS farther determined that the Petitioner did not meet the remaining three 
claimed evidentiary criteria associated with published material, original contribution, and high salary 
or remuneration at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii), (v) and (ix). On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he 
meets the same five criteria. Upon de novo review, we conclude that the Petitioner has not met the 
requisite number of evidentiary criteria and therefore is not eligible for classification as an individual 
of extraordinary ability. 
A. Published Materials 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires "[p]ublished material about the alien in 
professional or major trade publications or other major media, relating to the alien's work in the field 
for which classification is sought." These materials must include "the title, date, and author of the 
material, and any necessary translation." Id. The published material should be about the alien and 
their work, rather than the alien's employer and its work (or about an organization and its work). 
Professional or major trade publications may be identified by their intended audience, while other 
major media and major trade publications should have elevated circulation or readership relative to 
other media in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy­
manual. 
We first note that although the record contains 21 articles from various publications, the Petitioner 
only submitted supporting evidence regarding the reputations of two of the publications, Milliyet and 
Hurriyet. Therefore, the Petitioner did not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the 
remaining publications qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media. Id. 
Further, of the four remaining articles, only one of them contains an author's name. On appeal, the 
Petitioner contends that the "regulation only requires that the published material include 'the title, 
date, and author'- it does not mandate that the author be a specific person's name." He farther explains

without a personal byline" and therefore, this should not be viewed as a negative factor. To support 
his claim, that this is a common practice in Turkey, the Petitioner submitted a report titled, 
"Disinformation Risk Assessment: The Online News Market in Turkey." Although the report 
confirms that this is a practice amongst the Turkish media; the report concludes that the lack of a 
personal byline is one of the reasons "nearly two-thirds of the domains in the study demonstrated a 
high or maximum risk of disinforming their online users." Therefore, this report indicates that 
information in online news articles may not be trustworthy, in part, due to this practice. Aside from 
being a negative factor to the reputation of the publications in Turkey, we are not convinced that the 
regulations permit articles without a specific author's name. The Petitioner contends that the 
requirement of a "named reporter imposes an extra-regulatory requirement and is legally improper." 
However, the regulation contains separate requirements for the "author of the material" and that the 
material be published in "professional or major trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(iii). Further, we consider the term "author" using its ordinary, common meaning. See, 
e.g., Perrin v. United States, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979) ("A fundamental canon of statutory construction 
is that, unless otherwise defined, words will be interpreted as taking their ordinary, contemporary, 
common meaning ...."). The Merriam-Webster Dictionary defines an author as "the writer of a 
literary work (such as a book)" or "one that originates or creates something." 1 As such, we conclude 
that the term "author" refers to an individual and therefore, as three of the remaining four articles do 
not list an author, they will not be included in our analysis. Javakhadze v. Mayorkas, No. 3:21-CV-
00617-E, 2023 WL 8037730, at *6 (N.D. Tex. Nov. 20, 2023) (upholding articles did not contain the 
required authors).

C. High Salary or Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) calls for "evidence that the alien has commanded a high 
salary or other significantly high remuneration for services, in relation to others in the field." A 
credible employment contract or job offer may establish that an alien "has commanded" such salary 
or remuneration, as may evidence of past earnings such as tax returns and pay statements. 
Comparative wage or remuneration data that is appropriate for the alien's occupation and location 
should be submitted to establish that their compensation is high relative to others working in the field. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
As CEO and co-founder of the Company, the Petitioner contends that he "has been compensated at a 
level commensurate with his leadership role and the company's profitability." He highlights the 
Company's financial statements in the record which show their "General Administrative Expenses." 
The Petitioner states that this "includes executive salaries and benefits." The Petitioner further states 
that, for example, the "large figures" such as $492,855 in 2022, "indicate the budget for management 
compensation was significant" and that "[i]t is reasonable to infer that [the Petitioner] as CEO, drew 
a considerable salary from these funds ...." Further, the Petitioner asserts that in that same year, the 
Company's after-tax profit was over $164,000, and that as a major equity holder, he "benefited from 
the company's profits through dividends and distributions to shareholders...." He then compares 
these figures to evidence that the median annual salary for a remote CEO in Turkey is $67,344, and 
that the top 10% earn roughly $150,000. However, the record does not contain documentation 
detailing the Petitioner's actual salary or remuneration. Although the record contains company bank 
statements and details the money set aside for executives and the total profit distributed among 
shareholders, the record does not include what the Petitioner actually earned in order for us to 
determine ifit is high compared to a "remote CEO in Turkey." On appeal, the Petitioner contends that 
he is not able to provide documentation such as W-2s or 1099s as he has been working in Turkey and 
these types of documents are not applicable. However, without any documentation reflecting the 
Petitioner's salary or remuneration, we cannot determine that he has met this criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner claimed to satisfy five of the ten initial evidence requirements under 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3). However, we conclude that the Petitioner did not satisfy at least three of the five criteria. 
Since the Petitioner did not fulfill the initial evidentiary requirement of three and has not satisfied the 
threshold initial evidence requirements at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we need not determine whether the 
Petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and is one ofthe small percentage at the very 
top of the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. Allen, -- F.Supp.3d 
---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation "establishes a necessary, 
not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
Therefore, we also decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in this matter. 
See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required 
to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision).
