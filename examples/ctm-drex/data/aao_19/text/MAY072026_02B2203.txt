Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability 
through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in 
their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied 
at least three of the initial evidentiary criteria, as required, she did not show her sustained national or 
international acclaim and demonstrate she is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal. 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) (holding 
that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely "coveted," and are 
"reserved for only the most talented" aliens"). The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained acclaim and the 
recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, 
internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) - (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. US. Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 596 F.3d 
1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted 
and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits 
determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. US. 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339, 1346 (W.D. Wash. 2011), ajf'd, 683 F.3d 1030 
(9th Cir. 2012).

3. Published Materials 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner fulfilled this criterion. However, for the reasons discussed 
below, we will withdraw that determination. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) requires 
"[p ]ublished material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major media, 
relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence shall include 
the title, date, and author of the material, and any necessary translation." 
Initially, the Petitioner provided screenshots of articles posted on realtytoday.com, minfin.com, 
mortgage-bankrates.com, usinsider.com, ceoweekly.com, nyweekly.com, and entertainmentpost.com. 
The Petitioner did not provide the author's name for the minfin.com article, and we note SCOPS 
indicated in the RFE that the articles from ceoweekly.com and nyweekly.com reflected "branded 
7 
content." 1 Regardless, the Petitioner did not include any information about the websites in order show 
they qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media. USCIS determines 
whether the publication qualifies as a professional publication, major trade publication, or major media 
publication. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). In evaluating whether a 
submitted publication is a professional publication, major trade publication, or major media, relevant 
factors include the intended audience (for professional and major trade publications) and the relative 
circulation, readership, or viewership (for major trade publications and other major media). Id. In 
her RFE response, the Petitioner did not provide any further information about these websites besides 
resubmitting the same articles and screenshots. See Hristov v. Roark, No. 09-CV-2731, 2011 WL 
4711885, at *10 (E.D.N.Y. Sept. 30, 2011) (finding the record contained "no evidence beyond the four 
comers of the articles" that the newspapers were major); see also Kumar v. Dir. of US. Citizenship & 
Immigr. Servs., No. CV RDB-23-2251, 2024 WL 1374794, at *5 (D. Md. Apr. 1, 2024) (upholding the 
plaintiff did not submit sufficient evidence to establish the material qualified as professional or major 
trade publications or other major media). As such, the Petitioner did not establish that the websites 
qualify as professional or major trade publications or other major media, as required under this 
regulatory criterion. 
The Petitioner did offer three additional website articles in her RFE response from u-news.com.ua, 
timesla.com, and I I(self-authored), discussed under the awards criterion above. 
Accompanying the articles, the Petitioner submitted screenshots from SimilarWeb reflecting various 
metrics and figures, such as the 7,393 global rank, 1,360 country rank, and 7.1 million visits for 
timesla.com; the 27,381 global rank, 305 country rank, and 2.9 million visits for u-news.com.ua; and 
the 20,771 global rank, 252 country rank, and 3.4 million visits fo~ IHowever, the Petitioner 
did not contextualize the significance, if any, of these analytics and numbers to show the major status 
of any of these websites. See Branski v. Seng, No. 24-CV-360, 2024 WL 4678939, at *5 (E.D. Wis. 
Nov. 4, 2024) (indicating that the failure to provide context to circulation figures makes it difficult to 
assess their significance); Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d at 185 (finding argument that internet-accessible 
outlets by their nature have a worldwide reach substantially undermines any reasonable interpretation 
of "major"). Without further explanation through documentation, the Petitioner did not show that the 
articles occurred in major trade publications or other major media. 2 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner did not show her eligibility for this criterion. 
Accordingly, we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination.

9. Commercial Successes 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet this criterion through comparable evidence, and 
we agree with that conclusion. The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) requires "[e]vidence of 
commercial successes in the performing arts, as shown by box office receipts or record, cassette,

compact disk, or video sales." And the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows that "[i]fthe above 
standards do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable 
evidence to establish the beneficiary's eligibility." When evaluating such comparable evidence, 
officers must consider whether the regulatory criteria are readily applicable to the person's occupation 
and, if not, whether the evidence provided is truly comparable to the criteria listed in that regulation. 
See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner argues that "the proper inquiry is not whether the [Petitioner] sold concert tickets or 
recordings, but whether she demonstrated commercial performance in her field equivalent to the level 
of success contemplated by the regulation - i.e., revenue generation, profitability, and market 
dominance measurable through objective financial data." As evidence, the Petitioner references 
I I $332,531 total revenue for 2024 and the 3,800 home-development project in Texas 
"representing a projected contract value in excess of $19 million through 2029." But under the 
commercial successes criterion, simply offering receipts or sales is insufficient unless a petitioner 
establishes that those receipts or sales qualify as commercially successful. The mere fact that a person 
has recorded and released musical compilations or performed in theatrical, motion picture, or 
television productions would be insufficient, in and of itself: to meet this criterion. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). Likewise, in order to demonstrate that the Petitioner's 
evidence is truly comparable to the commercial successes criterion, the Petitioner must establish that 
I ~evenue and/or the home-development project is analo,ous to commercial successes. Here, 
the Petitioner did not contextualize the significance oflrevenue or projected contract value 
of the home project. The Petitioner did not show that a mortgage brokerage company's revenue of 
$332,531 represents "success" in the field, such as compared to other mortgage brokerage companies, 
or that a "projected contract value" for an anticipated projected that may never be realized can also be 
considered a "success." See Joseph v. Dir. of Texas Serv. Ctr., United States Citizenship & Immigr. 
Servs., No. 24-40249, 2025 WL 458001, at *4 (5th Cir. Feb. 11, 2025) (finding prospective success is 
not comparable to actual commercial success that the regulations require). 
Accordingly, the Petitioner did not establish her eligibility for this criterion through comparable 
evidence.
