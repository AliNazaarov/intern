Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a chief executive officer (CEO) of a clean energy company in China, seeks 
classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an 
alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification makes immigrant visas available to 
those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, petitioners must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual.

First, the Petitioner claims that USCIS imposed novel requirements outside the bounds of the 
regulations. Specifically, the Petitioner contends that USCIS erroneously required a "career of 
acclaimed work" in analyz{ng the levidence pertaining to his published articles, scholarly articles, and 
leading or critical role at and evidence of his original contributions to reach "beyond his 
company." 
However, we find the Petitioner's contentions unpersuasive. Although these exact words are not found 
in the plain language of the regulations, they are reasonably inferenced or derived from the statutory 
and regulatory language. In fact, the seminal House Judiciary Committee report accompanying the 
Immigration Act of 1990 used the exact wording, "career of acclaimed work," in defining the 
legislative intent and establishing the highly restrictive standard used for the extraordinary ability visa. 
See H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990) ("[a]n alien can also qualify on the basis of a career 
of acclaimed work in the field"). Additionally, in clarifying the criterion ( v) ( original contribution of 
major significance in the field), the USCIS Policy Manual indicates that "the person developed a 
patented technology that has attracted significant attention or commercialization may establish the 
significance of the person's original contribution to the field." See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l), Criterion 5. Based on this, USCIS can logically conclude that the Petitioner's 
patents do not indicate his high standing and recognition in the field that rises to the sustained acclaim 
demanded by this visa classification because they did not attract significant attention or 
commercialization "beyond his company." Therefore, we do not agree that USCIS imposed ultra vires 
requirements. 
Secondly, the Petitioner contends that USCIS incorrectly attached certain frequency not found in the 
regulatory requirements when it concluded that producing "three research papers in a twenty+year 
timeframe" or judging activity's "frequency or level" was insufficient in demonstrating sustained 
national or international acclaim. However, the policy manual clearly dictates that in demonstrating 
sustained acclaim, a petitioner must show that he has maintained that acclaim over a period of time. 
See 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(A)(l) (providing Black's Law Dictionary's definition of 
"sustain" is "to support or maintain, especially over a long period of time ... To persist in making (an 
effort) over a long period of time"). Such demonstrations of sustaining or maintaining acclaim may 
require examining the frequency or repetition of achievements throughout one's career. As such, we 
do not agree that USCIS erred in these instances. 
In conjunction with this claim, the Petitioner asserts that USCIS failed to articulate "a comparator 
standard" or explain what publication rate or frequency would suffice to characterize an individual as 
being at the very top of his field. But we note that the Petitioner has the burden of showing that he 
has risen to the top of his field and sustained acclaim to qualify as an individual of extraordinary 
ability. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (a petitioner bears the burden of proof to 
demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence). This means that the Petitioner, not

Regarding this claim, we note that when USCIS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition, and 
has made adequate findings, it will not be required to specifically address each claim the Petitioner 
makes, nor is it necessary for it to address every piece of evidence the petitioner presents. See Abdel­
Mas ieh v. US. INS., 73 F.3d 579, 585 (5th Cir. 1996) (noting "[w]e do not require the BIA to

Furthermore, in reviewing the Petitioner's membership in CSSPE and CSSPE's constitutions in the 
record, we find that the society is "a national, academic, non-profit organization voluntarily formed 
by individuals and related entities engaged in the study of China's private economy" and the 
membership requirements include "conscientiously study[ ing] the theoretical system ofsocialism with 
Chinese characteristics," being "enthusiastic about private economy research work," and 
"recommendation by two or more council members or relevant units." But the record does not include 
any additional information to show that being part of CSSPE requires acclaim or achievements 
superseding the required recommendations. Therefore, the Petitioner's membership in CSSPE does 
not sufficiently show his sustained acclaim. 
Similarly, the record does not include sufficient details about the honors or awards that the Petitioner's 
company received to establish that the company has a distinguished reputation and elevate the 
Petitioner as someone who has achieved a career of sustained acclaim or at the very top of his field. 
The Petitioner claimed that the company received awards or honors but only provided a list of 
"Company Honors" in the company's own informational materials (page 6 of Part 1, Company 
Introduction) and did not explain the significance, dates, and relevance of these honors demonstrating 
the company's distinguished reputation. The Petitioner must support his assertions with relevant, 
probative, and credible evidence. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376. In addition, the EB-1 
classification requires a "very high standard ... to present more extensive documentation than that 
required." See H.R. Rep. No. at 59; see also Proposed Rule for Employment-Based Immigrants, 56 
Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 (July 5, 1991). Here, the Petitioner has not met his burden of presenting such 
extensive documentation with probative evidence. 
Lastly, the Petitioner's contention that USCIS failed to apply the preponderance of the evidence 
standard to the record as a whole is not persuasive as he does not specifically point to such instances 
and generally claims that the record has "reliable indicators" of his acclaim. To determine whether a 
petitioner has met his burden under the preponderance of the evidence standard, we consider not only

the quantity, but also the quality (including relevance, probative value, and credibility) ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 376; see also Matter ofE-M-, 20 I&N Dec. 77, 79-80 (Comm'r 
1989). Here, the record overall did not sufficiently include corroborating evidence to show that he is 
viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, as being among that small percentage at the 
very top of the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The recommendation letters in the 
record simply described the Petitioner's work without showing how it represents an individual whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. Additionally, the 
Petitioner did not establish that he has made impactful or influential contributions in the field reflecting 
a career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or international 
acclaim. See H.R. Rep. No. at 59 and section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The evidence depicts a successful businessman whose company is in the process of building its 
reputation in the field ofclean energy technology, but the record as a whole does not reflect recognition 
of an individual who has reached the top of his field with sustained acclaim and does not establish the 
Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. 5 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa 
classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those 
progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954; Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 
(stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, 
No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *l (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended 
to be available to only an exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this 
case, the Petitioner has not shown the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. 
See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more 
accomplished to qualify for the classification sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he 
is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. While he meets the initial evidence requirements, he has not sufficiently 
established his sustained national or international acclaim and that he is among the small percentage 
at the very top of his field. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act; Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
