Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a tattoo artist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that although the record established that the Petitioner satisfied 
the initial evidentiary requirements, it did not establish, as required, that the Petitioner has sustained 
national or international acclaim and is an alien in the small percentage at the very top of the field. 
The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a petitioner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

Instead, he claimed to have satisfied five of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R.

The Petitioner also claimed that his guest appearances at prest1g10us studios and a corporate

under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(x) and (4). 
Later, in response to a request for evidence, the Petitioner contended that some ofhis convention prizes 
also qualify as major, internationally recognized awards under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met four ofthe criteria, pertaining to published material; judging; 
display; and remuneration. SCOPS declined to consider the Petitioner's "comparable evidence" claim 
because several of the initial criteria readily apply to the Petitioner's occupation. Because SCOPS 
concluded that the Petitioner's awards do not meet the lower threshold set forth in 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), SCOPS did not directly address the Petitioner's claim of a major, internationally 
recognized award. 
In the ensuing final merits determination, SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner had not met his burden 
of proof to establish sustained national or international acclaim.

Because SCOPS granted at least three initial criteria and proceeded to a final merits determination, the 
Petitioner's claims regarding a one-time achievement and comparable evidence are arguably moot. 
We have considered the evidence underlying those claims in the context of the final merits 
determination. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate that they have extraordinary 
ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); 
see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 1 In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not 
established eligibility. 2 
The Petitioner asserts on appeal that SCOPS improperly used the final merits determination "to 
disregard probative evidence [ and] reimpose new thresholds on criteria already credited .... Kazarian 
does not permit USCIS to deny a petition by re-weighing evidence that has already been found to 
satisfy the regulatory criteria or by imposing qualitative thresholds beyond the text of the regulation." 
But the final merits determination considers the totality of the evidence in the broader context of 
sustained national or international acclaim as required by the statute. Issues that "are not relevant to 
the antecedent procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of 
evidence" "might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a petitioner is at the very 
top of his or her field of endeavor." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121. Considerations that might 
impermissibly amount to "a novel evidentiary requirement" within the narrow confines of an initial 
criterion nevertheless "might be relevant to a final merits determination." Id. 
The Petitioner documented his attendance at a number of tattoo festivals and conventions, both as an 
exhibitor and as a judge. The Petitioner's service as a judge on these occasions, particularly at exclusive 
invitational events, is a favorable factor. But it cannot suffice for the Petitioner to list his 
accomplishments. He must also provide context to show that he has reached the very top ofhis field and 
has remained there. For example, with respect to his judging work, the Petitioner has not provided 
objective evidence to show that he has consistently acted as a judge at a level consistent with sustained 
national or international acclaim at the very top of the field. 
Even as the Petitioner judged the work ofother tattoo artists, his own work won awards at several ofthese 
events. SCOPS acknowledged the awards, but concluded that "the petitioner did not establish that being 
a recipient of these awards is indicative of a 'career of acclaimed work in the field."' On appeal, the 
Petitioner maintains that festival awards are the highest honors in his field.

award ceremonies at the conventions themselves. In terms of relative acclaim, the Petitioner did not 
establish that he has received, and continues to receive, a sufficiently high number of convention awards 
to place him in the small percentage at the very top of the field. 
This is significant because the Petitioner correctly argues on appeal that we cannot consider each piece 
ofevidence in isolation; we must consider the overall pattern they form. But by the same logic, we cannot 
simply look at the Petitioner's career in isolation. The burden is on the Petitioner to establish not only 
that he has achieved a level of success or prominence in his field, but that he has achieved a level of 
recognition commensurate with the very top ofthat field. The Petitioner cannot meet this burden only by 
describing his own evidence and then asserting that this evidence demonstrates the required acclaim. The 
record lacks sufficient context to show whether, and how, his achievements place him at the very top of 
his field.

We also note the Petitioner's submission ofletters from prominent tattoo artists. These individuals clearly 
hold the Petitioner's skills in high regard. At the same time, in the course of establishing their own 
credentials, a number of these individuals cite levels of achievement that the Petitioner himself has not 
reached. Some ofthem, for instance, indicate that they have hundreds ofthousands of followers on social 
media, figures substantially higher than what the Petitioner has documented for himself. And some of 
the writers have been prominently featured on television programs about tattoo artists, a claim that the 
Petitioner has not made for himself. The record, therefore, establishes that there are levels of recognition 
and acclaim possible within the realm of tattoo art that the Petitioner has not yet attained. Earning the 
admiration of such artists is an enviable accomplishment, but it is not commensurate with reaching the 
very top of the field. 
The Petitioner has established that his considerable abilities as a tattoo artist have attracted some 
attention within his field, including the admiration of prominent artists. But the Petitioner has not 
submitted the "extensive documentation" that section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act demands to place him 
in the small percentage at the very top of the field. See 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(2). 3 
III. CONCLUSION 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields, rather than those progressing 
toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). (concluding that even 
major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory standards for classification as an 
individual of "extraordinary ability,"); Visinscaia, 4 F. Supp. 3d at 131 (internal quotation marks 
omitted) (finding that the extraordinary ability designation is "extremely restrictive by design,");
