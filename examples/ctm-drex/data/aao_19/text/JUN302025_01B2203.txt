Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a photojournalist, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See 
Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
her sustained national or international acclaim and that she is among the small percentage at the very 
top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by apreponderance of the evidence. 
Matter of Chawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de nova. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement 
(that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, 
then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). If an evidentiary standard under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) does not "readily apply" to a petitioner's occupation, the petitioner may submit "comparable 
evidence" to establish eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) 
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022) (upholding USCIS' two-step review process as 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claims eligibility for this classification based on extraordinary ability in the area of 
photojournalism. 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must demonstrate that she satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). She provided evidence for five of the ten initial evidentiary 
criteria. SCOPS determined that she met three criteria relating to published materials, participation as 
a judge of others' work, and authorship of scholarly articles. SCOPS found that she did not meet the 
requirements for the criterion of original contribution of major significance, and did not discuss or 
make a determination whether the Petitioner met the requirements for the criterion of display of work 
at artistic showcases or exhibitions. 
Although the Petitioner met three of the evidentiary criteria, SCOPS concluded that she did not 
demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim, that 
she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field, and that her achievements have been 
recognized in the field through extensive documentation. 
On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that having met her burden by satisfying three of the regulatory 
criteria, she is eligible as an alien of extraordinary ability, unless USCIS sets forth evidence to the 
contrary. She maintains that according to the Act and regulations, demonstrating receipt of a one-time 
achievement, or meeting three of the criteria is all that is required to be an alien of extraordinary ability. 
She argues that neither the Act nor regulations relating to extraordinary ability sets forth a two-part 
analysis, requiring a review of the totality of the evidence in a final merits determination. 
Acknowledging the concept of a two-part analysis arose from Kazarian and has been incorporated in 
2 
USCIS policy guidance, she explains that the Kazarian decision "mentions the concept of 'final merits 
determination,"' but "merely in passing and does not explain or provide a structural framework for 
such a determination." Since Kazarian, she explains that "USCIS has issued no formal rulemaking to 
clarify the regulatory standard, nor has the 'final merits determination' been incorporated into the 
regulations." In addition, she contends that while the USCIS Policy Manual provides examples when 
considering the totality of the evidence analysis, it "does not provide much clarification" with most 
examples relevant to academic researchers and scientists, not artists. Citing Matter of Price, 20 l&N 
Dec. 953 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) and Buletini v. INS, 860 F. Supp. 1222 (E.D. Mich. 1994), she argues 
that USCIS' evidentiary review for extraordinary ability is limited to the plain language of the 
regulations and previously issued AAO and federal court decisions 
We are not persuaded by the Petitioner's arguments. Her proposed methodology is directly refuted by 
both the Kazarian decision and the USCIS Policy Manual, and she doesn't argue why those authorities 
should be set aside in favor of the method she proposes in this appeal, except referencing lower court 
cases decided prior to Kazarian. As pointed out by the Petitioner, the Kazarian decision described the 
two-part adjudicative approach to evaluating evidence and in 2010, USCIS amended its policy based 
on that approach. See generally USCIS Policy Memorandum PM-602-0005.1, Evaluation of Evidence 
Submitted with Certain Form 1-140 Petitions; Revisions to the Adjudicator's Field Manual {AFM) 
Chapter 22.2, AFM Update ADJ 1-14 3 (Dec. 22, 2010). The Kazarian court stated, "[w]hile other 
authors' citations (or a lack thereof) might be relevant to the final merits determination of whether a 
petitioner is at the very top of his or her field of endeavor, they are not relevant to the antecedent 
procedural question of whether the petitioner has provided at least three types of evidence." Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1121 (emphasis added). This serves as the Kazarian court's acknowledgement 
that it is within the final merits determination that USCIS is to evaluate the qualitative statutory and 
regulatory requirements to determine whether a petitioner has demonstrated sustained national or 
international acclaim and that their achievements have been recognized in the field of expertise, 
indicating that the petitioner is one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field 
of endeavor. USCIS will consider all relevant evidence in the record in the totality, even if such 
evidence does not fit one of the criteria or as comparable evidence. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F.2(B)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
Next, the Petitioner argues that if we find that a two-part analysis with a final merits determination is 
required, SCOPS' decision did not consider all the evidence she submitted or adequately explain why 
the evidence was deemed insufficient. Although we conduct de nova review, we agree with the 
Petitioner and conclude that aremand is warranted in this case because SCOPS' decision is insufficient 
for review. 
SCOPS' decision does not acknowledge or discuss much of the evidence submitted with the initial 
petition and in response to the request for evidence (RFE). Both the RFE and the decision contain 
few specific references to the evidence submitted or the Petitioner's explanation of why the evidence 
demonstrates her eligibility under the claimed criteria and under the final merits determination. For 
instance, the RFE does not discuss the claimed regulatory criterion for authorship of scholarly articles 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v). In addition, the decision generally references recommendation letters 
and other evidence without providing an analysis of the evidence, and it does not address whether the 
Petitioner meets the criterion for display of work at artistic showcases or exhibitions at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(vi i). 
3 
Moreover, we agree with the Petitioner that SCOPS' decision contains factual errors which appear to 
support the decision. For instance, SCOPS' analysis of the regulatory criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 
204.5(h)(3)(v) included some problematic statements. SCOPS refers to the Petitioner's field, 
photojournalism, as being "research-driven" and that she "has engaged in extensive original research 
studies in the field of photograph" which have "a moderate amount of citations to [her] work ...." 
The Petitioner, however, has not submitted evidence of any research work or citation history of her 
work. The characterization of her field of photojournalism as being related to research is not supported 
by evidence in the record. 
In addition, in the decision's final merits analysis section, SCOPS discusses scholarly articles, 
characterizing an article published in the L.A. Times as being authored by the Petitioner and having a 
high citation history. The article, however, was authored by another individual, not the Petitioner, and 
the record does not include citation record information. We note that considering the factual errors in 
the final merits discussion of authorship of scholarly articles, it is unclear whether these errors were 
the basis for SCOPS concluding the Petitioner met the criterion for authorship of scholarly articles. 
SCOPS' decision did not include an explanation for how the Petitioner met the requirements for the 
criterion of authorship of scholarly articles. 
Finally, SCOPS generally dismisses other materials in the record without providing any specific 
identification or analysis of the evidence, leaving uncertainty as to what evidence was deemed 
insufficient. An officer's written decision must fully explain the specific reasons for denial. See 8 
C.F.R. § 103.3(a)(1)(i). When a decision does not meet these requirements, the petitioner does not 
have a fair opportunity to contest the decision on appeal. See Matter of M-P-, 20 l&N Dec. 786 (BIA 
1994) (finding that a decision must fully explain the reasons for denying a motion to allow the 
respondent a meaningful opportunity to challenge the determination on appeal). Because SCOPS' 
decision does not sufficiently address the evidence submitted with the petition and in response to the 
RFE, we will remand the matter. 
Ill. CONCLUSION 
We will withdraw SCOPS' denial of the petition and remand the matter for further review and entry 
of a new decision. SCOPS may request any additional evidence considered pertinent to the new 
determination and any other issues. As such, we express no opinion regarding the ultimate resolution 
of this matter on remand. On remand, SCOPS is instructed to re-evaluate the evidence submitted in 
support of the petition to determine whether the Petitioner satisfied the plain language of at least three 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), and to issue a new decision. SCOPS should also review the 
Petitioner's appellate brief, which further discusses the previously submitted evidence submitted in 
support of the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), as well as the supplemental evidence 
submitted on appeal. 
If SCOPS determines that the Petitioner satisfied at least three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the 
new decision should evaluate, based on the totality of the evidence in the record, whether she has 
demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, her sustained national or international acclaim, that 
she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and that her achievements 
4 
have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A) of the 
Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
