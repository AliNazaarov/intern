Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software engineer, seeks first preference immigrant classification as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner satisfied at least three of the ten required regulatory criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b )( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate sustained 
acclaim and the recognition of achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a 
major, internationally recognized award) or qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, judging others, 
published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination).

B. Final Merits Determination. 
The Petitioner has satisfied at least three of the ten evidentiary requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We therefore need not consider his arguments that he meets additional 
evidentiary criteria. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating that agencies are not 
required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision); 
see also Matter ofL-A-C-26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to reach alternative issues 
on appeal where the applicant did not otherwise meet their burden of proof) 
3 
However, granting the third initial criterion does not suffice to establish eligibility for the classification 
the Petitioner seeks. USCIS must now make a final merits determination on the Petitioner's filing. 
SCOPS did not make such a finding. Rather than remanding the matter to SCOPS to make this 
determination in the first instance, we will conduct the final merits determination. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their successes are sufficient to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the 
field of endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
We evaluate whether the Petitioner has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his 
sustained national or international acclaim, 1 that he is one of the small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor, and that his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. We also consider the evidence submitted in support of those evidentiary criteria. In 
this analysis we determine that the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility by a preponderance 
of the evidence. 
We recognize that the Petitioner has authored scholarly articles, has participated as a judge ofthe work 
of others, and has made contributions of major significance in the field. In support of the claim that 
the Petitioner has achieved sustained national and international acclaim and is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of software engineering, the Petitioner largely relied on his 
publication of scholarly articles discussing artificial intelligence and video games. Although we 
acknowledge that these articles show his achievements in the field, the record does not support the 
claim that having published 2 articles, one in 2021 and another in 2022, by itself: is indicative of 
contributions of major significance in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.(2)(B)(l) ( explaining that evidence that the person's work was funded, patented, or published, while 
may show the work's originality, will not necessarily establish its major significance to the field); see 
also Jafarov v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, 
*7 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) (finding the absence of detail about the actual impact of the petitioner's 
work and the limited citations his work had thus far received meant that he did not satisfy this 
criterion). Regarding his participation as the judge of the work of others, the record indicates, as an 
example, that the Petitioner was invited to serve as a 'jury member" and served as a 'jury member" 
atl 12020. The record reflects that the Petitioner was one of many "jury members." As 
such, following the presentations, he participated in the formal deliberation and co-authored the final 
verdict of the jury, and signed the final jury verdict as a panel member. Thus, although the Petitioner 
was a judge, according to the plain language of the regulation, he was part of a panel ofjudges and by 
his own account, he did not act independently. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 
WL 1566071, at *5 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding that simply being one judge among many does 
not clearly demonstrate plaintiff's national or international acclaim). 
The Petitioner also points to his patent application. The Petitioner describes his patent as an innovative 
system and method that represents a significant leap forward in enhancing content search functionality

However, although the Petitioner contributed to this software program, we determine that he was

national or international level. See Strategati, LLC v. Sessions, No. 18-cv-1200, 2019 WL 2330181, 
at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 31, 2019) (affirming USCIS' statement that "the phrase 'contributions of major 
significance in the field' requires substantial influence beyond one's employers, clients, or 
customers.") Moreover, while the Petitioner is listed as one of the inventors, there are approximately 
20 other inventors listed on the patent application. This undercuts the Petitioner's claim that he is in 
a small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, as his positioning in this patent application 
indicates that his contributions were commensurate with, rather than exceeding, those of his 
collaborators on this patent application. Moreover, a patent application is not necessarily evidence 
of whether the wider field considers the Petitioner's contribution to be of major significance. The 
significance of innovation is determined by USCIS on a case-by-case basis. Furthermore, the United 
States Patent and Trademark Office has consistently approved over 300,000 patent applications 
annually in last decade. Thus, the mere possession of a patent does not automatically confer 
significance ofthe invention in the field. As a general matter, the Petitioner did not show that his work 
has garnered wide attention in his field indicative of being at the very top of his field of endeavor. 
In considering the totality of the evidence, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements 
are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. 1990 
U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 6739. Furthermore, the Petitioner has not shown that he has risen to that small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3). The commentary for the proposed regulations implementing section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of 
the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary 
ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive 
documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 FR 30703-01, 30704. Here, the 
Petitioner has not established that the Petitioner's career meets this very high standard. 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought.2 Here, the Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification 
intended for individuals already at the top of their respective fields. Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment); 
Onaghise v. Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. 
Sept. 2, 2022) (holding that extraordinary ability visas are known as "Einstein" visas, are extremely 
"coveted," and are "reserved for only the most talented" aliens"); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 
8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *1 (D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visa are intended to be 
available to only an exceedingly small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, 
the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained 
national or international acclaim or it is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See 
section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. While the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. Therefore, the appeal will be dismissed and the petition will remain denied.
