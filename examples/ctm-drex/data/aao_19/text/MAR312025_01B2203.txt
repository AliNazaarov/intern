Appeal of Texas Service Center Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an entrepreneur in the digital exhibition field, requests classification under the 
employment-based, first-preference (EB- I) immigrant visa category as an alien of "extraordinary 
ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b )(I )(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this category must 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document recognition of 
their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )( 1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
The Director of the Texas Service Center denied the petition. The Director found that the Petitioner 
met the requested category's initial evidentiary requirements. But the Director ultimately concluded 
that the Petitioner did not demonstrate her claimed extraordinary ability in her field. On appeal, the 
Petitioner contends that the Director's decision contradicts U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) policy, disregards and misinterprets evidence, and introduces novel requirements. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating eligibility for the requested benefit by a 
preponderance of the evidence. Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). 
Exercising de novo appellate review, see Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 
2015), we agree with the Director that the Petitioner's evidence does not place her among the small 
percentage at her field's very top. We will therefore dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the country. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
received "a major, international recognized award," they must satisfy at least three of ten lesser 
evidentiary criteria. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 1 
If a petitioner meets either evidentiary standard and the requirements at section 203(b )(I)( A )(ii), (iii) 
of the Act, USCIS must then make a final merits determination. To merit approval, the record- as a 
whole - must establish a petitioner's "sustained national or international acclaim" and recognized 
achievements placing them among the small percentage at their field's very top. See Amin v. 
Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner and Her Field 
The record shows that the Petitioner, a Chinese native and citizen, earned a bachelor's degree in 
broadcasting/hosting art and a master's degree in art. She initially worked as a model, ambassador, 
hostess, and planner before focusing on researching culture and art. She gained experience in art 
display design and exhibition planning beginning in 2016 as director of the artistic display department 
at a Chinese art consultancy. In 2022, she became art director of a new digital art museum in China. 
In this role, she leads the planning, design, and organization of major exhibitions. 
Fluent in both Mandarin and English, the Petitioner states that she wants to continue working in her 
field in the United States, where she says the art industry faces financial and employment challenges. 
She plans to operate her own digital art exhibition design and planning company, which she registered 
before the petition's filing. The Petitioner states that integrating digital technologies- such as artificial 
intelligence and augmented, virtual, and extended reality - with classic and modem art will help to 
popularize art and culturally educate people. 
The record does not indicate - nor does the Petitioner claim - her receipt of a major, internationally 
recognized award. She therefore had to meet at least three of the ten evidentiary requirements listed 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i-x). 
The record supports the Director's findings that the Petitioner met the following six evidentiary 
requirements: 
• Membership in associations in her field requiring outstanding achievements; 
• Published material about her and her work; 
• Participation as a judge of others' work in her field; 
• Authorship of scholarly articles in the field; 
• Performance in a leading or critical role for organizations with distinguished reputations; and 
• Commandment of a high salary or significantly high remuneration relative to others in the 
field.

B. The Final Merits Determination 
To receive final petition approval, the Petitioner must demonstrate that she has sustained national or 
international acclaim and that her achievements have been recognized in her field so as to identify her 
as one of that small percentage who has risen to the field's very top. See generalZv 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual F .2(8)(2). users must consider any potentially relevant evidence of record, even if it does 
not fit one of the evidentiary criteria or was not presented as comparable evidence. Id. 
The Petitioner bears the burden of explaining the significance ofher evidence and how it demonstrates 
her receipt of sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in her field. Id. The 
petition's approval or denial should hinge on the evidence's type and quality. Id. 
The Director found insufficient evidence of the Petitioner's claimed acclaim or recognition of her 
achievements in her field. On appeal, she contends that the Director's analysis contained various 
errors. 
First, the Petitioner asserts that the Director violated users policy by finding insufficient evidence of 
her claimed national or international acclaim "because her career only spans ten (10) years." She notes 
that users policy states: 
In determining whether the [petitioner] has enjoyed "sustained" national or 
international acclaim, the officer should consider that such acclaim must be 
maintained. However, the term sustained does not imply an age limit on the 
[petitioner]. A [petitioner] may be very young or early in his or her career and still be 
able to show sustained acclaim. There is also no definitive time frame on what 
constitutes sustained. 
6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(A)(l ). 
The record, however, does not establish that the Director faulted the length of the Petitioner's career. 
Discussing published material about the Petitioner, the Director stated that "the record indicates all 
articles appeared in 2024 despite the petitioner having worked in the field for many years."2 Thus, the 
Director did not question the length of the Petitioner's career, but rather whether she has "sustained" 
acclaim after apparently receiving it only shortly before the petition's filing in August 2024. 
The Petitioner also criticizes the Director's finding of insufficient evidence of her claimed status 
among the small percentage at her field's top. She asserts that she submitted "clear and convincing

29.

proof that she is considered to be a leader and a pioneer in a specialized industry that integrates art and 
digital technology to design and present exhibitions for education and public appreciation." She states 
that her original statement and her affidavit in her response to the Director's notice of intent to deny 
(NOID) the petition "definitively detailed" her place among the small percentage at her field's top. In 
the affidavit, for example, the Petitioner stated: "Even at my relatively young age, I am widely known 
as one of the few pioneers and experts who are leading the innovation path in this field." 
But the Petitioner's own statements are insufficient to demonstrate her claimed extraordinary ability 
in her field. The record must include independent, objective evidence corroborating her statements 
and showing how others in the field regard her. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("[S]tatements 
made by the witnesses should be corroborated by documentary evidence in the record.") 
As expert testimony, the Petitioner submitted a letter from a director of a Chinese university's cultural 
relics and museum department. He similarly stated that the Petitioner "pioneers" in applying advanced 
technologies and design concepts to create exhibitions and that she has "distinguish[ ed] herself from 
other professionals by emphasizing innovation." But the letter does not identify the basis of the 
author's knowledge of the Petitioner or provide specific information or documentary evidence 
supporting his statements. Support letters "should explain in specific terms" why witnesses believe a 
petitioner has extraordinary ability. See 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3) ("Letters that ... make 
general and expansive statements regarding the [petitioner] and [her] accomplishments are generally 
not persuasive.") 
The Petitioner submitted insufficient evidence that she is among the small percentage at her field's 
top. A September 2023 online article describes the museum where she works as one of the first 24 
"immersive cultural tourism experiences" in China. Another online article states that the museum's 
parent company, a publicly traded Chinese technology firm, plans to build more digital art museums 
in the country. The record, however, does not discuss the directors of the other digital exhibition 
projects or how their acclaim and recognition compare to the Petitioner's. 
The Petitioner contends that the Director did not give enough evidentiary weight to the letters from 
the expert and her employer because the Director "did not fully understand [her] new field." She states 
that the letters discuss her leadership role at the museum that employs her. The letters, for example, 
state that she "has played a leading role in making [the museum] an industry leader with a 
distinguished reputation" and "has played a critical role in making [the museum] one of the most 
prestigious digital art Centers in China." 
As previously indicated, support letters should provide specific information corroborated by 
documentary evidence. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3). The letters from the 
Petitioner's expert and employer demonstrate her performance in a leading role at the museum. But 
the letters lack specific and corroborating evidence sufficient to identify her as among the small 
percentage at her field's very top. As previously indicated, the record does not discuss other digital 
art museum directors or how their acclaim and recognition compare to the Petitioner's. 
The Director faulted the Petitioner for omitting evidence detailing "how, exactly, you have influenced 
others in your field at least on a national scale by serving in the roles described." Noting that no 
4 
regulation requires her to produce evidence describing how she has influenced others in her field, she 
asserts that the Director's "novel requirement" violates USCIS policy and federal case law. 
We recognize that USCIS may not "unilaterally impose novel substantive or evidentiary requirements 
beyond those set forth at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5." Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010). 
But the Director did not do so. The regulations at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5 do not address final merits 
determinations. Rather, USCIS adopted the procedure for determining extraordinary ability from the 
Kazarian case. Id. at 1119-20 ("If a petitioner has submitted the requisite evidence, USCIS determines 
whether the evidence demonstrates both a 'level of expertise indicating that the individual is one of 
that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the[ir] field of endeavor,' . . . and 'that the 
alien has sustained national or international acclaim and that his or her achievements have been 
recognized in the field of expertise.'") Also, as previously indicated, USCIS policy states that "the 
petitioner must explain the significance of the submitted evidence, and how it demonstrates that the 
person has achieved sustained national or international acclaim and recognition in their field of 
expertise." 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2). Thus, the Director did not violate USCIS policy or 
federal case law by requiring detailed evidence of the Petitioner's claimed influence on her field. See 
also section 203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act (requiring "extensive documentation" of an alien's 
extraordinary ability). 
The Director also faulted the Petitioner for providing insufficient evidence that she has consistently 
participated as a judge of others' work in her field. She notes that there is no provision requiring her 
participation in "a substantial number" of judging activities. She asserts that the "contrived novel 
requirement is unconstitutionally vague and therefore violates federal law." 
The Petitioner misapplies the vagueness doctrine, which relates to laws and regulations. It does not 
apply to decision findings. Even if the Petitioner had challenged a law or regulation for vagueness, 
we would lack authority to declare a statute or regulation unconstitutional. See, e.g., Matter of C-, 
20 I&N Dec. 529,532 (BIA 1992). 
The Petitioner contends that the Director's finding regarding published materials about her "makes no 
sense" and lacks evidentiary support. The Director found that "the petitioner did not demonstrate that 
the limited media reporting [ about her] reflects a 'career of acclaimed work in the field."' 
The phrase a "career of acclaimed work in the field" stems from legislative history of the Immigration 
Act of 1990, which implemented this immigrant visa category. The House of Representatives' report 
on the law regarding aliens of extraordinary ability states: "Recognition can be through a one-time 
achievement such as receipt of the Nobel Prize. An alien can also qualify on the basis of a career of 
acclaimed work in the field." H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, pt. 1, at 59 (1990). Where a petitioner 
participated in judging activities since only 2018, a federal court upheld USCIS' finding that the 
activities did not reflect "a career of acclaimed work in the field." Jafarov v. USCIS, No. 23 CIV. 
3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *10 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024). We therefore find that the Director 
appropriately analyzed published materials about the Petitioner on the same basis. 
The Petitioner also cites a letter from an organizer of an event at which the Petitioner judged others' 
work in the field. The letter states that she: 
5 
has been regarded as a top expert in successfully integrating advanced digital 
technologies with art display. Her accomplishments and significant influence in 
establishing the first large digital art museum in China has been highly acclaimed in 
the art industry all over the country. 
But the letter does not explain why she is considered to be above other experts or what acclaim she 
has received from the Chinese art industry. The evidence is insufficient to conclude that the Petitioner 
is among the small percentage at her field's very top. 
The Petitioner contends that the Director erred in finding insufficient evidence of the "significance" 
of her scholarly articles. She argues that no provision requires her to show her articles' significance. 
True. But, without evidence of their significance, the Petitioner's articles do not tend to indicate her 
claimed extraordinary ability. She has not shown that others in her field have cited to her work or 
implemented her ideas. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(2) (stating that a petitioner's 
relatively high number of citations "may indicate a person's high overall standing for the purpose of 
demonstrating that the person is among the small percentage at the top of the field"). 
The Petitioner states that the Director's decision "completely ignores the fact that [the] Petitioner's 
memberships involved appointed leadership positions that required much higher admissions criteria 
than the implied general admissions." But the record does not support that claim. Copies of 
organizations' regulation, by-law, and charter do not sufficiently detail any higher admissions criteria 
for leadership positions. 
The Petitioner notes that, when discussing her judging of others' work in her field, the Director 
misunderstood evidence. A letter from a committee director of a digital art exhibition states: "Based 
on their expertise and professional experience, the judge panel provided reviews and comments." The 
Director misinterpreted the statement to mean that expertise and professional experience constituted 
the selection criteria for the judges. 
This error, however, is immaterial. See Chavez-Chilel v. Att'y Gen. United States, 20 F.4th 138, 144 
(3d Cir. 2021) (citations omitted) (stating that an error is harmless "when it is highly probable that [it] 
did not affect the outcome of the case"); see generally Matter ofO-R-E-, 28 I&N Dec. 330, 336 n.5 
(BIA 2021) ( citing cases regarding harmless or scrivener's errors). 
Considering the evidence in its totality, the Petitioner has not demonstrated sufficient acclaim or 
recognition to establish extraordinary ability. The record lacks sufficient details to identify her as one 
of the small percentage at her field's very top. See Amin, 24 F.4th at 386 ("[W]e cannot say that the 
agency acted arbitrarily when it determined that [the petitioner] was not 'extraordinary' but merely 
very good.") 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
