Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a business development manager, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. § 1153(b )(l)(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition. SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner 
satisfied the initial evidence requirement for this classification by meeting at least three of the 
evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). However, SCOPS determined the Petitioner did not 
establish his sustained national or international acclaim and that he is one of that small percentage at 
the very top of his field. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3 . 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish: 
• They have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary 
ability; and 
• Their entry will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation farther states that the term "extraordinary ability" means "a level of 
expertise indicating that the individual is one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top 
of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). A petitioner can demonstrate that they meet the 
initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification through evidence of a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). If such 
evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of the 
ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that they possess 
the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa classification. See 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the 
documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the 
context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022).

1. Membership 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) requires "[d]ocumentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of 
their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met this criterion based on evidence that he was admitted as a 
fellow of the United Kingdom's Chartered Institute of Marketing (CIM) in 2025. We will withdraw 
SCOPS' determination. 
The Petitioner submitted an excerpt from the CIM's public website stating that the fellow level of 
membership is for "marketers with significant senior strategic experience." The Petitioner also 
provided an excerpt from the CIM's constitution and by-laws, which indicate there are six different 
combinations of qualifications and experience which may qualify a marketing professional for fellow 
membership. He highlighted the following text: 
v. provide evidence of 15 years' experience at a sustained senior level dealing with 
strategic marketing issues (general or specialist) including ten years at, or close, to 
Board level influencing organizational direction and decision making. He/she must 
demonstrate authority, innovation and professional integrity, applying advanced and 
specialist marketing skills and techniques, including syntheses and evaluation to solve 
complex problems which help redefine existing professional practice and its interface 
with other functional areas, which implies knowledge at the advanced frontiers of 
marketing. 
In evaluating whether CIM requires its fellow members to demonstrate "outstanding achievements," 
we acknowledge that an association's bylaws or other documentation of membership requirements 
need not parrot this regulatory language to establish eligibility under this criterion. We also 
acknowledge that certain organizations may require outstanding achievements of their fellow 
members. See generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(8)(1), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual 
(discussing how USCIS evaluates evidence submitted in support of the criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii)). Examples of membership requirements that may meet the "outstanding 
achievements" element of this criterion, while not exhaustive, include accomplishments that have 
"contributed importantly" to the advancement of a field or demonstrate "significant, sustained 
contributions" to a field. See generally id. Memberships in associations that base membership on 
possession of qualifications such as a level of education or years of experience in a particular field 
generally will not qualify. Id. 
Here, the Petitioner stated that CIM' s "fellowship status is reserved for an elite minority of senior 
professionals who have demonstrated long-term field-defining impact." However, he also appears to 
indicate that he qualified for his CIM fellow membership based on the criteria quoted above, which 
requires "sustained senior level experience," demonstrating "authority, innovation and professional 
integrity," exercising influence within an organization, and possession of "advanced marketing skills 
and techniques." These requirements do not appear to equate to "long-term field defining impact." 
3 
Even if we determined that possession of advanced skills, organizational decision-making authority, 
professional integrity, and 15 years of senior level employment in the field2 can be equated to 
"outstanding achievements," we note the record indicates there are multiple ways in which a person 
may establish eligibility for fellow membership. For example, some of the admission criteria 
applicable to fellows, as outlined in the CIM's by-laws, are based on obtaining the CIM's own 
"Chartered Marketer" credential, possessing a combination of significant experience in the field and 
a regular membership in CIM, or by possessing a suitable "professional, vocational or academic 
qualification" and holding a professor position. This evidence does not demonstrate that "outstanding 
achievements" are required as an essential condition for fellow membership in the CIM. 
To demonstrate that his membership satisfies the plain language of this criterion, the Petitioner must 
also demonstrate that CIM only admits as fellows those whose achievements have been judged as 
outstanding by "national or international experts in their fields." Here, the record does not contain 
sufficient evidence to demonstrate that this element of the criterion has been met. 
The Petitioner asserted that CIM's selection process "involves rigorous peer review and is not 
achievable through application alone, but only by invitation or nomination after institutional vetting." 
These processes are not documented in the record. It appears based on the submitted materials that an 
application is required for membership, but the evidence in the record does not describe the application 
process, any supporting documentation required, how applications are evaluated, or who evaluates 
them, other than noting that persons applying for membership at any level are ultimately admitted by 
"the Board."3 Without such information, we cannot evaluate whether CIM's fellow membership 
applications are judged by "recognized national or international experts" in the field. 
Based upon the foregoing analysis of the record, we disagree with and withdraw SCOPS' conclusion 
that the Petitioner meets this criterion.

3. High Salary or other Significantly High Remuneration 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix) requires "[e]vidence that the alien has commanded a high

salary data such as geographical and position-appropriate compensation surveys. See generally 6 
USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). We will withdraw SCOPS's determination that the 
Petitioner satisfied this criterion.

To support his claim that his earnings are high or significantly high in relation to others in the field,

However, the Petitioner seeks to compare his claimed dividend payments (which are based on his

Because the comparative government- and industry- sourced data provides information regarding base 
salaries paid to general managers, it is appropriate to compare the Petitioner's base salary to this data. 
As noted, the Petitioner's partial-year, bi-weekly salary payments, reflected as payroll deposits in his 
bank statements, equate to an annual salary of $91,000. This amount is below the mean or median 
wage reported in all but one of the submitted sources. The Petitioner's annual salary is higher than 
the statewide average of $73,643 reported by Indeed, but well below the high salary of $179,365 
reported by that source. Overall, the evidence provided does not establish that the Petitioner earns a 
high salary in relation to others. 
Even if the Petitioner had articulated an alternative claim that he has commanded "other significantly 
high remuneration for services" in relation to others, the Petitioner has not provided sufficient 
objective corroborating his receipt of the 2024 dividend payment, has not shown that he received such 
payment as "remuneration for services," and has not provided an adequate basis for comparing his 
dividend earnings to those of other business owners in his field. 
Therefore, based upon the foregoing analysis of the record, we withdraw SCOPS' determination that 
the Petitioner satisfied the criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix).

B. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we analyze an individual's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if their achievements are sufficient to establish sustained national or 
international acclaim and that they are one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 
F.3d at 1119-20. 8 Here, the Petitioner has not offered sufficient evidence that he meets that standard. 
As a preliminary matter, we acknowledge the Petitioner's contention that SCOPS erred because it 
"denied the petition based on final merits grounds that were never raised in the [ request for evidence 
(RFE)]" and "issued an adverse decision on a basis for which the Petitioner had no prior notice or 
opportunity to respond." However, pages 8 and 9 of the RFE specifically address the final merits 
determination and advised that "this is the petitioner's opportunity to articulate further details or 
provide additional evidence submitted in the initial filing or in response to the [RFE] establishes that 
the [petitioner] meets the requirements regarding the required high level of expertise for the immigrant 
classification." 
In the denial notice, SCOPS concluded that the evidence does not establish sustained national or 
international acclaim. While much of the discussion in SCOPS' final merits determination is couched 
in terms referring to the initial criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), SCOPS also addressed the 
broader issue of sustained national or international acclaim and the lack of independent corroboration,

The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above, does not establish the Petitioner's 
eligibility for the benefit sought. Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary 
ability by requiring that they demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present 
"extensive documentation" to show their achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act. This highly restrictive visa classification is intended for individuals at the 
top of their respective fields. See Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 
1994)( concluding that even major league level athletes do not automatically meet the statutory 
standards for classification as an individual of "extraordinary ability"); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 
383, 394 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that extraordinary ability is such an elite level of accomplishment). 
Here, the Petitioner's claims regarding his achievements and recognitions, particularly as they relate 
to his career prior to 2023, are not extensively documented in the record. Considering the foll measure 
of the Petitioner's career and documented achievements in his field, he has not established that the 
significance of his work is indicative of the required national or international acclaim or is consistent 
with a career of acclaimed work in his field. Further, he has not demonstrated that his achievements, 
to the extent they are supported in the record, place him amongst the small percentage at the top of his 
field.
