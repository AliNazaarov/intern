Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an administrative services and facilities manager, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of the ten initial evidentiary criteria for this classification, 
as required. We dismissed a subsequent appeal but reopened the matter in response to litigation. 
Following the Petitioner's response to our motion, the matter is again before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b)(l) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 20 l0) ( discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022).

A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner did not meet any of the five 
evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) for which she submitted supporting evidence: 
• (i) Documentation of the alien's receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized 
prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor; 
• (ii) Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which 
classification is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged 
by recognized national or international experts in their disciplines or fields; 
• (iv) Evidence of the alien's participation, either individually or on a panel, as a judge of the 
work ofothers in the same or an allied field of specialization for which classification is sought; 
• (v) Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related 
contributions of major significance in the field, and; 
• (viii) Evidence that the alien has performed in a leading or critical role for organizations or 
establishments that have a distinguished reputation.

On appeal, the Petitioner claims to meet only three ofthese criteria, pertaining to awards, judging, and 
leading or critical role. 1 We previously determined that she did not establish that she met the awards

1. Evidence of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt of lesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." We 
first determine whether the alien is the recipient of prizes or awards, and then whether the award is 
nationally or internationally recognized and was granted based upon excellence in the alien's field of 
endeavor. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner submitted evidence of several certificates she received from three previous employers, 
all of which are related to her employment a~ lfrom 2007 to 2018. One from 
each employer is titled "Certificate of Appreciation," and another two from A- Europe state that they 
were granted for "exceptional performance of duties." As she did on appeal, the Petitioner asserts that 
these certificates, given to her for her job performance, were granted by "well-known national 
institutions," citing to USCIS guidance renarding this criterion. Id. She relies upon these employers' 
association with thel . in her effort to establish that these certificates are nationally 
or internationally recognized prizes or awards. But while an award from a "well-known national 
institution" is one example of a potentially qualifying award, the focus on the first element of this 
evidentiary criterion is on the recognition of the award, not the organization which issued the award. 
Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F.Supp.3d 168, 183 (D.N.J. 2021). The record does not include evidence 
showing that any of these certificates were recognized in the field of administrative serv1ces 
management at the national or international level, beyond the issuing organizations. 2 
The plain language of this evidentiary criterion also requires that prizes or awards be granted "for 
excellence in the field of endeavor." Factors such as the criteria applied in granting a prize or award 
and the number of awardees or recipients are relevant to determining whether it has been granted for 
excellence. Id. As we pointed out in our appeal decision, the Petitioner did not provide any 
information about these certificates which might show that they were given to her for excellence in 
administrative services management, and she does not address this deficiency in response to our 
motion. The text ofthe certificates indicates only that she performed her job duties well, and the many 
reference letters from the Petitioner's former supervisors and colleagues do not provide additional 
information regarding her receipt of the certificates.

2. Participation as a Judge of the Work of Others in the Field 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iv) requires "[e]vidence of the alien's participation, either 
individually or on a panel, as a judge of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization for which classification is sought." The alien must show that they not only have been 
invited to participate as a judge of the work of others in their field, but also that they have actually 
participated in such judging. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
The Petitioner bases her claim on her work for her employers at Bagram Air Base. In response to our 
motion, she describes two positions in which she asserts that her duties involved judging the work of 
others in her field or an allied field. She states that while employed by S-S-S-, she "managed over 
275 security personnel" and "confirm[ ed] their qualifications" as guards. A reference letter from this 
employer confirms that she was employed as a human resources administrator for approximately six 
months. Another reference letter from her former supervisor at this employer provides more detail 
regarding her duties, which included hiring, vetting, badging, and payroll for the security force. While 
this evidence shows that she performed duties related to administrative services in this position, it does 
not indicate that she reviewed the work performance of others in that field or an allied field. 
Similarly, the record includes several letters regarding her work with M-E-, in which she states that 
she "managed the Local National Linguist Program" and evaluated linguists for their "linguistic and 
operational suitability for mission deployment." She refers to a letter from M-M- who writes that she 
served as a hiring authority for M-E- and submitted "daily reporting documentation ... as part of the 
vetting requirements" for those employed with M-E-. Another letter, submitted with her appeal, states 
that the Petitioner was responsible for "hiring and signing on of new linguist[ s ]" and ensuring that 
requests for linguists were met "with a well-qualified linguist for the needs of the Mission." As with 
her duties with S-S-S- discussed above, these letters do not indicate that the Petitioner was responsible 
for judging the work of others in the administrative services field. Rather, she evaluated the 
qualifications and suitability of linguists. 
In another letter from a former colleague at M-E-, D-C- states that he supervised the Petitioner for four 
years. He writes that as the supervisor of operations, she had five subordinates and was responsible 
for maintaining the operations records for over 20,000 personnel. But the letter does not indicate that 
the Petitioner served as a judge of her subordinates' work or discuss any specific instances when such 
judging might have occurred. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not established that she has met the requirements 
of this evidentiary criterion. 
3. Performed in a Leading or Critical Role for Organizations with a Distinguished Reputation 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) calls for "[e]vidence that the alien has performed in a 
leading or critical role for organizations or establishments that have a distinguished reputation." The 
4 
role may be shown to be leading or critical for a division or department of an organization or 
establishment, or for the organization or establishment overall. Evidence ofa leading role should show 
that the alien was a leader for the claimed component, and a job title with matching duties may help 
to establish that a role was leading. For a critical role, evidence should show that the alien has 
contributed in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or 
establishment's activities or those of a division or department of the organization or establishment. In 
determining whether the organization or establishment, or a department or division thereof, has a 
distinguished reputation, relevant factors include its size, longevity, scale of its customer base, and 
relevant media coverage. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l). 
Here, the Petitioner contends that her former roles with A- and M-E- qualify as leading or critical, and 
that both of those organizations have distinguished reputations. She also asserts that SCOPS erred in 
not specifically evaluating all ofthe reference letters pertaining to these roles in its decision. But when 
USCIS provides a reasoned consideration to the petition, and has made adequate findings, it will not

Letters of reference from those with personal knowledge of a petitioner's duties or responsibilities in

B. Final Merits Determination 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Because the Petitioner has not established that she meets any of the evidentiary criteria under 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) and, therefore, has not met the "initial evidence" requirement for the requested 
classification, we need not conduct a final merits determination. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 
1119-20. Nevertheless, we have reviewed the totality of the evidence in the record and determined 
that she has not established the requisite level of sustained acclaim and standing within her field to 
show eligibility for the requested benefit.

As stated above, the Act requires "extensive documentation" to show recognition of an alien's 
achievements in the field. In response to our motion, as she did on appeal, the Petitioner relies nearly 
exclusively on the reference letters in the record in asserting her eligibility as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. While we have given these reference letters due consideration, such letters should not form 
the cornerstone of a successful claim under this immigrant visa classification and should be 
corroborated by documentary evidence to verify a petitioner's accomplishments and put them in a 
broader context. See generally 6 USC IS Policy Manual F .2(8)( 1 ). Here, the record does not include 
substantial evidence beyond these reference letters and is therefore insufficient to show recognition of 
the Petitioner's achievements in her field or that she is one of the small percentage at the very top of 
that field.
