Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a composer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See Immigration 
and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
SCOPS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner had either 
received a major, internationally recognized award or satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary 
criteria, as required. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with 
the following analysis. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability 
in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation. A petitioner must establish that these aliens seek to enter the United States 
to continue work in the area of extraordinary ability, and that their entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit the United States. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those aliens in 
"that small percentage who have risen to the very top ofthe field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a 
petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of an alien's achievements in the field through a 
one-time achievement in the form of a major, internationally recognized award. Or the petitioner can 
submit evidence that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), 
including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles. If those 
standards do not readily apply to the alien's occupation, then the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) 
allows the submission of comparable evidence. 
Once a pet1t10ner has met the initial evidence requirements, the next step is a final merits 
determination, in which we assess whether the record shows sustained national or international 
acclaim and demonstrates that the alien is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where 
the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in 
the context of a final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 
(D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
The Petitioner claimed to have satisfied all ten of these criteria, pertaining to (i) lesser nationally or 
internationally recognized prizes or awards; (ii) membership in associations that require outstanding 
achievements; (iii) published material about the alien in professional or major media; (iv) participation 
as a judge of the work of others; (v) original contributions of major significance; (vi) authorship of 
scholarly articles; (vii) display at artistic exhibitions or showcases; (viii) leading or critical roles for 
distinguished organizations or establishments; (ix) high remuneration for services; and (x) commercial 
success in the performing arts. 
SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner met only two of the criteria, pertaining to judging and authorship 
of scholarly articles. On appeal, the Petitioner asserts that he also meets nearly all the other criteria, 
except for the ninth criterion relating to remuneration, and that he has submitted comparable evidence 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
In the denial notice, SCOPS concluded that the Petitioner submitted evidence relating to two different 
fields, specifically musical composition and music education. SCOPS stated: "The petitioner 
indicated he intended to continue work as a composer in the field of the arts rather than as a music 
educator in the field of education. As such, the evidence regarding the petitioner's work as a music 
educator was not considered when evaluating his ability." SCOPS added: "USCIS will only consider 
his work as a music educator under final merits." But because SCOPS granted only two ofthe claimed 
initial criteria, SCOPS did not undertake a final merits determination. 
On appeal, the Petitioner argues that he "has claimed one field of expertise and that is Music 
Composition," and that his educational activities are part o±: and compatible with, that field. The 
record is consistent with this claim, showing, for instance, that the Petitioner has composed several 
original pieces for his educational books and an app that he co-developed. We therefore conclude that 
the Petitioner's various activities are sufficiently related that SCOPS should have considered the 
Petitioner's educational work in the context of the initial evidentiary criteria.

Because the Petitioner has submitted the required initial evidence to satisfy three of the criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), the next step is to determine whether he has demonstrated, by a preponderance 
of the evidence, his sustained national or international acclaim; that he is one of the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor; and that his achievements have been recognized in the field 
through extensive documentation. A final merits determination involves analyzing an alien's 
accomplishments and weighing the totality ofthe evidence to determine iftheir successes are sufficient 
to demonstrate extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20, and see generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(2). 
We will remand the matter in order for SCOPS to make a final merits determination. In doing so, 
SCOPS must bear in mind that the determination involves consideration of the totality of the record, 
rather than simply re-evaluating the evidence submitted regarding the individual underlying criteria. 
SCOPS must determine whether the record as a whole shows that the Petitioner has achieved sustained 
national or international acclaim. 
The prior approval of an 0-1 petition is a relevant consideration and can be an indicator of eligibility 
in adjudicating an immigrant petition for a person with extraordinary ability, but it is not determinative. 
See, generally, 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(3), https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. If SCOPS' 
final merits determination results in another denial, then SCOPS should briefly explain why, 
notwithstanding the previous 0-1 nonimmigrant visa petition approval, the Petitioner has not met his 
burden to establish eligibility for immigrant classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. Id. 
Because the Petitioner's intended future work pertains to film and television scores rather than educational 
products, it is appropriate for SCOPS to give more weight to the evidence of acclaim and recognition 
pertaining to the Petitioner's past film and television work relative to his educational endeavors. 
The determining factor is the Petitioner's own acclaim and recognition, rather than the reputations of 
his employers, associates, projects, and organizations to which he belongs. 
We will remand the matter to SCOPS for a final merits determination. 
ORDER: SCOPS' decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
