Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is a soccer coach and former player who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S .C. 
§ l 153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation 
USCIS denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish that the Petitioner met the 
initial evidence requirements for this classification by meeting at least three of the evidentiary criteria 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) or through evidence of a one-time achievement (a major, internationally 
recognized award). The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To establish eligibility as an individual of extraordinary ability, a petitioner (or anyone on the 
petitioner's behalf) must establish that they: 
• Have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• Seek to enter the United States to continue work in their area of extraordinary ability; and that 
• Their entry into the United States will prospectively substantially benefit the United States. 
Extraordinary ability must be demonstrated by evidence of sustained national or international acclaim 
as well as extensive documentation that the petitioner's achievements have been recognized in the 
field. Section 203(b )(1) of the Act. 
The implementing regulation further states that the term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those 
aliens in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." A petitioner 
can demonstrate that they meet the initial evidence requirements for this immigrant visa classification 
through evidence of a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If 
such evidence is unavailable, then they must alternatively provide evidence that meets at least three of 
the ten listed criteria, which call for evidence about lesser awards they may have received, published 
material about them in qualifying media, and their authorship of scholarly articles, among other types 
of evidence. 8 C.F.R. §§ 204.5(h)(2),(3). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination, assessing whether the record shows that the alien 
possesses the acclaim and recognition required for this highly exclusive immigrant visa 
classification. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review 
where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, 
considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 
394 (5th. Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. The Petitioner's Field of Expertise 
In Part 5 of the petition, the Petitioner listed his occupation as "Soccer Coach" and referenced his 
supporting cover letter at Part 6 of the petition, which asks the Petitioner's job title. The supporting 
cover letter describes the Petitioner as "a remarkable soccer talent, coach, and social innovator" and 
further states that he has transitioned to "coaching and management" and "is committed to mentoring 
soccer players and teaching sports, activities that provide tangible contributions to U.S. society." 1 The 
Petitioner discussed the "critical leadership positions" he has held since transitioning away from 
playing soccer competitively more than ten years ago, listing his roles supervising a soccer academy 
and "serving as Executive Director" of a non-profit organization where his duties included overseeing 
"multi-site program operations," coaching and mentoring staff: evaluating national tournaments, and 
developing "original sport-for-development initiatives now recognized internationally." 
A second letter was submitted in response to a request for evidence (RFE) and offers further 
information about the Petitioner's role as executive director at a non-profit organization, where he 
"personally conceived, designed, and executed soccer-based development programs that integrate elite 
training with documented social-impact objectives" and where his primary focus was on "program 
strategy, implementation, and outcomes." However, despite having been retired from his career as a 
soccer player for over a decade, the Petitioner relied on evidence relating to his career as a soccer 
player to demonstrate that he met several ofthe evidentiary criteria which are discussed in the analysis 
below. For example, on appeal the Petitioner challenges USCIS' decision regarding the evidentiary 
criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i), noting that the decision "appears to evaluate the awards criterion 
primarily through the lens of the [Petitioner]' s coaching career." The Petitioner asserts that the field 
of endeavor rather than "a single professional role or stage of a career" should be the reference point 
for determining whether the Petitioner is an individual of extraordinary ability. We disagree.

It is reasonable to interpret continuing to work in one's "area of extraordinary ability" 
as working in the same profession in which one has extraordinary ability, not 
necessarily in any profession in that field. For example, Lee's extraordinary ability as

or professions in the baseball industry such as a manager, umpire or coach.

B. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he received a major, internationally 
recognized award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met two of the evidentiary criteria 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x), relating to his judging the work of others in the same or allied field 
and holding a leading or critical role for organizations with a distinguished reputation. On appeal, the 
Petitioner asserts that he also meets the evidentiary criteria3 relating to: 
• Receipt of lesser nationally or internationally recognized awards; 
• Published material about him and his work in the field in a qualifying publication; and 
• Original contributions of major significance in the field. 
After reviewing the evidence in the record, we conclude that the Petitioner has not established that he 
meets at least three ofthe evidentiary criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) and therefore does not meet 
the initial evidence requirements for classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. 
1. Evidence of Lesser Nationally or Internationally Recognized Prizes or Awards 
The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) calls for "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt oflesser 
nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for excellence in the field of endeavor." In 
determining eligibility for this criterion, we look to the plain language of the regulation at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i), requiring "[d]ocumentation of the alien's receipt" of prizes or awards. In addition, 
we consider the term "alien's receipt" using its ordinary, common meaning. See, e.g., Perrin v. United 
States, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979) ("A fundamental canon of statutory construction is that, unless 
otherwise defined, words will be interpreted as taking their ordinary, contemporary, common meaning 
....") Moreover, the description of this type of evidence in the regulation indicates that the focus 
should be on the person's receipt of the awards or prizes, as opposed to the employer's receipt of the 
awards or prizes. 4 Thus, prizes or awards not received by or bestowed upon the Petitioner do not 
satisfy this regulatory criterion. 
In the initial supporting statement, the Petitioner claimed that he has "demonstrated outstanding 
achievements as a coach, earning recognition for his leadership and success across multiple youth 
soccer categories." Evidence of the Petitioner's prior coaching achievements consists of a photograph 
of trophies with handwritten notes on the photograph stating that the trophies were awarded by the 
for wins in the U9, Ul 1, and Ul5 categories. The Petitioner did not provide '-----------' 
evidence that he was the recipient of these awards or even evidence that he coached the teams who 
were identified as the trophy recipients. The Petitioner must support its assertions with relevant, 
probative, and credible evidence. See Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 376 (AAO 2010).

3. Original Contributions of Major Significance 
Finally, the regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) calls for "[e]vidence of the alien's original 
scientific, scholarly, artistic, athletic, or business-related contributions of major significance in the 
field." The regulatory phrase "major significance" is not superfluous and, thus, it has some meaning. 
Nielsen v. Preap, 139 S. Ct. 954, 969 (2019) (finding that every word and every provision in a statute 
is to be given effect and none should needlessly be given an interpretation that causes it to duplicate 
another provision or to have no consequence). Contributions of major significance connotes that the 
Petitioner's work has significantly impacted the field. The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying 
all these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
Here, the Petitioner highlights his "innovative leadership as a coach and executive shaping soccer's 
social impact in Costa Rica," claiming that in his role as executive director ofthe nonprofit foundation 
I lhe "designed and implemented programs that integrate sports, education, and social 
development." In his original supporting statement, the Petitioner stressed his various achievements, 
which included obtaining additional operational fields, conducting over 2,000 training sessions, 
recruiting staff, securing sponsorships, and ensuring school enrollment of all participants; he claimed 
that he had a "pivotal role in creating programs that have transformed the lives of children and young 
people." The Petitioner pointed to an article that he wrote about the foundation and its philanthropic 
goals and discussed information provided in the foundation's annual reports which highlight the 
achievements listed above. The Petitioner did not, however, explain how his work with the nonprofit 
organization constitutes original contributions that are of major significance in his field. 
In his RFE response statement, the Petitioner stated that he "personally conceived, designed, and 
executed soccer-based development programs that integrate elite training with documented social­
impact objectives." He claimed that as executive director ofI I"he bears primary 
responsibility for program strategy, implementation, and outcomes" and credited himself with the 
foundation's "sustained and expanding program activity." However, the Petitioner did not specify his 
original contribution and instead highlighted the foundation's annual reports from 2022-2024, broadly 
claiming that the reports reflect the program's "continuity and measurable outcomes benefiting 
hundreds of children and community members in Costa Rica, demonstrating impact well beyond 
routine involvement." 
The Petitioner also provided a letter of support from D-M-, the nonprofit's founder, who praised the 
Petitioner's leadership and accomplishments, which included fundraising, strengthening "key 
partnerships," and innovating methodology by "adapt[ing] and implement[ing] a comprehensive S4D 
methodology" that incorporates "sport-based sessions, socioemotional workshops, community events, 
and family engagement." However, the Petitioner has not established that fundraising, strengthening 
partnerships, or adapting and implementing an existing methodology are original contributions. And 
while D-M- also listed three "innovative community engagement strategies" that he attributed to the 
Petitioner, the letter contains no description of the strategies to explain how they are original, nor is 
there evidence that the strategies were of major significance in the Petitioner's field. 
On appeal, the Petitioner contends that USCIS applied "a standard that is not appropriate for the field 
of professional athletics and sports development," where significant contributions "are demonstrated 
through the design and implementation of development programs, measurable outcomes in athlete

For all of the reasons discussed above, we conclude that the Petitioner does not meet this evidentiary 
criterion. 
III. CONCLUSION 
The Petitioner has not shown that he met either a one-time award, or three of ten initial criteria. The 
Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of 
their respective fields. USCIS has long held that even athletes performing at the major league level 
do not automatically meet the "extraordinary ability" standard. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 
954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). Here, the Petitioner has not shown that the significance of his work is 
indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or that it is consistent with a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise 
demonstrate that the Petitioner has garnered national or international acclaim in the field, and that he 
is one of the small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Act and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
