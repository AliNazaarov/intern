Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a data analyst, seeks classification under the employment-based, first-preference 
(EB-1) immigrant visa category as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This EB-1 classification 
makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through 
sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their 
field through extensive documentation. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at 
least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits 
determination did not show sustained national or international acclaim demonstrating that the 
Petitioner is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, a petitioner must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics;" 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one that small percentage who 
have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner can 
demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), they must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services 
(USCIS) must then consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and 
assess whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the 
individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. 
USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first 
counted and then, iffulfilling the required number ofcriteria, considered in the context ofa final merits 
determination); see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two­
step extraordinary ability analysis "consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner claims that she is "a leading expert in data analysis, data modeling, artificial intelligence 
(AI) and machine learning (ML) within the banking and insurance industries." She is a Senior Member 
of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) and lists her notable achievements to 
include "continuous employment at globally distinguished organizations," "prestigious international 
awards, memberships in elite professional organizations, and high-impact contributions to enterprise 
data architecture." 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not claimed or established her receipt of a major, internationally recognized 
award, she must satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
Before SCOPS, the Petitioner claimed she met eight of the regulatory criteria. SCOPS determined the 
Petitioner satisfied six of the claimed eight criteria, 1 but that the record did not demonstrate her 
sustained national or international acclaim and that she was one ofthe small percentage at the very top 
ofthe field ofendeavor, and that her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. 
Having met at least three criteria, the Petitioner does not discuss her eligibility for other criteria on 
appeal and only claims that SCOPS erred in the final merits determination. Before we evaluate the 
totality of evidence under Kazarian, we withdraw SCOPS' initial conclusions that the Petitioner 
satisfied the evidentiary criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) and (ix).

2. High Salary or Significantly High Remuneration 
The Petitioner also claimed that she meets the criterion of earning higher salary in relation to other 
data analysts. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). Evidence regarding whether the person's compensation is 
high relative to that of others working in the field may take many forms and examples may include, 
but are not limited to, geographical or position-appropriate compensation surveys and organizational 
justifications to pay above the compensation data. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l), Criterion 9. 
The Petitioner claimed that her position corresponds to "computer systems analyst" under Standard 
Occupation Classification (SOC) code 15-1211 2 and her 2024 salary of $125,539.76 exceeded the 
prevailing wage ofthe same position by submitting the U.S. Department ofLabor's wage data for July 
2024 through June 2025 in thd IIowal Iarea, listing the highest prevailing 
wage as $107,432 (Level IV). The Petitioner also provided printouts from CareerOneStop showing a 
median salary of $101,310 to a high salary of $138,270 for computer systems analysts and claimed 
that her total compensation for 2024 was higher as it totaled "$153,079.76, reflecting $125,539.76 in 
base salary plus $27,540 in bonus." 
However, the record does not sufficiently establish that her position corresponds to "computer systems 
analysts." USCIS records indicate that in the Petitioner's H-1 B specialty occupations petition 
approved in 2023, the Petitioner filed her labor condition application (LCA) with a position of "Data 
Warehousing Specialist" with SOC code 15-1243.01 (wage level III). Due to this inconsistent 
representation of her position, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that salary information for a 
computer systems analyst in the record is the proper basis for comparison as a data warehouse 
specialist may perform at higher level of work and receive higher salary. Here, the evidence is 
insufficient to provide a valid point of comparison. Both precedent and case law support this

B. Final Merits Determination 
After evaluating the totality of the evidence in the context of a final merits determination, we agree 
with SCOPS' conclusion that the Petitioner has not demonstrated she sustained national or 
international acclaim, 3 she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor, and 
her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation. See section 
203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20.4 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that SCOPS failed to properly conduct the final merits determination 
by using "a piecemeal approach at the final merits stage, improperly requiring each criterion to 
independently demonstrate that I had 'risen to the very top' ofthe field." In other words, the Petitioner 
contends that SCOPS' individualized discussion of each criterion in the second step of the Kazarian 
analysis is erroneous and cites to "USCIS Policy Memorandum PM-602-005.1 (December 22, 2010)" 
which states: "Adjudicators should not apply a separate 'extraordinary ability' test to the individual 
evidentiary criteria. The evaluation of extraordinary ability should take place in the final merits 
determination, where all the evidence is weighed together."5 
We find the Petitioner's argument unpersuasive. In the first step of the Kazarian framework, SCOPS 
should not yet make a determination regarding whether or not the person is one of that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field and make only limited determinations regarding 
whether the person has met the applicable evidentiary criteria. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, at F.2(B)(l). However, this does not mean that SCOPS is somehow prevented from 
individually evaluating each individual criterion in performing the Kazarian's second step analysis, 
or that in order to be holistic and cumulative, SCOPS must structure its discussion to compound all 
criteria. 
Here, SCOPS' final merits determination properly considered each individual criterion for sustained 
acclaim rising to the very top of the field and discussed relevant evidence. SCOPS thoroughly

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
