Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a risk analyst and a doctoral student in business administration, seeks classification 
under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-I) immigrant visa category as an alien with 
"extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This EB-I classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can 
demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Section 
203(b )( I )(A)(i) of the Act. 
USCIS denied the petition concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial 
evidentiary criteria, but the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination did not 
show sustained national or international acclaim, demonstrating that the Petitioner is among the small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 
8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify for this immigrant visa category, petitioners must demonstrate that they: 
• Have "extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• Seek to continue work in their field of expertise in the United States; and 
• Would substantially benefit the United States as an immigrant. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" means expertise commensurate with "one of that small percentage 
who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). Unless a petitioner 
can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award), he or she must provide sufficient 
qualifying documentation that meets at least three ofthe ten lesser evidentiary criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable 
evidence if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not 
readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
If a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, USCIS must then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,391 (5th Cir. 2022) (finding USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability analysis 
"consistent with the governing statute and regulation"); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
II. ANALYSIS 
A. Initial Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or shown that he received a major, internationally recognized 
award, he must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
USCIS concluded that the Petitioner met three criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) (having published 
materials about him), (iv) (participating as a judge of other's work) and (vi) (authoring scholarly 
articles) out of five criteria he claimed. Upon review of the record, we agree that the record contains 
evidence that the Petitioner was quoted in major online media, judged other's work by performing 
peer reviews of journals, and co-authored two scholarly articles on topic of economics and business 
management. 
As we conclude that the Petitioner has already met the required three initial criteria, we need not 
address the Petitioner's appellate claims that he also meets two additional criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ii) (membership in associations) and (ix) (commanding a high salary). See INS v. 
Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding that agencies are not required to make 
"purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the ultimate decision). Therefore, we now 
proceed to the second step of the Kazarian analysis, the final merits determination. 
B. Final Merits Determination 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine ifhis successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability 
in the field of endeavor. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also 
Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. The commentary for the proposed regulations implementing section 
203(b)(1 )(A)(i) of the Act provides that the "intent of Congress that a very high standard be set for 
aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more 
2 
extensive documentation than that required" for lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704 
(July 5, 1991). 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that USCIS erred by conducting a final merit determination because 
this second step analysis is "a substantive rule adopted without notice and comment, and cannot serve 
as an independent basis to deny my petition once the regulatory criteria are met," citing Mukherji v. 
Miller, No. 4:24CV3170, 2026 WL 895708 (D. Neb. Jan. 28, 2026). We acknowledge the court in 
Mukherji found that USCIS' adoption of the two-tiered system under Kazarian is not valid because it 
was implemented without the proper notice and comment rulemaking process. However, the Mukherji 
decision issued by a district court in Nebraska is not a binding precedential authority for the Petitioner 
who is in Florida. See Matter ofK-S-, 20 I&N Dec. 715 (BIA 1993) (holding that in contrast to the 
precedential authority of the case law of a U.S. circuit court, the Board of Immigration Appeals is not 
bound to follow a decision of a U.S. district court). Therefore, we will address the substantive issues 
in Mukherji when it is properly before us, but for now, we evaluate whether USCIS properly applied 
the final merits determination under Kazarian. 
As claimed by the Petitioner on appeal, we note that USCIS made contradicting statements before 
launching the second step analysis. USCIS first indicated that the Petitioner has not met three of the 
evidentiary criteria, then immediately reversed this conclusion by stating that he has met three 
regulatory criteria. But these contradicting statements are not material to the ultimate conclusion. 
Here, USCIS proceeded to make the final merits determination, analyzed relevant evidence in 
accordance with the Act and regulations, and explained why the Petitioner has not demonstrated that 
he has sustained national or international acclaim, he is one of the small percentage at the very top of 
the field of endeavor, and his achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) ofthe Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian, 
596 F.3d at 1119-20. Therefore, we consider this harmless error. 1 
Upon review of the totality of evidence presented, we determine that the Petitioner has not met his 
burden of demonstrating his eligibility. We concur with the USCIS' findings in the final merits 
determination as summarized below and provide additional analysis on the evidence not discussed in 
the denial.

Beyond the three criteria that the Petitioner satisfied, we also consider additional documentation in the 
record to determine whether the totality ofthe evidence demonstrates eligibility. The Petitioner provided 
evidence ofhis membership with the Institute of Management Consultants (IMC) and claimed that his 
election to the "Fellow" grade of IMC membership, higher than general membership, confirms his 
extraordinary ability. However, the Petitioner's certificate of membership indicates that he was 
elected as a "Fellow" member on October 10, 2025. The fact that he has been a "Fellow" member of 
IMC for only three months at the time of filing does not sufficiently demonstrate that his membership 
has sustained national or international acclaim or a career of acclaimed work. See Donskoy v. United 
States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 21-CV-04757-CRB, 2021 WL 5240224, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 
11, 2021) (upholding the finding that the petitioner's membership did not demonstrate the requisite 
level of achievement or sustained acclaim to establish extraordinary ability). The record does not 
contain evidence indicating that based on his membership he received national or international 
recognition or that he has distinguished himself from others in the field, gaining national or 
international attention or placing him among the upper echelon in his field. 
The Petitioner farther presented evidence of his salary from! Iconsisting of his paystubs from 
July 2025 to September 2025, and claimed that his salary is higher than the average pay range for a 
risk analyst position in Florida as shown on glassdoor.com, salary.com, or salaryexpert.com. 
However, this evidence does not sufficiently support the finding that he commanded earnings 
commensurate with sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner has not shown his 
salary was high for a sustained period of time, or that his wages are tantamount to other renowned 
financial risk analysts or economists who are at the very top of the field, or that he received notoriety 
or attention based on his earnings separating himself from others in the field or placing him in the 
upper echelon.

sufficiently demonstrated that he is viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, as among

the upper echelon or that he has garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with 
being among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The Petitioner submitted 
letters from his former employers and work colleagues 3 who praise the Petitioner's expertise in risk 
management, his critical roles at the place of employment, or his journal articles on the impact of U.S. 
monetary policies during the pandemic as "a major, original contribution to financial economics." 
However, all these authors use regulatory language such as "original contribution" or "critical role" 
without providing persuasive details about the Petitioner's specific achievements, sustained acclaim, or 
level of acceptance by the field of financial management. Letters that make general and expansive 
statements regarding the Petitioner and his accomplishments are generally not persuasive. See generally 
6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(3). Although the letters highlight the Petitioner's talents, they 
do not reflect a career of acclaimed work in the field, garnering the required sustained national or 
international acclaim. See Mishra v. Richardson, No. 120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 
(E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding the plaintiff submitted letters only from people within his circle of 
professional acquaintances, and none of the letters point to evidence of extensive documentation of his 
accomplishments to show sustained national or international acclaim); see also Jafarov v. United States 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) 
(finding testimonial letters were insufficient to establish his relative status in the field and do not compare 
him to others). 
The record as a whole, including the evidence discussed above and evidence not specifically 
referenced, does not establish the Petitioner's eligibility for the benefit sought. 4 Here, the Petitioner 
seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top of their 
respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 
(Assoc. Comm'r 1994); Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th at 394 (stating that extraordinary ability is such 
an elite level of accomplishment); Lapteva v. Schuurmans, No. 8:24CV83, 2025 WL 815087, at *l 
(D. Neb. Mar. 10, 2025) (stating that EB-1 visas are intended to be available to only an exceedingly 
small amount of individuals with extraordinary ability). In this case, the Petitioner has not shown the 
significance of his work is indicative of the required sustained national or international acclaim or it 
is consistent with a career of acclaimed work in the field. See Section 203(b )(1 )(A) of the Act. While 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification 
sought, the record is insufficient to demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the top of his 
field. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
