Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner is an information security engineer who seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary 
ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )(1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b )(1 )(A). 
This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Through its Service Center Operations (SCOPS), USCIS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition 
for Alien Workers (petition), concluding the record did not establish that the Petitioner had received a 
major, internationally recognized award, nor did she demonstrate that she met at least three of the ten 
regulatory criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. The Petitioner 
bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de novo. Matter of Christa's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo 
review, we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field ofendeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See 
also H.R. Rep. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 1990 WL 200418 
at *6739 (stating: "In short, admission under this category is to be reserved for that small percentage 
of individuals who have risen to the very top of their field of endeavor."). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthat petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 20 I 0) ( discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner earned a foreign Bachelor of Engineering in 2016 and a Master of Science in Applied 
Information Technology from a U.S. university in 2020. She is currently in the United States as an 
H-lB nonimmigrant. 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally 
recognized award, she must satisfy at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). SCOPS decided that the Petitioner satisfied two ofthe criteria relating to judging 
and the authorship of scholarly articles but that she had not satisfied the criteria associated with 
membership, published material, or performing in a leading or critical role. On appeal, the Petitioner 
maintains that she meets those denied evidentiary criteria. After reviewing all the evidence in the 
record, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated she is eligible for this highly restrictive 
immigrant classification. 
l. "Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional or major 
trade publications or other major media." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary elements the Petitioner must demonstrate. The first 
element is that the Petitioner is an author of scholarly articles in her field in which she intends to 
engage once admitted to the United States as a lawful permanent resident. We consider these articles 
to fall within two distinct areas. 
One area is within the academic arena in which a scholarly article reports on original research, 
experimentation, or philosophical discourse. It is written by a researcher or expert in the field who is 
often affiliated with a college, university, or research institution. In general, it should have footnotes, 
endnotes, or a bibliography, and may include graphs, charts, videos, or pictures as illustrations of the 
concepts expressed in the article. The next area lies outside of the academic arena in which a 
scholarly article should be written for learned persons in that field. "Learned" is defined as "having 
or demonstrating profound knowledge or scholarship." Learned persons include all persons having

profound knowledge of a field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
https://www.uscis.gov/policymanual. 
The second evidentiary element this criterion requires is that the scholarly articles appear in one of 
the following: (1) a professional publication, (2) a major trade publication, or (3) in a form of major 
media. Relevant factors a petitioner should demonstrate include the intended audience (for 
professional and major trade publications) and the relative circulation, readership, or viewership are 
high relative to similar publications (for major trade publications and other major media). See 
generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). The Petitioner must submit evidence 
satisfying each of these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided a single two-paged article titled ______________ 
SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met the requirements of this criterion. However, we will 
withdraw that determination. 
The Petitioner works in the field of information technology and cybersecurity. Therefore, any 
evidence she offers under this criterion must be in that field. Her published paper presents a system 
of continuously monitoring a patient's heart rate and body temperature using sensors that upload 
ratings to a centralized database and if the readings exceed certain thresholds, it sends alert and 
location data to a doctor or guardian, which helps enable a rapid response and reduced health risks for 
elderly patients. Although the Petitioner attempted to connect the concepts in the paper with 
cybersecurity within this filing, she doesn't support her assertions with evidence, or an explanation, 
establishing that this article was in her field. The Petitioner's unsupported statements have little 
evidentiary value and will not make a prima facie eligibility showing, much less satisfy her burden of 
proof Matter of G-C-I-, 29 T&N Dec. 176, 178 (BIA 2025); Matter of L-R-M-C- & V-A-M-M-, 
29 I&N Dec. 757, 758 (BIA 2026) (concluding assertions without corroborating evidence are 
generally inadequate to meet a filing party's evidentiary burden). 
Based on these shortcomings we withdraw SCOPS' favorable determination under this criterion. 
2. "Published material about the alien in professional or major trade publications or other major 
media, relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought. Such evidence 
shall include the title, date, and author ofthe material, and any necessary translation." 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(iii). 
This criterion contains multiple evidentiary requirements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the 
published material must be about the Petitioner and the contents must relate to the Petitioner's work 
in the field under which they seek classification as an immigrant. The published material must also 
appear in professional or major trade publications or other major media. Professional or major trade 
publications are intended for experts in the field or in the industry. To qualify as major media, relevant 
factors include the relative circulation, readership, or viewership that should be compared to other 
similar statistics. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
The phrase "other major media" is generally accepted to mean a publication with significant reach and 
recognition, and it follows that we may reasonably require evidence of the same type of reach and 
recognition through circulation data. Krasniqi v. Dibbins, 558 F. Supp. 3d 168, 185 (D. N.J. 2021 ). 
3 
The final requirement is that the Petitioner provide each published item's title, date, and author and if 
the published item is in a foreign language, they must provide a translation that complies with the 
requirements found at 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(3). The Petitioner must submit evidence satisfying all of 
these elements to meet the plain language requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner provided one article and although SCOPS requested additional evidence under this 
criterion, they did not make a determination of her eligibility in the denial decision. In the request for 
evidence (RFE), SCOPS questioned whether the source in which the article appeared was a qualifying 
publication type stating "the evidence submitted is not sufficient to demonstrate that the published 
material was published in professional or major trade publications or other major media." 
Here, the Petitioner has not supported her claims with probative evidence that the article appeared in 
a form of major media as she claims. Some evidence cuts against the Petitioner's claims that the 
publication is major media, other material is not from the same year as the Petitioner's article, and 
other documentation does not compare similar resources. 
The Petitioner initially offered material from scimagomedia.com and that documentation reflected the 
relevant publication was ranked! in the Petitioner's home country. And for the content from 
similarweb, the Petitioner did not include the first page of the printout where this resource generally 
includes the website's ranking. As a result, she did not demonstrate how her publication compared to 
other similar resources. The necessity for a petitioner to offer circulation statistics in which to compare 
with the circulation data from the claimed major media in which their article appeared derives from 
the USCIS Policy Manual's term "relative." "In evaluating whether a submitted publication is a [ a 
form of] major media, relevant factors include . . . the relative circulation, readership, or 
viewership ...." See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). 
After SCOPS noted the initial evidence did not meet the regulatory requirements, the Petitioner's RFE 
response included evidence from semrush.com. But that item was not from the year in which the 
article about the Petitioner was published. Next, the material from Statista had two shortcomings: it 
was not a comparison of the online sphere and instead measured television, radio, and print media 
reach; it also was from a different year than the relative published material about the Petitioner. 
Her RFE response also included material from two of the same resources from her initial filing, but 
neither were for the year her article was published. And finally, we are not required to accept a 
publication's own claims relating to whether it qualifies as major media, as such self-serving claims 
are not probative. Krasniqi, 558 F. Supp. 3d 185 (citations omitted). Probative evidence is the type 
that "must tend to prove or disprove an issue that is material to the determination of the case." Matter 
ofE-F-N-, 28 I&N Dec. 591, 593 (BIA 2022) (quoting Matter ofRuzku, 26 I&N Dec. 731, 733 (BIA 
2016)); see also Evidence, Black's Law Dictionmy (12th ed. 2024). 
Therefore, if some form of the Petitioner's evidence does not adequately prove her contention, then it 
is not considered to be probative. When combined with other favorable material, evidence that is not 
probative on its own could exist on a palette in which the Petitioner "paints a mosaic" that sufficiently 
demonstrates the Petitioner's claims. However, the Petitioner has not crafted such a tapestry here. In 
summary, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the regulation's requirements relating 
to other major media, so she has not met this criterion's requirements.

3. "Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification 
is sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized 
national or international experts in their disciplines or fields." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). 
This criterion contains several evidentiary elements the Petitioner must satisfy. First, the Petitioner 
must demonstrate that she is a member of an association in her field. Second, the Petitioner must 
demonstrate both ofthe following: (1) the associations utilize nationally or internationally recognized 
experts to judge the achievements of prospective members to determine if the achievements are 
outstanding, and (2) the associations use this outstanding determination as a condition of eligibility 
for prospective membership. The Petitioner must satisfy all of these elements to meet the plain 
language requirements of this criterion. Tapia v. Jaddou, No. 8:23-CV-813-CEH-AAS, 2025 WL 
2780815, at *3--4 (M.D. Fla. Sept. 30, 2025). 
The Petitioner did not initially claim eligibility under this criterion and in the RFE response only 
claimed and provided evidence as a fellow of the National Institute of Professional Engineers and 
Scientists (NIPES). As evidence she provided the fellow certificate, the organization's About Us 
website page, their code of conduct and constitution which included the selection process for 
membership, and other related evidence. SCOPS determined that the Petitioner did not meet the 
requirements of this criterion. 
The Petitioner has not sufficiently demonstrated that this association requires outstanding 
achievements of its members seeking to be a fellow. Article III of the constitution reflected the 
following requirements for the grade of fellow: 
Candidates for Fellowship should meet several of the following criteria: 
l. Demonstrating excellence in their field through achievements such as awards, grants, 
patents, or significant industry innovations; 
2. Contributing to research projects or leading initiatives in academia or industry; 
3. Presenting work at conferences, symposia, or industry events; 
4. Taking on leadership roles in professional organizations, companies, or community groups; 
5. Publishing in peer-reviewed journals or producing influential industry reports and white 
papers; 
6. Developing innovative technologies, processes, or methodologies; 
7. Participating in knowledge dissemination through various channels (e.g., academic 
journals, industry publications, social media, public engagement); 
8. Showing commitment to mentoring and developing others in their field; 
9. Demonstrating the practical application or commercialization of their work; 
l0. Actively promoting and implementing EDT [ equity, diversity, and inclusion] principles in 
their work and team environments; 
11. Engaging in interdisciplinary collaborations or cross-sector partnerships; and 
12. Showing potential for future impact and leadership in their field. 
As for those judging whether a candidate's achievements are qualifying, the constitution provided:

Selection and Composition of the Fellowship Committee 
a) Purpose of the Fellowship Committee 
The Fellowship Committee is responsible for reviewing and evaluating candidates for 
the Fellowship (FNIPES) of the National Institute of Professional Engineers and 
Scientists (NIPES). The Committee ensures that only those who have demonstrated 
extraordinary achievements and made outstanding contributions to the fields of 
engineering and science are awarded Fellowship status. 
First, we note a discrepancy between the level of achievement required of candidates. The candidate 
criteria reflected one must demonstrate excellence in their field through achievements, but under the 
"Purpose of the Fellowship Committee" section, it states that the "Committee ensures that only those 
who have demonstrated extraordinary achievements and made outstanding contributions" may be 
awarded fellowship status. The Petitioner has submitted evidence that contains internal conflicts. 
Consequently, she has created a visible friction within the record, which she must resolve through 
some form of evidence that preponderantly establishes where the truth lies. Matter ofHo, 19 I&N 
Dec. 582, 591-92 (BIA 1988). The record does not contain any material resolving this conflict, which 
significantly diminishes the evidentiary value of this documentation as it goes to the heart of the 
criterion's requirements. 
Even setting that aside, the majority of the criteria for fellow level membership appears to fall outside 
of what would be considered outstanding achievements such as: awards, grants, or patents; 
contributing to research projects or leading initiatives, presenting at conferences or symposia; taking 
on leadership roles; publishing or participating in knowledge dissemination; showing a commitment 
to mentoring; promoting and implementing principles; engaging in interdisciplinary collaborations; 
and showing potential for future impact in leadership in the field. The Petitioner has not offered 
evidence demonstrating how these types of factors amount to outstanding achievements. It is for 
USCIS to make the determination of whether the association for which the alien claims membership 
requires that prospective members have outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS 
Policy Manual, supra, F.2(B)(l). And here, we conclude she has not made such a showing. 
For these reasons, the Petitioner has not submitted evidence that meets the plain language requirements 
of this criterion.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring a petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. "In creating the 'extraordinary ability' visa, Congress hoped to 'draw the world's best and brightest 
to American shores' by giving 'preferential treatment to highly talented immigrants."' Branski v. USCIS, 
No. 25-CV-1695, 2026 WL 2427439, at *11 (E.D. Wis. Aug. 19, 2026) (quoting Amin, 24 F.4th at 386). 
Because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that she is an alien of extraordinary ability and it is unnecessary 
that we provide further analysis of the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20; 
see also Nagaiah v. Allen, 784 F. Supp. 3d 798, 814 (E.D. Pa. 2025) (finding the regulatory criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) "establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a 
petitioner who seeks an extraordinary ability visa."). 
The two-step adjudication process is an interpretive rule that does not violate the Administrative 
Procedure Act, nor is it a legislative rule. Taneja v. USCIS, No. 24-CV-05657 (RER), 2026 WL 
1734266, at *8 (E.D.N.Y. June 16, 2026); Nagaiah, 784 F. Supp. 3d at 81 O; Agrawal v. Miller, No. 
4:24CV3143, 2025 WL 2879217, at *12 (D. Neb. Sept. 12, 2025); Etsy, Inc. v. Jaddou, No. 
4:22CV3022, 2023 WL 3689555, at *23 (D. Neb. May 25, 2023) (citing Perez v. Mortg. Bankers 
Ass 'n, 575 U.S. 92, 96 (2015). This approach is rooted in an exhaustive historical review of the 
congressional record, the governing statutory language, and the comprehensive regulatory scheme 
intended to ensure only those at the very top of their field receive this classification. 
To date, no other federal court has offered an alternative framework that is similarly grounded in such 
a thorough analysis of the legislative and agency history. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, 
supra, F.2(B)(2). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final merits determination in 
this matter. Patel, 596 U.S. at 332. 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated her eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision.
