Appeal of U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a software developer/software development manager in the field of cybersecurity, 
requests classification under the employment-based, first-preference (EB-1) immigrant visa category 
as an alien with "extraordinary ability." See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203 
(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). Successful petitioners for U.S. permanent residence in this 
category must demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and extensively document 
recognition of their achievements in their fields. Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act. 
United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) Service Center Operations (SCOPS) 
denied the petition, concluding that although the Petitioner satisfied three initial evidentiary criteria 
for this classification, he did not show as required his sustained national or international acclaim and 
demonstrate that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. The matter 
is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F .R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
An alien is eligible for the extraordinary ability classification if he or she has extraordinary ability in 
the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national 
or international acclaim and his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation; he or she seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability; and his or her entry will substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate a one­
time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). Ifthe petitioner does not submit 
this evidence, then the petitioner must provide documentation that meets at least three of the ten 
categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i) to (x) (including items such as awards, published material 
in certain media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner 
to submit comparable material if the petitioner is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1119-20 (9th Cir. 
2010); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. 
Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011).

C. "Evidence that the alien has commanded a high salary or other significantly high remuneration 
for services, in relation to others in the.field." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ix). 
In order to satisfy this criterion, the Petitioner must show that the person's salary or remuneration is 
high relative to the compensation paid to others working in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l). USCIS does not interpret the phrase "has commanded" to mean that the 
person must have already earned such salary or remuneration in order to meet the criterion. Rather, a 
credible contract or job offer showing prospective salary or remuneration may establish that the person 
has been able to command such compensation. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l). Evidence regarding whether a petitioner's compensation is high relative to what of others 
working in the field can include compensation surveys and organizational justifications so long as the 
description of the occupation matches, the validity of the survey is credible and reliable, and the 
location, currency, and salary rate being measured are comparable. See generally 6 USCIS Policy 
Manual, supra, at F.2(B)(l).

The Petitioner asserts that his total pay of $695,940.80 in 2024 demonstrates that he has commanded 
a high salary, or other significantly high remuneration for his services in relation to others in his field. 
In support of his commanded salary, the Petitioner submitted a copy of his 2024 USIRS Form W-2, 
Wage and Tax Statement, a copy of a PayScale summary identifying $142,872 per year as the average 
salary for software development managers inl ITexas, documents from salary.com identifying

development manager at I Ibetween $417,000 and $667,000, and a printout from levels.fyi 
identifying L6 Software Development Manager salary atl las $462,394. 
The regulatory requirement that the commanded salary be high "in relation to others in the field" is 
interpreted as allowing comparisons to those employed in the same occupation at a similar level. 
See Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. 953,954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994) (considering a professional golfer's 
earnings versus other PGA Tour golfers); see also Skokos v. US. Dept. of Homeland Sec., 420 F. 
App'x 712, 713-14 (9th Cir. 2011) (finding salary information for those performing lesser duties is 
not a comparison to others in the field); Grimson v. INS, 934 F. Supp. 965, 968 (N.D. Ill. 1996) 
( considering NHL enforcer's salary versus other NHL enforcers); Muni v. INS, 891 F. Supp. 440, 444-
45 (N. D. Ill. 1995) ( comparing salary ofNHL defensive player to salary of other NHL defensemen). 
We conclude the evidence in the record does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's compensation is 
high relative to others in his field. None of the wage scale or other printouts containing salary ranges 
or medians contain any job description. And even the documentation from the Petitioner's employer 
I Iapparently describing its wage scale and median salary for positions with the same title does 
not convincingly demonstrate eligibility under the criterion because job title by itself is not 
determinative to whether it applies to employment in the same occupation and at a similar level. 
Without a job description we cannot reliably evaluate ifthe salary benchmarks apply to the Petitioner's 
compensation. 
So, we conclude the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his salary is high compared to others working 
in the field and we withdraw SCOPS' conclusion to the contrary. 
H. Final Merits Determination 
The Petitioner did not meet the evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), 
(iii) of the Act. So, we are not required to make a final merits determination. But, as the Petitioner's 
appeal contends that he has sustained acclaim and will substantially benefit the United States of 
America prospectively as a permanent resident, we will conduct a final merits determination. Upon 
our de novo review of the record we conclude that, even if the Petitioner had established that he met 
the evidentiary standard and the requirements of section 203(b )(1 )(A)(ii), (iii) of the Act, we would 
nonetheless conclude he did not demonstrate eligibility for the classification because the evidence 
does not indicate he is one of the small percentage at the very top of his field of endeavor. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine if his or her successes are sufficient to by a preponderance of the evidence 
demonstrate that he or she has extraordinary ability in the field of endeavor through sustained national 
or international acclaim, that he or she is one of the small percentage at the very top of the field of 
8 
endeavor, and that his or her achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive 
documentation. See section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2), (3); see also Kazarian 
v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
The record as a whole does not demonstrate that the Petitioner's achievements are reflective of a 
"career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 
(Sept. 19, 1990). As stated above, the Petitioner claims to have served as a judge ofthe work ofothers. 
But an evaluation of the significance of his experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence 
indicates the required extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 
F. 3d at 1121-22.2 The Petitioner listed several occasions where he served in a judging capacity and 
so met the plain language of the criterion. However, the Petitioner did not establish that these judging 
opportunities contribute to a finding that he has a career of acclaimed work in the field or indicate his 
sustained national or international acclaim. The Petitioner did not show, for example, how his judging 
experience compares to others at the very top of the field. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner 
apart from others in his field, such as evidence that he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging 
recognized, acclaimed experts in his field, the Petitioner has not shown that his judging experience 
places him amongst that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See 
Hamal v. United States Dep 't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding the 
petitioner did not show that he served as a judge of renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists 
or the competitions were considered prestigious or attracted significant attention by the field). 
Likewise, authorship and publication do not automatically place one at the top of the field. The record 
reflects the Petitioner presented evidence showing that he has published articles in scholarly journals. 
But the Petitioner did not show the significance of his authorships or how his publication record 
compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See Jafarov v. United States 
Citizenship & Immigr. Servs., No. 23 CIV. 3372 (PAE), 2024 WL 69056, at *9 (S.D.N.Y. Jan. 5, 2024) 
(finding it reasonable for USCIS to focus on the fact that a publication record of six publications over 
seven years inconsistent with a career of acclaimed work and sustained national or international 
acclaim). And the Petitioner provided no citation history or other evidence of the influence of his 
written work so that we could evaluate the impact and recognition that his publications have had on 
the field and whether such influence has been sustained. See Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 
WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the Petitioner did not show the significance 
of authorships reflecting that he is among the small percentage at the very top of the field). 
The Petitioner provided letters generally praising him and his work, especially the realization of 
specific objectives that were well received by his superiors. But the letters do not contain sufficient 
information and explanation to show the Petitioner made original contributions of major significance 
such that the Petitioner is viewed by the overall field, rather than by a solicited few, among the upper 
echelon or that he has garnered recognition on a national or international scale, consistent with being 
among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. Mishra v. Richardson, No. 
120CV991LMBTCB, 2021 WL 1566071, at *3 (E.D. Va. Apr. 20, 2021) (finding the plaintiff 
submitted letters only from people within his circle of professional acquaintances, and none of the

and the other is termed restricted stock unit, or RSU. An RSU is a form of equity-based employee 
compensation where an employer compensates an employee with shares of stock in the company at a 
future date. When the RSU vests, its value is reported on the Form W-2 as income and taxed as such. 
In 2024, the Petitioner stated that he earned a base pay of $204,600 and received $441,802 in RSUs. 
The Petitioner's assertions and evidence emphasize the factor by which his income exceeds benchmark 
figures he provided. But the evidence does not support that the benchmarks he provided apply to his 
occupation, his level, or to those individuals who have sustained acclaim such that they can be 
considered amongst the very top of their field of endeavor. The evidence in the record does not set 
forth any minimum factor by which a Petitioner could establish his sustained acclaim or ascension to 
a level occupied by the small percentage at the top of his field based on comparison of his salary to 
the benchmarks. So, the record does not convincingly establish that the Petitioner's total salary is to

The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields, rather than those progressing toward the top. USCIS has long held that even 
athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the statutory standards for 
classification as an individual of"extraordinary ability." Matter ofPrice, 20 I&N Dec. at 954. Whilst 
the Petitioner need not establish that there is no one more accomplished to qualify for the classification 
sought, we find the record insufficient to demonstrate that he has sustained national or international 
acclaim and is among the small percentage at the top of his field. See section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the 
Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 3 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above-stated reasons, with each considered 
as an independent and alternate basis for the decision. 
ORDER: The appeal is dismissed.
