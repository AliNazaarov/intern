Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner seeks classification as an individual of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and 
Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference 
classification makes immigrant visas available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary 
ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been 
recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that although the record 
established the Petitioner met the requirements of at least three of the ten evidentiary criteria; the 
record did not establish that he warranted a favorable determination in a final merits analysis. The 
matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter afChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter a/Christa's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to individuals with extraordinary 
ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained 
national or international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, provided that the individual seeks to enter the United States to continue 
work in the area of extraordinary ability, and the individual's entry into the United States will 
substantially benefit prospectively the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
international recognition of his or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that 
is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then 
he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria 
listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain 
media, and scholarly articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit 
comparable evidence if they are able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)­
(x) do not readily apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011).

determined that the Petitioner met this criterion. However, upon de novo review, we disagree and 
withdraw SCOPS' determination.

B. Final Merit Determination 
Although we conclude the Petitioner did not establish he meets the criterion described above at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i); he has still met the requisite initial evidentiary criteria and therefore we will 
evaluate whether he has demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, his sustained national or 
international acclaim and that he is one ofthe small percentage at the very top ofthe field of endeavor. 
In a final merits determination, we analyze a petitioner's accomplishments and weigh the totality of 
the evidence to determine ifhis successes are sufficient to demonstrate that he has extraordinary ability 
in the field of endeavor. 1 In this matter, we determine that the Petitioner has not shown his eligibility. 
As an initial matter, we consider the Petitioner's claim that SCOPS' final merits determination was 
"inconsistent, conclusory, and contrary to Kazarian." The Petitioner contends that "[t]he decision 
fails to explain how evidence [SCOPS] deemed sufficient to meet multiple criteria somehow becomes 
insufficient when considered in the aggregate." The Petitioner's contention relies on a

misunderstanding ofKazarian and USCIS policy. In determining whether a petitioner has established 
extraordinary ability, we use a two-step analysis to evaluate the evidence. See Kazarian v. USCIS,

the first step does not establish that the person in fact meets the requirements for classification as a 
person with extraordinary ability. 4 
Therefore, SCOPS' analysis, in a final merits determination, as to whether the evidence-including 
evidence that demonstrates initial regulatory criteria-shows that the Petitioner meets the 
requirements for classification as a person with extraordinary ability, is a proper application of this 
framework. In fact, the USCIS Policy Manual provides the following example that relates to judging: 
Participating in the judging of the work of others in the same or an allied field of 
specialization alone, regardless of the circumstances, should satisfy the regulatory 
criteria in the first step of the analysis. However, the second step requires the officer 
to evaluate the person's participation to determine whether it was indicative of the 
person being one of that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of 
endeavor and enjoying sustained national or international acclaim. 5 
This analysis is not, as the Petitioner claims, "inconsistent, conclusory, and contrary to Kazarian." 
Rather, this analysis reflects the proper analysis in which the officer, at step one, determines only if 
the evidence objectively meets the regulatory criteria and, at step two, considers whether the evidence 
in its totality demonstrates sustained national or international acclaim. 
In considering the totality of the evidence, the Petitioner has not demonstrated that his achievements 
are reflective of a "career of acclaimed work in the field" as contemplated by Congress. 1990 
U.S.C.C.A.N. 6710, 6739. Furthermore, the Petitioner has not shown that he has risen to that small 
percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). The commentary for 
Congress that a very high standard be set for aliens of extraordinary ability is reflected in this 
regulation by requiring the petitioner to present more extensive documentation than that required for 
lesser classifications. 56 Fed. Reg. 30703, 30704. For the reasons discussed below, we conclude that 
the Petitioner has not established that he enjoys a career that meets this very high standard. See Amin 
v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383, 386-87 (5th Cir. 2002) (stating that the narrow definition reflects the 
stringency of the extraordinary ability standard).

We agree with SCOPS' determination that the Petitioner has met the criteria for judging the work of 
others in his field. Nonetheless, as it relates to a final merits determination, an evaluation of the 
significance of his experience is appropriate to determine if such evidence indicates the required 
extraordinary ability for this highly restrictive classification. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22. On 
appeal the Petitioner asserts that SCOPS erred in imposing a "quantitative threshold[ d] in evaluating 
the Petitioner's judging and peer-review evidence" and that the "regulation requires evidence of 
participation as a judge of the work of others; it does not require continuous annual assignments, a 
minimum number of reviews per year, or a particular 'volume' of conference papers." As discussed 
above, although evidence of participation as a judge ofthe work ofothers meets the evidentiary criteria 
for 8 CFR 204.5(h)(3)(iv); this is only the first step in analysis set forth in Kazarian. Here, in a final 
merits determination, we must determine whether the Petitioner is one of that small percentage who 
has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and that the person has sustained national or 
international acclaim. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22. While there is no "quantitative threshold" 
the quantity and quality of evidence is a consideration in a final merits analysis if it reflects that the 
Petitioner one of that small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor, and has 
sustained national or international acclaim. See Kazarian, 596 F. 3d at 1121-22.

The Petitioner provided evidence of judging and reviewing submissions for three academic 
conferences, scholarly journals and publications, and an industry competition; asserting that "his 
judging record spans multiple years and institutions." However, the Petitioner did not establish that 
these instances contribute to a finding that he has the required sustained national or international 
acclaim. The Petitioner did not show, for example, how his judging experience compares to others at 
the very top of the field. In addition, the Petitioner did not establish, for instance, that he garnered 
wide attention from the field based on his review work. Without evidence that sets the Petitioner apart 
from others in his field, such as evidence that he has a consistent history of reviewing or judging 
recognized, acclaimed experts in his field, the Petitioner has not shown that his experience places him 
among that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. See Hamal v. 
United States Dep't ofHomeland Sec., No. 21-5160, 2023 WL 1156801, at *2 (finding the petitioner 
did not show that he served as a judge of renowned directors as opposed to aspiring artists or the 
competitions were considered prestigious or attracted significant attention by the field).

Likewise, authorship and publication of scholarly articles do not automatically place one at the top of 
the field. In support of this evidentiary criterion, Petitioner submitted evidence of three papers, one 
article, and provided evidence of his role as a speaker at conferences. However, the Petitioner did not 
demonstrate that this number of papers, articles, and presentations is consistent with a career of 
acclaimed work, sustaining national or international acclaim, or being among the small percentage at 
the very top of the field. The Petitioner, for instance, did not show the significance of his authorship 
or how his publication record compares to others who are viewed to be at the very top of the field. See 
Bhanu v. Garland, No. 22-103, 2023 WL 6119357, at *9 (E.D. Pa. Sept. 18, 2023) (upholding the 
Petitioner did not show the significance of authorships to reflect that he is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field).

III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated his eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability. The appeal will be dismissed for the above stated reasons.
