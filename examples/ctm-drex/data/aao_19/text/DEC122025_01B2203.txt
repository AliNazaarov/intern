Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a debt recovery and fraud prevention specialist, seeks classification as an alien of 
extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those who 
can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and 
whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the Petitioner had satisfied 
only two of the ten initial evidentiary criteria, of which he must meet at least three. The matter is now 
before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance ofthe evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc., 26 l&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)( 1 )(A) of the Act makes visas available to immigrants with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics 
which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim and whose 
achievements have been recognized in the field through extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of extraordinary 
ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively the 
United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). The implementing regulation 
at 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate recognition 
ofhis or her achievements in the field through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally 
recognized award). If that petitioner does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide 
sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly 
articles). The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4) allows a petitioner to submit comparable material 
if he or she is able to demonstrate that the standards at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) do not readily 
apply to the individual's occupation. 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010). 
(discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner indicates that he is an accomplished executive leader with 20 years of experience in 
fraud prevention, collections, and cashflow management. The Petitioner seeks to establish a U.S. 
software development company named I I that he claims will transform the financial 
operations of small and medium sized businesses across the United States by enabling them to 
streamline their financial operations, significantly reduce costs, and mitigate risk associated with fraud 
and non-compliance. Because the Petitioner has not indicated or established that he has received a 
major, internationally recognized award at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), he must satisfy at least three of the 
alternate regulatory criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
In denying the petition, SCOPS determined that the Petitioner satisfied only two criteria, specifically 
those relating to a leading or critical role at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) and high salary at 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(ix) and had not satisfied the remaining three criteria that he had claimed. On appeal, the 
Petitioner maintains that he has established eligibility for three additional criteria beyond the two that 
SCOPS found he had met. After reviewing all of the evidence, the record does not reflect that the 
Petitioner meets at least three of the ten criteria, as required, because he has not established that he 
satisfies the three additional criteria he claimed below and on appeal. Moreover, for the reasons 
discussed below, we will withdraw SCOPS conclusion that the Petitioner has provided evidence 
establishing that he has commanded a high salary in relation to others in the field.

The regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii) calls for "documentation of the alien's membership in 
associations in the field for which classification is sought." In addition, the association must require 
"outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international experts 
in their disciplines or fields." Once membership in a qualifying association has been established, we 
determine whether the association requires those at the alien's level of membership to have attained 
outstanding achievements in the field. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual F.2(B)(l), 
www.uscis.gov/policy-manual. 
The Petitioner claims that his associate membership with theAssociation ofCertified Fraud Examiners 
(ACFE) and his affiliation with the Association of Credit and Collection Professionals (ACA 
International) fulfill this criterion. However, SCOPS found that the Petitioner's two memberships did 
not satisfy the criterion because the associations do not require outstanding achievements as an 
essential condition for admission to membership and the organizations do not have recognized national 
or international experts in the discipline or field to determine membership qualifications. 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that membership with ACFE is premised on having a minimum of a 
bachelor's degree or equivalent work experience, at least two years of professional experience in 
fraud-related disciplines, passage of a four-part examination, and submission of a character and 
integrity review, including endorsement by a current ACFE member in good standing. He also asserts 
that membership in ACA International requires candidates to document their substantial expe1ience in 
credit management, debt restructuring, and compliance risk mitigation and to have a proven record of 
high level contributions, especially when seeking professional tier affiliation. According to the 
Petitioner, such membership requirements establish that the organizations require outstanding 
achievement as an essential condition for admission to membership, as judged by national or 
international experts in their disciplines or fields. 
Although ACFE and ACA International require that candidates possess a certain level of education, 
experience, and credentialling, the Petitioner has not established that the membership requirements of 
holding a minimum of a bachelor's degree or equivalent work expe1ience, passing a credentialling 
exam, receiving endorsement from a current member and passing an integrity and character review 
are in fact outstanding achievements in the field. Similarly, with respect to the ACA International 
membership requirements, including a proven record of high level contributions, he has not shown or 
explained how such requirements equate to outstanding achievements in the field. Accordingly, the 
Petitioner has not demonstrated that either ACFE or ACA International membership is equivalent to 
being judged to have outstanding achievements by recognized national or international expe1is in their 
disciplines or fields. The Petitioner has not established on appeal that ACFE or ACA International 
require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or international 
expe1is in their disciplines or fields. 8 C.F .R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii). Consequently, the Petitioner has not 
overcome this portion of SCOPS' denial on appeal.

III. CONCLUSION 
Congress set a very high benchmark for aliens of extraordinary ability by requiring the petitioner 
demonstrate "sustained national or international acclaim" and present "extensive documentation" to 
show his or her achievements have been recognized in the field. Section 203(b)(l)(A)(i) of the 
Act. Here, because the Petitioner has not satisfied the threshold "initial evidence" requirements at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), we cannot conclude that he is an alien of extraordinary ability and need not 
further analyze the merits under the statute. See Kazarian, 596 F .3d at 1119-20; see also Nagaiah v. 
Allen, -- F.Supp.3d ---, 2025 WL 1508028, at *10 (E.D. Pa. May 27, 2025) (finding the regulation 
"establishes a necessary, not sufficient, evidentiary requirement for a petitioner who seeks an 
extraordinary ability visa."). Therefore, we decline to reach and hereby reserve any final me1its 
determination in this matter. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (per curiam) (holding 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision). Consequently, the petition remains denied.
