Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a lawyer, seeks classification as an alien of extraordinary ability. See section 
203(b)(l)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first 
preference classification makes immigrant visas available to those aliens who can demonstrate their 
extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim and whose achievements have 
been recognized in their field through extensive documentation. Id. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding the Petitioner had not established 
she had a major, internationally recognized award, or that she met at least three of the ten regulatory 
criteria. The matter is now before us on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christa's, Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537,537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will dismiss the appeal. 
I. LAW 
Section 203(b)(l)(A) of the Act makes immigrant visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
they have extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics which has been 
demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim; they seek to enter the United States to 
continue work in the area of extraordinary ability; and their entry will substantially benefit the United 
States prospectively. The tenn "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in "that small 
percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis of the evidence 
to determine whether a petitioner has sustained national or international acclaim and whether their 
achievements have been recognized in the field. First, a petitioner can provide evidence of either "a 
major, international recognized award," or satisfaction of at least three of ten evidentiary criteria at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3).1 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration 
Services (USCIS) then considers the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination 
and assesses whether the record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates 
that the individual is among the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian 
v. USCJS, 596 F.3d 1115, 1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation 
is frrst counted and then, if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a 
final merits determination); see also Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); 
Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 (W.D. Wash. 2011); see generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual 
F.2(B), www.uscis.gov/policy-manual (providing, as guidance, USCIS' two-step extraordinary ability 
analysis). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner holds the equivalent of a U.S. bachelor's degree in law from Brazil, which she obtained 
in 2011. She was approved for membership in the Brazilian Bar Association in 2012. The Petitioner 
authored several articles while pursuing her legal degree. After obtaining her bar membership, the 
Petitioner worked in law firms for a few years and then worked as an advisor for a public prosecutor' s 
office for a city in Brazil from 2014 to 2018. 
The Petitioner has not indicated or established that she has received a major, internationally recognized 
award under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). She must therefore satisfy at least three ofthe alternate regulatory 
criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). 
A. Evidentiary Criteria 
The Petitioner claimed that she satisfied five of the regulatory criteria, specifically at 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(ii), (iii), (v), (vi), and (viii). SCOPS determined that the Petitioner met 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii) by submitting evidence of published material about herself relating to her 
work in the field in major media and 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(vi) by submitting evidence of her 
authorship of scholarly articles in the field or other major media. We disagree that the Petitioner has 
met 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(iii).

3. Remaining Evidentiary Criteria Not Met 
On appeal, the Petitioner contests SCOPS' determination of 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v) and (viii), 
arguing she submitted sufficient evidence of her original contributions of major significance in the 
field and performing in a leading or critical role for organizations or establishments of distinguished 
reputation. 
a. Original Contributions of Maj or Significance 
In order to meet 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(v), a petitioner must establish that she not only made original 
contributions but that they have been of major significance in the field. Relevant suppmiing evidence 
may include, for example, published materials, testimonials, letters, and affidavits about the 
significance of the person's original work; documentation that the person's original work was cited at 
a level indicative ofmajor significance in the field; patents or licenses deriving from the person's work 
or evidence of commercial use of the person's work. See generally 6 USCIS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F .2(B)( I). The Petitioner submitted articles she authored, a few of which appear to be published 
electronically in 'I l" However, the Petitioner did not provide infonnation on the 
publisher, 'l l" to demonstrate whether it is a professional publication, major trade 
publication, or major media publication to explain the significance ofit publishing her work. She also 
included an abstract of an aiiicle published on the I I's 
website. She included a blurb froml I website stating it was publishing on its website a series of 
relevant articles on the purposes of punishment and the prison system but does not explain the major 
significance to the field of having her work included on the university's website. The Petitioner did 
not provide evidence that her work was cited by others. Further, her recommendation letters make no 
mention ofher publications. On appeal, the Petitioner argues that citations of her work and references 
in other scholarly and professional legal publications were overlooked, however, this evidence was 
not provided in the record below or on appeal. As a result, the Petitioner has not established the articles 
she authored were of major significance in the field. See id. (providing, as guidance, that published 
research that has provoked widespread commentary on its importance from others working in the field,

and documentation that it has been highly cited relative to others' work in that field, may be probative 
of the significance of the person's contributions to the field of endeavor). 
The Petitioner also argued below that she contributed originally and significantly through her 
commitment to the practice of law. She described her pro bono work as a mediator, the workshops 
she conducted in underserved communities, and her community advocacy. She, however, does not 
explain how her pro bono activities amount to original contributions ofmajor significance to the field. 
The Petitioner's recommendation letters do not mention her pro bono work and her supporting 
documentation do not detail the significance of her work as a lawyer. See id. (providing, as guidance, 
that detailed letters from experts in the field explaining the nature and significance of the person's 
contribution may also provide valuable context for evaluating the claimed original contributions of 
major significance). As a result, the Petitioner has not demonstrated she satisfies this criterion. 
b. Leading or Critical Role 
The criterion at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(viii) requires a petlt10ner first demonstrate that she has 
performed in a leading or critical role for an organization, establishment, or a division or department 
of an organization or establishment. A supporting role may be considered critical if the petitioner's 
performance in the role is ( or was) impmiant. See generally 6 USCJS Policy Manual, supra, at 
F.2(B)(l ). The Petitioner submitted recommendation letters in support of her performing a leading or 
critical role for the prosecutor's office. 
However, none of the letters refer to the Petitioner's role as leading. See id. (providing, as guidance, 
for a leading role, the evidence should establish that the person is ( or was) a leader within the 
organization or establishment or a division or department thereof). The letters also do not support her 
role was critical. To establish she played a critical role, the Petitioner must show that she contributed 
in a way that is of significant importance to the outcome of the organization or establishment's 
activities or those of a division or depaiiment of the organization or establishment. Id. The Petitioner 
submits a letter authored by a colleague at the prosecutor's office who speaks to the Petitioner's deep 
understanding of Brazilian law, coupled with her proactive approach to problem-solving, and 
concludes she was a critical asset to the prosecutorial team. However, neither this letter nor the others 
explain how her role was of such significant importance to the outcome of the prosecutor's office or 
her division within that office to establish her role was critical. 
In addition, we disagree with SCOPS' determination that the prosecutor's office had a distinguished 
reputation. Distinguished is defined as "marked by eminence, distinction, or excellence" or "befitting 
an eminent person." See Distinguished, Merriam-Webster.com, www.men-iam-webster.com. The 
Petitioner did not meet her burden to establish the distinguished reputation of the prosecutor's office 
by a preponderance ofthe evidence. Matter ofChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. at 375-76. Here, the Petitioner 
has not provided any infonnation on the prosecutor's office to substantiate her statements that it has a 
distinguished reputation. As a result, she has not established this criterion. 
B. Summary of Evidentiary Criteria and Reserved Issue 
The Petitioner has not established that she has received a major, internationally recognized award 
under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3), or that she satisfies at least three of the alternate regulatory criteria at 
4 
8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). As a result, we need not provide the type of fmal merits determination 
referenced in Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. See INS v. Bagamasbad, 429 U.S. 24, 25 (1976) (stating 
that agencies are not required to make "purely advisory findings" on issues that are unnecessary to the 
ultimate decision); see also Matter ofL-A-C-, 26 I&N Dec. 516, 526 n.7 (BIA 2015) (declining to 
reach alternative issues on appeal where an applicant is otherwise ineligible). Nevertheless, we advise 
that we have reviewed the record in the aggregate, concluding that it does not support a conclusion 
that the Petitioner has established the acclaim and recognition required for the classification sought. 
The Petitioner seeks a highly restrictive visa classification, intended for individuals already at the top 
of their respective fields. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2) (explaining "extraordinary ability" refers only to 
those in "that small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor"). USCIS has 
long held that even athletes performing at the major league level do not automatically meet the 
"extraordinary ability" standard. Matter of Price, 20 I&N Dec. 953, 954 (Assoc. Comm'r 1994). 
Here, the record as a whole does not demonstrate a level of recognition that indicates the required 
sustained national or international acclaim or demonstrates a "career of acclaimed work in the field" 
as contemplated by Congress. H.R. Rep. No. 101-723, 59 (Sept. 19, 1990); see also section 
203(b)(1 )(A) of the Act. Moreover, the record does not otherwise demonstrate that the Petitioner is 
one of the small percentage who has risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 
III. CONCLUSION 
For the reasons discussed above, the Petitioner has not demonstrated eligibility as an individual of 
extraordinary ability.
