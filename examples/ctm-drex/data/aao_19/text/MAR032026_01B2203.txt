Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, a lawyer in her home country with a specialization in conflict mediation, seeks 
classification as an alien ofextraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 
203(b)(l)(A), 8 U.S.C. § 1153(b)(l)(A). This first preference classification makes immigrant visas 
available to those who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or 
international acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in their field through extensive 
documentation. 
SCOPS denied the Form I-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (petition), deciding that the 
Petitioner's achievements and occupation or position did not properly fall under one of the statutorily 
mandated fields of the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics. The matter is now before us on 
appeal under 8 C.F.R. § 103.3. The Petitioner bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility to 
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) by a preponderance ofthe evidence. Section 291 
of the Act; Matter afChawathe, 25 I&N Dec. 369,375 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this 
matter de nova. Matter a/Christa 's Inc., 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de nova 
review, we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent 
with the following analysis. 
I. LAW 
To qualify under this immigrant classification, the statute requires the filing party demonstrate: 
• The alien enjoys extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; 
• They seek to enter the country to continue working in the area of extraordinary ability; and 
• The alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit the country in the future. 
Section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i)-(iii) of the Act. The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those in "that 
small percentage who have risen to the very top of the field of endeavor." 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). 
The implementing regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-step analysis. In the first 
step, a petitioner can demonstrate international recognition of his or her achievements in the field 
through a one-time achievement (that is, a major, internationally recognized award). If that petitioner 
does not submit this evidence, then he or she must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that 
meets at least three of the ten criteria listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items such as 
awards, published material in certain media, and scholarly articles). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then move to the second step to 
consider the totality of the material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the 
record shows sustained national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the individual is among 
the small percentage at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115, 
1121 (9th Cir. 2010) (discussing a two-step review where the documentation is first counted and then, 
if fulfilling the required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits dete1mination); 
see also Amin v. Mayorkas, 24 F.4th 383,394 (5th Cir. 2022). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner has been a licensed attorney since 2009 in her home country and has both engaged in 
the practice of law as well as performing much of her work as an arbitrator or mediator. On the 
petition, the Petitioner indicated that her occupation was considered under the occupation of "Lawyers 
and Judges" and that her job title was an "Arbitrators, Mediators, and Conciliators." SCOPS issued a 
notice of intent to deny (NOID) the petition putting forth the following reasoning: 
• The phrase "in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics" is not superfluous and must 
be understood to have a specific meaning; 
• A qualified beneficiary who is a member ofthe professions and who holds an advanced degree 
qualifies for an EB-2 immigrant visa; 
• The practice oflaw is a profession for purposes of eligibility for the EB-2 immigrant visa; 
• The practice ofa profession is not one of the fields within the EB-1 category; 
• Congress specifically included members of the professions in sections 203(b )(2)(A) and 
203(b)(3)(A)(ii) and excluded them from section 203(b)(l)(A); 
• If Congress had intended all aliens ofextraordinary ability, regardless of their field, to qualify 
under section 203(b )(l)(A), there would have been no purpose in including the phrase "in the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"; 
• As Congress did use that phrase, we can presume that there may be aliens of extraordinary 
ability who enjoy sustained national or international acclaim that are nevertheless ineligible 
for classification under section 203(b )( 1 )(A) solely because their occupation does not fall 
within the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; and 
• To hold otherwise would render the clear language of the statute meaningless and undermine 
Congressional intent. 
The Petitioner's NOID response reflected the following: 
The record demonstrates, beyond doubt, that [the Petitioner] is not seeking to practice law in 
the United States. Instead, she intends to establish a business consultancy in advanced 
Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR), mediation, and arbitration, with applications across 
corporate, cultural, and social sectors. Her work involves creating systems of negotiation and 
conflict prevention, not representing parties in litigation. This is why her endeavor falls within 
the statutory field of business, not "law." 
2 
Within the NOID response, the Petitioner cited to this office's non-precedent decisions that she 
characterizes as "a consistent adjudicative pattern: when the petitioner shows that their extraordinary 
ability will be exercised in the statutory field of business, EB-1 eligibility is proper-even if their 
professional background includes law." 
SCOPS denied the petition unpersuaded by the Petitioner's claims. SCOPS determined that the 
Petitioner's "occupation as an Arbitrator/Mediator/Conciliator has been determined to be directly 
related to the field of law" and as such, "the record indicates that you intend to continue practice 
Arbitration/Mediation/Conciliation as a lawyer in the field of law in the United States." Relying on 
the reasoning in the NOID, SCOPS made a conclusion similar to the following under each regulatory 
criterion: 
You only provided material regarding your [ claim under this criterion] as a 
Arbitrator/Mediator/Conciliator in the field of law. The field of law is not within the 
sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics required by this immigrant 
classification. As such, your evidence [under this criterion] does not fall within the 
fields of science, art, education, business, or athletics. Consequently, the submitted 
evidence does not indicate that you are eligible for this immigrant classification, nor 
does the evidence indicate that you meet this criterion. 
Based on SCOPS' reasoning, it appears that any occupation that meets the standards of being a 
member of the professions who holds an advanced degree is precluded from consideration as an 
immigrant of extraordinary ability, except for those who can dissect their extraordinary ability to also 
fit under one of the statutory and regulatory categories (i.e., the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics). That would apply to every occupation listed in section 101 (a)(32) of the Act, 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1101(a)(32) as well as any occupation for which a U.S. bachelor's degree (or its foreign equivalent) 
is the minimum requirement for entry into the occupation. See 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(k)(2). SCOPS did 
not cite to agency policy or another legal resource to support their reasoning to preclude lawyers 
simply because they are included as a profession. However, a 1995 legal opinion from the Office of 
General Counsel within the former Immigration and Naturalization Service states: 
First, although most lawyers are properly classified as EB-2 immigrants, a legal scholar 
of great distinction could well qualify for an EB-I immigrant visa as an "outstanding 
professor or researcher." INA§ 203(b)(l)(B), 8 U.S.C. § l 153(b)(l)(B). Secondly, an 
alien who is of extraordinary ability in business or in some other EB-I endeavor would 
not be ineligible for EB-1 classification simply because the alien is also a lawyer, or a 
member of any of the other professions that are more properly considered EB-2 
endeavors. 
See Genco Op. No. 95-3 (INS), 1995 WL 1796310, *5 titled "Construction of 'sciences' and 'arts' in 
Section 203(b)(l) and (2): Reconsideration of our March 3, 1994, Legal Opinion." We note that 
General Counsel opinions are advisory in nature and are not binding. R.L Inv. Ltd. Partners v. INS, 
86 F. Supp. 2d 1014. 1022 (D. Haw. 2000), aff'd. 273 F.3d 874 (9th Cir. 2001); see also Mitchell v. 
Advanced HCS. LLC, No. 4:21-CV-00155-P, 2021 WL 1247884, at *4 (N.D. Tex. Apr. 5, 2021), aff'd 
and remanded sub nom. Mitchell v. Advanced HCS, L.L.C., 28 F.4th 580 (5th Cir. 2022). 
3 
So, to the extent that SCOPS' reasoning relied on this General Counsel opinion, they should review 
its contents when compared with the Petitioner's claims throughout these proceedings. Here, the 
Petitioner has argued that the focus of much of her evidence and claims is not on her legal license 
establishing her ability to work as a lawyer in the United States, but on the functional impact of her 
work within the business sector. The record indicates the Petitioner's intended employment will be in 
a business-related role. So, consistent with section 203(b )(1 )(A)(i) of the Act, a preponderance of 
evidence demonstrates the Petitioner's proposed employment in the field ofbusiness. SCOPS did not 
provide analysis for the Petitioner's evidence under the regulatory criteria. See 8 C.F.R. 
§ 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x). We will therefore withdraw SCOPS' decision and remand this matter. 
On remand, SCOPS should review the entire record, including the Petitioner's submission and 
arguments on appeal. If they find that the Petitioner satisfies the minimum number of regulatory 
criteria, they should consider whether the evidence demonstrates that she has demonstrated sustained 
national or international acclaim that places her among the small percentage at the very top of her 
field. See Kazarian, 596 F.3d at 1119-20 and issue a new, fully reasoned decision. SCOPS may 
request any additional evidence considered pe1iinent to the new detennination. As such, we express 
no opinion regarding the ultimate resolution of this case on remand. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
