Appeal of Service Center Operations (SCOPS) Decision 
Form 1-140, Immigrant Petition for Alien Workers (Extraordinary Ability) 
The Petitioner, an attorney who is licensed to practice law in Ukraine, seeks classification as an alien 
of extraordinary ability. See Immigration and Nationality Act (the Act) section 203(b )( 1 )(A), 8 U.S.C. 
§ 1 l 53(b )( 1 )(A). This first preference (EB-1) classification makes immigrant visas available to aliens 
who can demonstrate their extraordinary ability through sustained national or international acclaim 
and whose achievements have been recognized in their fields through extensive documentation. 
Service Center Operations (SCOPS) denied the petition, concluding that the record did not establish 
the Petitioner had satisfied at least three of ten initial evidentiary criteria. The matter is now before us 
on appeal pursuant to 8 C.F.R. § 103 .3. On appeal, the Petitioner contends that SCOPS incorrectly 
held that the field of law is categorically excluded from EB-I classification eligibility, 
mischaracterized the Petitioner's field and intended work in the United States, applied an incorrect 
requirement of U.S. bar licensure, failed to conduct the Kazarian two-step analysis, and improperly 
rejected corroborating evidence. 
The Petitioner bears the burden ofproof to demonstrate eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. 
Matter ofChawathe, 25 l&N Dec. 369, 375-76 (AAO 2010). We review the questions in this matter 
de novo. Matter of Christo 's, Inc. , 26 I&N Dec. 537, 537 n.2 (AAO 2015). Upon de novo review, 
we will withdraw the decision and remand the matter for entry of a new decision consistent with the 
following analysis. 
I. LAW 
Section 203 (b)(1) of the Act makes visas available to aliens with extraordinary ability if: 
(i) the alien has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or 
athletics which has been demonstrated by sustained national or international 
acclaim and whose achievements have been recognized in the field through 
extensive documentation, 
(ii) the alien seeks to enter the United States to continue work in the area of 
extraordinary ability, and 
(iii) the alien's entry into the United States will substantially benefit prospectively 
the United States. 
The term "extraordinary ability" refers only to those individuals in that small percentage who have 
risen to the very top of the field of endeavor. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also Onaghise v. Dep 't of 
Homeland Sec., No. 3:20-CV-03033-X, 2022 WL 4073344, at *2 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 2, 2022) ("EB-1 
petitions, nicknamed Einstein visas, are coveted visas reserved for only the most talented noncitizens 
with extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics"). The implementing 
regulation at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) sets forth a multi-part analysis. First, a petitioner can demonstrate 
sustained acclaim and recognition of the beneficiary's achievements in the field through a one-time 
achievement, that is, a major, internationally recognized award. If that petitioner does not submit this 
evidence, then they must provide sufficient qualifying documentation that meets at least three of the 
ten categories listed at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3)(i)-(x) (including items, such as awards, published 
material in certain media, and scholarly articles). Additionally, if the criteria at 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3) 
do not readily apply to the beneficiary's occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence 
to establish the beneficiary's eligibility. 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(4). 
Where a petitioner meets these initial evidence requirements, we then consider the totality of the 
material provided in a final merits determination and assess whether the record shows sustained 
national or international acclaim and demonstrates that the beneficiary is among the small percentage 
at the very top of the field of endeavor. See Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d 1115 (9th Cir. 2010) 
( discussing a two-part review where the documentation is first counted and then, if fulfilling the 
required number of criteria, considered in the context of a final merits determination); see also 
Visinscaia v. Beers, 4 F. Supp. 3d 126, 131-32 (D.D.C. 2013); Rijal v. USCIS, 772 F. Supp. 2d 1339 
(W.D. Wash. 2011). 
II. ANALYSIS 
The Petitioner intends to work in the United States as an international attorney and governance and 
compliance advisor. With respect to his proposed endeavor, the Petitioner stated that "[i]n the United 
States, [he] intend[s] to apply [his] experience and skills in law, public administration, environmental 
regulation to serve the national interest in several critical ways," and "[t]o formalize this contribution, 
[he is] planning establish a U.S.-based legal consultancy that provides policy guidance and legal 
services to organizations working on Ukraine-related projects and broader international development 
initiatives." The Petitioner farther stated that "[his] goal is to integrate into the American legal field, 
offering specialized expertise in international environmental law, legislative design, and transitional 
legal system." 
Although the Petitioner claimed to meet eight of the ten evidentiary criteria, SCOPS did not 
sufficiently address the evidence presented under these criteria. Instead, SCOPS focused on the listing 
of "an international attorney and governance and compliance advisor" as a profession under section 
10l(a)(32) of the Act, concluding that the Petitioner is not eligible for classification as aliens of 
extraordinary ability unless his proposed field of endeavor and occupation fall within the sciences, 
arts, education, business or athletics. SCOPS found that the Petitioner's "proposed field of endeavor 
(law) and occupation (attorney) do not fall within the fields of science, arts, education, business, or 
athletics" and concluded that "the submitted evidence does not indicate that [the Petitioner is] eligible 
2 
for [ the EB-1 visa] classification because [his] field of endeavor (law) is not within the sciences, arts, 
education, business, or athletics." 1 
On appeal, the Petitioner claims that "[a]t no point did [he] indicate any intention to practice U.S. law 
or to seek admission to a state bar," and "[ a ]ny reference in the record to legal consultancy clearly 
pertain to international advisory services, not to the practice oflaw within any U.S. jurisdiction." Upon 
de novo review, we find that the record includes evidence, which demonstrates that the Petitioner's 
proposed employment in the United States as an international attorney and governance and compliance 
advisor, his proposed endeavor to establish a legal consulting firm that provides policy guidance and 
legal services to organizations working on Ukraine related projects and international development 
initiatives, and his claimed experience and skills in law, public administration, and environmental 
regulation place his area of expertise within the field of social science or business. We agree with the 
Petitioner that SCOPS did not sufficiently evaluate the evidence presented under his claimed eight 
criteria.2 
On remand, SCOPS should evaluate the evidence in the record to determine whether it establishes that 
the Petitioner as an attorney in the field of social science or business meets three of the evidentiary 
criteria under 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(3). If SCOPS determines that it does, SCOPS should then consider 
whether the totality of the evidence shows that the Petitioner has sustained national or international 
acclaim in the field of international advisory services and that the Petitioner is one of the very small 
percentage at the top of this field. See section 203(b )(1) of the Act; 8 C.F.R. § 204.5(h)(2). See also 
Kazarian v. USCIS, 596 F.3d at 1119-20. 
ORDER: The decision is withdrawn. The matter is remanded for the entry of a new decision 
consistent with the foregoing analysis.
